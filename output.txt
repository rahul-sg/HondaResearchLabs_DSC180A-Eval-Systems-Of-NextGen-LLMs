Overall winner: The first week of Principles of Accounting introduces accounting as the language of business—recording and communicating a company’s financial story to help users make informed decisions. Accounting serves two essential functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which presents this information through financial statements to external decision makers. Users include investors judging performance, creditors and suppliers evaluating repayment or reliability, management and boards monitoring operations, employees considering job stability, customers assessing reliability, and other groups such as analysts, regulators, and competitors.

Financial accounting captures three types of business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and creditors), **investing** (acquiring resources like buildings, equipment, and inventory), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production, marketing, and administration). These activities generate four key reports: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**. Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under the oversight of the **SEC**; international standards are set by the **IASB** under IFRS. **Auditors**, hired independently, assess whether statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements, issuing one of four opinions that add credibility to the results.

The lecture focuses on the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific date and must satisfy the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets represent resources providing future benefits; liabilities are obligations owed to creditors; and stockholders’ equity reflects owners’ residual claims. Each transaction has a dual effect that keeps the equation balanced. Through examples—raising capital from investors, borrowing from a bank, acquiring equipment, prepaying rent, or purchasing supplies—the class demonstrates how financing, investing, and operating activities affect financial position. Listing items such as cash, supplies, prepaid rent, equipment, payables, and common stock shows how resources and claims are organized in a formal balance‑sheet format.
