This lecture examines how natural selection, genetics, and the molecular mechanisms of inheritance together explain evolutionary change. Evolution is defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population across generations, measured at the population level. Natural selection operates on variation among individuals, leading to differential survival and reproduction. Traits with a genetic basis are passed to offspring, gradually shifting allele frequencies. Darwin’s key unanswered questions about variation, inheritance, and the origin of new traits were later explained through genetics. Artificial selection, such as the breeding of dogs and pigeons, illustrates how selective pressures can rapidly alter trait frequencies.  

Evolutionary change can occur over both long and short timescales depending on selective pressures; for example, aquatic species show structural adaptations shaped by environmental demands. The discovery of Mendel’s particulate inheritance clarified that traits are transmitted discretely rather than blending. His experiments with pea plants established that organisms carry two alleles per gene, which interact to determine genotypes and observable phenotypes. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, but dominance does not imply higher fitness. Predictive tools like Punnett squares reveal consistent genotype (1:2:1) and phenotype (3:1) ratios in crosses.  

Chromosomes were later identified as carriers of genes, explaining Mendel’s results. Mitosis produces identical cells, while meiosis forms gametes with half the chromosome number, maintaining genetic stability and enhancing variation through crossing over and independent assortment. Linked genes tend to be inherited together, but recombination can separate them, creating new allele combinations.  

DNA’s double-helix structure, composed of nucleotide base pairs, encodes proteins and replicates accurately. Mutations introduce the variation upon which selection acts. Mitochondrial DNA, inherited maternally without recombination, allows tracing of maternal lineages. Molecular analyses of DNA, including molecular clocks based on mutation rates, enable estimation of divergence times and phylogenetic relationships. Collectively, these mechanisms demonstrate that evolution is driven by genetic change, with natural selection acting on heritable variation grounded in molecular genetics.
