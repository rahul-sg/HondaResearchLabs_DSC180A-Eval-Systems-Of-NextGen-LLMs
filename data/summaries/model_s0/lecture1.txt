The first week of Principles of Accounting introduces students to the purpose, structure, and application of financial accounting. Accounting is described as the language of business—telling a company’s financial story through recorded data about successes, risks, and opportunities. Its two main functions are recordkeeping, which involves identifying, measuring, and recording economic events, and communication, which conveys this information to external decision makers through financial statements. Users of accounting information include investors assessing performance, creditors and suppliers evaluating repayment or reliability, management and boards monitoring operations, and other groups such as employees, customers, analysts, and regulators.

Financial accounting measures three main business activities: financing (transactions with investors and creditors), investing (acquiring productive resources), and operating (activities related to producing and selling goods and services). The results are communicated through four key financial statements: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. Financial reporting follows Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), established by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) and overseen by the SEC for public companies; international standards are set by the IASB under IFRS. Independent auditors evaluate whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements, issuing one of four opinions that add credibility to the reports.

The lecture emphasizes the balance sheet, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific point in time and must satisfy the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity. Assets represent resources with future economic benefits, liabilities are obligations to creditors, and equity shows owners’ residual claims. Examples show how transactions—such as issuing stock, borrowing funds, purchasing equipment, paying rent, or buying supplies—affect the equation but keep it balanced. The balance sheet classifies assets and liabilities as current or noncurrent based on liquidity and maturity, respectively, and lists shareholders’ equity as common stock and retained earnings. This framework provides the foundation for understanding how business transactions shape financial health and support informed decision-making.
