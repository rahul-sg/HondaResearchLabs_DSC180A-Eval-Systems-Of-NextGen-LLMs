This lecture introduces the fundamental principles of financial accounting and the structure of the balance sheet. Accounting is described as the “language of business” because it records and communicates information about a company’s financial story—its successes, risks, and performance. Its primary functions are recordkeeping, which identifies, measures, and records economic events, and communication, which provides decision-useful information to external parties such as investors, creditors, suppliers, managers, employees, and regulators.  

The course focuses on financial accounting rather than managerial accounting or financial statement analysis. Financial accounting measures three key types of business activity—financing, investing, and operating—and communicates results through four financial statements: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. These statements are governed by Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), set primarily by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) under the oversight of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC); internationally, the IASB issues IFRS standards. Independent auditors, required for public companies, examine statements for compliance with GAAP and issue one of four opinions ranging from unqualified to adverse, providing credibility for stakeholders.  

The lecture emphasizes the balance sheet as a “snapshot” of a company’s financial position at a specific point in time. It reports resources (assets) and the claims to those resources (liabilities and stockholders’ equity), which must always balance according to the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity. Examples using a start-up business show how transactions—such as equity investment, bank loans, asset purchases, and prepaid expenses—affect the equation. Assets are classified as current or noncurrent based on their liquidity and expected time of use, while liabilities are categorized by when obligations are due. Stockholders’ equity represents owners’ residual interest through common stock and retained earnings. Understanding these relationships provides a foundation for analyzing and interpreting financial statements and business activities.
