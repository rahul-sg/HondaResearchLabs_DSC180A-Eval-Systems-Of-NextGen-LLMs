This lecture introduces the fundamental principles and framework of financial accounting, focusing on how businesses record, organize, and communicate their financial activities. Accounting is presented as the “language of business,” conveying a company’s financial story—its successes, challenges, and opportunities—to internal and external decision makers. It fulfills two key roles: recordkeeping, which involves identifying, measuring, and recording transactions; and communication, which provides reliable financial information to stakeholders such as investors, creditors, management, employees, customers, and regulators.  

The financial accounting process measures three main types of business activity—financing, investing, and operations—and reports them through four primary financial statements: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. These are prepared under Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), overseen by the SEC and established through the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB). Internationally, the IASB issues IFRS standards. Independent auditors evaluate whether companies’ financial statements comply with GAAP, offering opinions that enhance credibility for decision makers.  

The lecture emphasizes the balance sheet as a key financial statement that reports a company’s financial position at a specific point in time. It presents assets as the organization’s resources, liabilities as obligations to creditors, and stockholders’ equity as owners’ residual claims, summarized by the fundamental equation: **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Each transaction affects at least two accounts, maintaining balance. Examples with a start-up illustrate financing from investors and creditors, investing in equipment, prepaying rent, and purchasing supplies on credit.  

Finally, the lecture details the structure of the balance sheet: assets and liabilities are categorized as current or noncurrent based on time horizon—current within one year and noncurrent beyond. Assets are ordered by liquidity, liabilities by maturity. Shareholders’ equity includes common stock and retained earnings, collectively representing the owners’ stake. Together, these components form an integrated framework for understanding a firm’s financial position and supporting effective business decision-making.
