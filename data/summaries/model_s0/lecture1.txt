This lecture introduces the fundamental concepts and framework of financial accounting. Accounting is presented as the “language of business,” describing a company’s financial story—its successes, failures, and opportunities—through measurable economic events. The discipline serves two main functions: recordkeeping, which identifies and measures economic activities, and communication, which reports this information to decision makers. Financial information users include investors, creditors, suppliers, management, boards of directors, employees, customers, and others who rely on accurate and comparable data.  

Financial accounting focuses on three core business activities: financing (transactions with investors and creditors), investing (acquiring and disposing of resources such as equipment or land), and operations (daily production and sales). These activities are summarized in four interrelated financial statements: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. Together, they communicate a firm’s financial position and performance within the framework of Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), which are developed by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) under the oversight of the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Internationally, accounting standards are guided by the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) through IFRS.

Auditors, acting as independent reviewers, enhance the credibility of financial statements by assessing compliance with GAAP and issuing one of four opinions—unqualified, unqualified with emphasis, qualified, or adverse.  

The lecture focuses on the balance sheet as a snapshot of a company’s financial position at a specific date, showing assets, liabilities, and stockholders’ equity according to the accounting equation (Assets = Liabilities + Equity). Assets are categorized as current (e.g., cash, receivables, inventory, prepaid expenses) or noncurrent (e.g., property, equipment, patents). Liabilities are similarly divided into current and long-term obligations, while equity reflects owners’ residual interests such as common stock and retained earnings. Illustrative examples demonstrate how transactions—such as issuing stock, borrowing funds, or purchasing assets—affect the balance sheet while maintaining its balance.
