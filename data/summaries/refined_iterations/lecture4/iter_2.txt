This lecture explored how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, consistent with Darwin’s postulates that organisms struggle for existence, vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and transmit these heritable variations to offspring. Darwin’s challenge was explaining how variation arises and persists when selection tends to remove less‑fit traits. Artificial selection in domesticated plants and animals, such as dogs and pigeons, demonstrated that targeted breeding can produce major changes in only a few generations. Examples of evolutionary rate variation included rapid evolution of placenta‑like structures in minnows and the gradual development of eyes through approximately 1,800 small 1 % improvements over nearly one million years.

Gregor Mendel’s pea‑plant experiments established the particulate theory of inheritance—traits are controlled by discrete units (“particles”) from each parent that preserve variation rather than blending. His ideas later connected with cytological discoveries. Walther Flemming (1882) identified chromatin within the cell nucleus and described chromosome separation during mitosis. Around the turn of the twentieth century, Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri proposed the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, demonstrating that hereditary information is carried on chromosomes passed from parent to offspring.

Cell division occurs through mitosis, producing identical diploid somatic cells, and through meiosis, which yields haploid gametes. Meiosis introduces variation via mutation, recombination, and independent assortment. Mendel’s principles distinguish genotype (allelic composition) from phenotype (expressed traits) and show how dominant alleles mask recessive ones. Crosses of heterozygotes, depicted by event trees and Punnett squares, reveal predictable inheritance ratios. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but linked genes located close together may segregate jointly; recombination rate (r) measures how often crossing‑over separates them.

The discovery of DNA’s double‑helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images, explained how genetic information is stored and copied. DNA has a sugar‑phosphate backbone and complementary base pairs: adenine pairs with thymine, cytosine with guanine. Genes—functional sequences of DNA—number about 20,000–25,000 in humans. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), inherited maternally and encoding 37 genes, is shorter and evolves more slowly than nuclear DNA. Analyses of mtDNA identify “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of all humans, who lived roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
