This lecture traced how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change, building from Darwin’s ideas to the molecular basis of DNA. Evolution was introduced as the change in allele frequencies over time within a population. According to Darwin’s postulates, organisms face a struggle for existence, show heritable variation affecting survival and reproduction, and those traits are transmitted across generations. His main difficulty was explaining how variation arises and persists if selection removes less‑fit traits. Artificial selection in domesticated species, such as dogs and pigeons, showed that targeted breeding can produce dramatic changes within only a few generations. Rates of evolutionary change vary—some traits evolve quickly, as in minnows developing placenta‑like structures, while others, like the eye, accumulate incremental changes over long periods.

Gregor Mendel’s breeding experiments with pea plants revealed that inheritance is based on discrete particles received from each parent, maintaining variation rather than blending. Early cytological discoveries connected this idea to physical structures in cells. In 1882 Walther Flemming described chromatin inside cell nuclei and the process of mitosis, while around 1900 Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri, working independently, proposed that hereditary material resides on chromosomes, forming the basis of the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance.

Cell division occurs through mitosis, producing identical diploid somatic cells, and meiosis, which generates haploid gametes. Meiosis introduces variation through mutation, recombination, and independent assortment. Mendel’s principles distinguish genotype (allelic composition) from phenotype (observable traits) and show how dominant alleles mask recessive ones. Crosses of heterozygotes, represented through event trees and Punnett squares, yield predictable inheritance ratios. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently, while linked genes located close together may segregate together unless separated by crossing‑over; recombination rate (r) quantifies this frequency.

In 1953 James Watson and Francis Crick, drawing on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images, described DNA’s double‑helix structure, revealing how genetic information is stored and replicated. DNA consists of a sugar‑phosphate backbone with complementary base pairs: adenine with thymine, cytosine with guanine. Genes—functional sequences of DNA—number roughly 20,000–25,000 in humans. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), located in mitochondria and inherited only from mothers, codes for 37 genes and evolves more slowly than nuclear DNA. Analyses of mtDNA identify “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal common ancestor of all humans, estimated to have lived about 100–200 thousand years ago in East Africa.
