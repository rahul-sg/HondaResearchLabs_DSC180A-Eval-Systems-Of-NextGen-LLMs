This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** and its continuing impact on modernity, capitalism, and identity in Latin America. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist, advanced from dependency theory and political‑economic analysis to broader questions of culture, knowledge, and decolonial thought. His work forms part of the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** project developed with other Latin American scholars such as Arturo Escobar, Walter Mignolo, and Catherine Walsh.  

The lecture outlines three interconnected dimensions: **modernity**, **coloniality**, and **decoloniality**. Modernity is presented not as an inevitable Western achievement but as a historically situated narrative that privileges Europe’s self‑image as the center of progress. **Coloniality** refers to the ongoing matrix of power created by colonialism—the persistence of racial hierarchies and epistemological dominance that outlast formal empire. **Decoloniality**, in turn, calls for centering non‑Western languages, visions, and forms of being to unsettle Eurocentric norms.  

According to Quijano (Slides 11–13), the coloniality of power took shape through two related developments: the division between “West” and “Rest” and the establishment of racialized systems of labor control in the Americas, including slavery, the encomienda, and the mita. These practices tied ideas of racial difference to economic exploitation, embedding whiteness and European standards as measures of authority and worth.  

**Eurocentrism** (Slides 2.3–2.5) reinforced this structure by portraying Europe as the origin of reason and progress. It organized global capitalism and knowledge through linear and dualistic contrasts—such as “Third World/First World” or “developed/underdeveloped.” The discussion of **democracy and development** (Slide 2.6) raises questions about how such Eurocentric frameworks continue to define success, highlighting the political and cultural dimensions of dependency rather than offering a settled critique.  

Subsequent slides (2.7–2.14) introduce **heterogeneity and discontinuity** as alternatives to linear histories. Historian Rebecca Earle’s analysis of “Indianesque nationalism” shows how post‑independence elites idealized Indigenous heritage to forge national identity while maintaining exclusionary power. The ideology of **mestizaje** likewise promoted racial harmony rhetorically but often reaffirmed whiteness as the national norm.  

The lecture closes with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality** (Slide 2.19): to free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts our image and to refuse imposed definitions of what we are. Decolonial thinking thus seeks to dismantle the enduring matrix of racial, economic, and epistemic domination shaping the Americas.
