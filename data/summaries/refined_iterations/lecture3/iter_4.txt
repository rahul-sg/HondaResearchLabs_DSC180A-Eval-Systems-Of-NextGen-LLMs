This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** and how it illuminates the enduring ties between race, capitalism, knowledge, and identity in Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano moved from **dependency theory** and political‑economic analysis to questions of culture and epistemology, establishing a foundation for the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** project with scholars such as Arturo Escobar, Walter Mignolo, and Catherine Walsh.  

The lecture structures its discussion around three interlinked dimensions: **modernity**, **coloniality**, and **decoloniality**. Modernity is not treated as a natural Western achievement but as a historically produced narrative that locates Europe at the center of progress. **Coloniality**, as Quijano defines it, extends beyond colonialism’s formal end: it represents an epistemological order that privileges Western categories of knowledge while obscuring Indigenous and other non‑Western ways of thinking. This “matrix of power” maintains racial hierarchies and cognitive dependence long after political independence. **Decoloniality**, therefore, signals the active centering of non‑Western cultures, cosmologies, and voices to unsettle Eurocentric assumptions.

According to Quijano (Slides 11–13), the coloniality of power emerged through two intertwined processes: (1) the construction of difference between conquerors and conquered—“West” and “Rest”—and (2) the imposition of new systems of labor control such as slavery, encomienda, and mita. These practices fused racial classification with capitalist exploitation, naturalizing whiteness and European norms as standards of civilization.  

**Eurocentrism** (Slides 2.3–2.5) reinforces this structure by presenting Europe as the universal source of reason and modernity. It shapes global capitalism through linear contrasts—“developed/underdeveloped,” “First World/Third World”—that frame dependency as cultural inferiority. The lecture asks who defines progress and democracy, exposing how Eurocentric visions of development continually reproduce inequality.  

Later slides (2.7–2.14) introduce **heterogeneity and discontinuity** as a challenge to such linear histories. Echoing Stuart Hall, these ideas highlight the coexistence of multiple, intersecting trajectories rather than a single European path. Rebecca Earle’s study of “Indianesque nationalism” illustrates this tension: post‑independence elites romanticized Indigenous ancestry to build national unity while excluding living Indigenous peoples, and **mestizaje** ideology further masked ongoing racial hierarchies.  

The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality** (Slide 2.19): to free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts our self‑image. Decolonial thought thus aims to dismantle the persistent racial, economic, and epistemic structures through which coloniality continues to shape the modern world.
