This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power**, showing how the intertwined histories of race, labor, knowledge, and modernity continue to organize social life in Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano’s work evolved from **dependency theory** and political economy toward questions of **culture, identity, and epistemology**, forming part of the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project** alongside scholars such as Arturo Escobar, Walter Mignolo, and Catherine Walsh.  

The lecture outlines three interlinked dimensions—**modernity**, **coloniality**, and **decoloniality**. Modernity is reframed as a historically contingent outcome rather than a self‑generated Western achievement. **Coloniality** names the persistent logic of colonial domination through Eurocentric ways of knowing that marginalize non‑Western epistemologies. **Decoloniality** seeks to center Indigenous and other suppressed worldviews, emphasizing plural modernities and alternative forms of knowledge.  

Quijano identifies two founding processes in the **colonization of the Americas**: first, the partition of humanity into the “West” (conquerors) and the “Rest” (conquered); second, the creation of racialized structures of labor control—slavery, the **encomienda**, and the **mita**—that supported the first global capitalist system. In this configuration, race became a mental category of modernity linking bodily difference to economic exploitation.  

**Eurocentrism**, described as the Eurocentrification of world capitalism, universalized European experience and portrayed other regions as “underdeveloped.” This ideology reinforced global hierarchies through binaries such as “developed/underdeveloped” and “First World/Third World.” The lecture extends this critique to **post‑independence governance**, questioning how democracy, development, and national identity in Latin America remain shaped by Eurocentric standards of progress.  

Subsequent discussion of **heterogeneity and discontinuity** (Slide 2.7) challenges European linear models of history. Non‑Western frameworks such as the **Aymara concept of *pachakuti*** express cyclical, locally grounded transformations. Historian **Rebecca Earle’s** analysis of *Indianesque nationalism* illustrates how early nation‑builders idealized an Indigenous past while excluding living Indigenous peoples, and how the ideology of **mestizaje** fostered myths of racial harmony that masked enduring inequalities.  

The lecture closes by foregrounding **Quijano’s call for decoloniality**—to free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts collective self‑understanding and to continue exploring, through assigned media and class discussion, how colonial patterns of power and knowledge might be dismantled.
