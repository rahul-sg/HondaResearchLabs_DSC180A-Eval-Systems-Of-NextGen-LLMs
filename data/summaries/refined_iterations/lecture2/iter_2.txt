This lecture extends the Week 1 foundations by demonstrating how financial statements interconnect and how the double‑entry system ensures that the accounting equation remains balanced. It begins by noting that economic activity is complex and that representing it accurately requires judgment and critical thinking.  

The session first reviews the **financial accounting framework**. The four financial statements—balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—together communicate a company’s financial position and performance. The balance sheet presents assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time (beginning and end of a period), while the other statements summarize changes over the fiscal year. These reports are linked: the income statement explains how revenues and expenses determine net income; that net income changes retained earnings in the statement of stockholders’ equity; and the statement of cash flows reconciles the change in cash between consecutive balance sheets. Slides also clarify that revenues increase stockholders’ equity, expenses reduce it, and dividends lower retained earnings.  

The lecture then expands the **accounting equation** from A = L + SE to A = L + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings. Retained earnings evolve from their beginning balance plus net income (revenues − expenses) minus dividends. This expanded form formally connects all statements and provides the basis for recording transactions.  

The core of the lecture introduces **double‑entry recordkeeping**, where every economic event has two sides—a “give” and a “take.” Each transaction affects at least two accounts and must keep total debits equal to total credits. Debits increase assets and decrease liabilities and equity; credits do the opposite. The slides illustrate this with sequential examples: (1) receiving a $100,000 bank loan, (2) purchasing equipment for $60,000 cash, (3) buying $15,000 inventory on account, (4) paying $8,000 of that account payable, (5) paying $600 cash for insurance, and (6) issuing shares for $100,000. For each transaction, T‑accounts show the debit and credit effects, and final account balances are carried forward as beginning balances for the next period.  

Finally, the lecture outlines the **accounting process**: analyze each transaction, record debits and credits as journal entries, and post them to T‑accounts (the ledger). The DEALOR mnemonic summarizes normal balances—Dividends, Expenses, and Assets are debit‑balance accounts; Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, and Revenues are credit‑balance accounts. Every journal entry keeps debits = credits, maintaining the fundamental equation A = L + SE.
