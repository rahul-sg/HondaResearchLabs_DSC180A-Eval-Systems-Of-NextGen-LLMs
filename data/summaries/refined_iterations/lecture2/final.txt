This lecture develops a deeper understanding of how financial statements connect through the accounting equation and how double-entry bookkeeping ensures those connections remain balanced over time. It begins by emphasizing that accounting interprets complex economic activity and communicates it through four interrelated financial statements: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. The balance sheet shows a company’s position at the beginning and end of a period, while the other statements explain the changes between those points. Revenues and expenses determine net income; net income affects retained earnings; and retained earnings change stockholders’ equity, linking performance over time to financial position.

The lecture revisits and expands the accounting equation from Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity into Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings (Beginning of Period + Revenues – Expenses – Dividends). This expanded version ties operational results to equity and clarifies how balances carry forward between periods. It also provides the basis for recording each economic event as an equal set of debits and credits.

Double-entry accounting is introduced as a framework in which every transaction has two sides—a give and a take—that must keep the equation balanced. Debits (left side) increase assets, expenses, and dividends, while credits (right side) increase liabilities, contributed capital, and revenues. No negative numbers are used; each transaction must include at least one debit and one credit, with debits equaling credits. The DEALOR mnemonic (Dividends, Expenses, Assets = Debit; Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, Revenues = Credit) helps recall normal balances.

Step-by-step examples illustrate this process: borrowing $100,000 from a bank, purchasing equipment and inventory, paying part of the amount owed, prepaying insurance, and issuing stock. The resulting journal entries are translated into T‑accounts that accumulate increases and decreases to compute ending balances. Slides demonstrate how formulas such as Beginning Balance + Increases – Decreases = Ending Balance generate the figures appearing on the balance sheet, which then become beginning balances in the next period.

Finally, the lecture summarizes the accounting cycle to this stage: analyze transactions, record them as journal entries (listing debits first, credits indented), post them to the ledger, and verify that Debits = Credits maintains Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity. This structure ensures accuracy and continuity across reporting periods.
