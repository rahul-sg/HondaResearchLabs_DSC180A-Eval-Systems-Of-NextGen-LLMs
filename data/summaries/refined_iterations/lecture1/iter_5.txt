The opening lecture of **Principles of Accounting** introduces financial accounting as the **language of business**, explaining how it communicates a company’s economic story—its successes, challenges, and risks—in measurable financial terms. Accounting serves two key functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records business transactions, and **communication**, which conveys that information through financial statements for external decision makers.

The **financial accounting framework** highlights why accounting matters and who uses it. **Investors** and **creditors** rely on financial data to evaluate profitability and repayment ability; **suppliers** consider it when extending credit; **management** and the **board of directors** use it to guide strategy and assess performance; and **employees**, **customers**, regulators, and analysts depend on it to judge financial stability, service reliability, or compliance.

Financial accounting measures three business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders such as stock issuance or borrowing), **investing** (acquiring or disposing of productive assets like buildings or equipment), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production, marketing, and sales). The results of these activities appear in four core **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—which together report a company’s financial position and performance.

Public companies communicate this information through the **financial reporting process**, filing periodic statements with the SEC: the **10‑K** annual report with audited financials and disclosure notes, the **10‑Q** quarterly report with unaudited figures, and the **8‑K** for significant current events such as acquisitions or leadership changes. Reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** to ensure comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** enforces these rules and delegates standard‑setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**; internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS**. Independent **auditors**, required for SEC‑registered firms, lend credibility by expressing professional opinions on whether statements follow GAAP and are free from material misstatement—issuing unqualified, explanatory, qualified, or adverse opinions.

The lecture concludes with the **balance sheet** and the **accounting equation**—**Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**—demonstrated through the Klein Inc. example. Each transaction carries a **dual effect**: issuing stock or obtaining a loan increases both assets and financing claims; purchasing equipment or prepaid rent alters asset composition without changing totals; buying supplies on account raises both assets and liabilities. These examples reinforce the principle that every transaction maintains balance and links a company’s resources to the claims upon them.
