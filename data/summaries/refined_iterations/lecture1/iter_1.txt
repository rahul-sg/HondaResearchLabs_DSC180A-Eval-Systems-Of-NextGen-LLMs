The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the role, scope, and purpose of financial accounting as the foundation for recording and communicating a company’s economic activities. Accounting is described as the language of business—telling an organization’s financial story, including its successes, failures, opportunities, and risks. Its two primary functions are **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys those results to decision makers through financial statements.

Students are introduced to who uses this information and why. **Investors** evaluate management performance and financial condition; **creditors** and **suppliers** assess repayment ability and reliability; **management** and the **board of directors** monitor operations and strategy; and **employees**, **customers**, and others—such as analysts and regulators—use financial data to make informed choices about employment, purchasing, or oversight.

Financial accounting measures three categories of business activity:  
1. **Financing activities**, involving transactions with investors and creditors such as stock issuance or borrowing;  
2. **Investing activities**, the acquisition and disposal of resources required to produce and sell goods or services; and  
3. **Operating activities**, the day‑to‑day processes of producing, marketing, and delivering those goods and services.

These activities culminate in four primary financial statements—**the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows**—that communicate a company’s performance and financial position. The reporting process follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, established by the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) under oversight of the SEC. International reporting uses **IFRS** issued by the IASB. To ensure credibility, independent **auditors** assess whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free from material misstatements, issuing one of four opinions ranging from unqualified to adverse.

The lecture emphasizes the **balance sheet** as a snapshot of financial position at a specific point in time. It reflects the **accounting equation**—**Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**—which must always remain balanced because every transaction has a dual effect. Assets represent resources providing future benefit; liabilities are obligations to creditors; and stockholders’ equity reflects owners’ residual claims. Through examples such as issuing stock, borrowing funds, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, or buying supplies on account, the lecture demonstrates how each transaction changes accounts yet keeps the equation intact—illustrating the conceptual framework that underlies all financial reporting.
