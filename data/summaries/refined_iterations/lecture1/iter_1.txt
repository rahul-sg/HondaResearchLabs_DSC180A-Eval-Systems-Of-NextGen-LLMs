The first lectures introduce accounting as the *language of business*, explaining that it records a company’s economic activities and communicates those results to decision makers. Accounting tells a firm’s financial story—its successes, risks, and challenges—so that investors, creditors, managers, employees, suppliers, customers, and others can make informed decisions. The course emphasizes understanding how financial statements are created, interpreted, and used to evaluate performance and guide choices.

Financial accounting organizes information about three primary activities—financing, investing, and operating—into four key reports: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. These financial statements are governed by Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP), ensuring consistent and comparable reporting. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC) requires publicly traded companies to issue periodic statements and delegates standard‑setting authority to the Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB); internationally, the International Accounting Standards Board (IASB) issues International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). Independent auditors, required for public companies, enhance the credibility of financial reports by verifying compliance with GAAP and issuing opinions that may be unqualified, qualified, or in rare cases, adverse. Firms communicate results through regular filings such as the annual 10‑K, quarterly 10‑Q, and event‑driven 8‑K reports.

The lectures then focus on the balance sheet, which presents a company’s financial position at a specific moment using the fundamental accounting equation: **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets are resources expected to yield future benefits; liabilities are obligations owed to outsiders; equity represents owners’ residual interest after debts are settled. Each transaction must maintain this balance, demonstrating the dual effect principle. Example transactions for a start‑up—issuing stock, obtaining bank loans, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and buying supplies on credit—show how financing and investing activities shape total resources. The completed balance sheet lists assets and liabilities in order of liquidity or maturity and separates owners’ equity into common stock and retained earnings. Together, these concepts establish how accounting identifies, measures, and communicates financial information as the essential tool for understanding and evaluating business performance.
