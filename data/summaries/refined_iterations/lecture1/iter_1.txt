The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces students to the role and framework of financial accounting as the foundation for understanding business performance. Accounting is described as the language of business—the process of telling a company’s financial story through data on operations, risks, and opportunities. Its two main functions are recordkeeping—identifying, measuring, and recording economic events in value terms—and communication—providing this information to external decision makers through financial statements.

The lecture emphasizes that many groups use accounting information. Investors and shareholders assess management performance and financial condition, creditors and suppliers evaluate the company’s ability to repay or maintain reliable relationships, management and the board use reports to monitor performance and guide strategy, and others—such as employees, customers, analysts, and regulators—use the information for their own decisions.  

Financial accounting captures three types of activities. **Financing activities** involve transactions with investors and creditors, such as issuing stock or borrowing. **Investing activities** cover acquiring or disposing of assets like equipment, land, or inventory. **Operating activities** include production, promotion, and sales of goods or services. The results of these activities are communicated through the four core financial statements: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows.

Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, which guide what, when, and how economic events are reported. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees standards for public firms and delegates their development to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS**. Public companies must have their reports independently audited, with auditors providing an opinion—unqualified, qualified, adverse, or with explanatory paragraphs—on whether statements comply with GAAP and are free from material misstatements.

The lecture concludes by focusing on the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific date based on the accounting equation: **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets represent resources with future economic benefits; liabilities are obligations owed to creditors; and equity is the owners’ residual claim. Through examples such as issuing stock, taking a loan, purchasing equipment, paying rent, or buying supplies on credit, the lecture illustrates that every transaction affects at least two accounts but always keeps the equation in balance—demonstrating the fundamental logic that underpins all financial reporting.
