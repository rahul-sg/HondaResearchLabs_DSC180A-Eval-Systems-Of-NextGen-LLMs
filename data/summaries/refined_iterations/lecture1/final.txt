The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces financial accounting as both a measurement system and a communication framework—the language of business. Accounting translates a company’s operational activities, successes, and risks into structured financial information used to make informed decisions. Its two essential functions are **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which reports that information externally through standardized financial statements.

Financial information serves multiple users. **Investors** assess management performance and financial health; **creditors** and **suppliers** evaluate repayment ability and credit terms; **managers** and **boards of directors** gauge operations and strategic direction. Other stakeholders—**employees**, **customers**, **regulators**, and **analysts**—also rely on accounting data to judge a company’s reliability and stability.

Financial accounting captures three kinds of business activity: **financing** (transactions with investors and creditors, such as stock issuance or loans), **investing** (acquiring or disposing of productive resources like buildings or equipment), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production, marketing, and sales). These activities culminate in four core financial statements: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**.

The reporting process follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**—rules established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under the oversight of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**, which requires public companies to submit periodic reports: audited annual **10‑K** filings, unaudited interim **10‑Q** reports, and event‑driven **8‑K** disclosures. Globally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS** standards. Independent **auditors** enhance credibility by verifying GAAP compliance and issuing opinions—unqualified, unqualified with emphasis, qualified, or adverse—on whether statements are fairly presented.

The lecture emphasizes the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific date using the equation **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. **Assets** are resources with future benefits, **liabilities** are obligations to creditors, and **stockholders’ equity** represents owners’ residual claims. Through the *Klein, Inc.* example, students saw how issuing stock, borrowing funds, and making purchases all have **dual effects**—every transaction affects at least two accounts but the equation always remains balanced. This demonstration underscores how financial accounting systematically measures, records, and communicates the results of business decisions.
