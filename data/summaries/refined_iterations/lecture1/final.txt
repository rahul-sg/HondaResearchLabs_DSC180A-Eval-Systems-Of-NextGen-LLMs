This lecture introduces the foundational framework and purpose of financial accounting, presenting it as the **language of business**—a means of telling a company’s financial story, including its successes, challenges, and risks, through measurable economic events. Accounting performs two main functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records transactions in monetary terms, and **communication**, which summarizes and reports this information to external decision makers through financial statements.

A wide range of **users** rely on accounting information. Investors, creditors, suppliers, management, boards of directors, employees, customers, and others—such as analysts, regulators, and competitors—use financial data to evaluate performance, creditworthiness, and the reliability of an enterprise. Financial accounting captures three categories of business activity: **financing** (transactions with investors and creditors for funds), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of productive resources like equipment or inventory), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production, marketing, and sales). Results of these activities are communicated through four key **financial statements**: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**.

Preparation and presentation of these reports follow **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, which ensure consistency and comparability in reporting. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** requires public companies to issue periodic statements and delegates standard‑setting authority to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)**. Independent **auditors** provide an external evaluation of whether a company’s financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatement. Their opinions—unqualified, unqualified with explanation, qualified, or adverse—lend credibility to financial information used by decision makers.

The latter part of the lecture focuses on the **balance sheet**, which reports a firm’s financial position at a specific date, showing how its **assets** (resources providing future economic benefit) are funded by **liabilities** (obligations to creditors) and **stockholders’ equity** (owners’ residual claims). These categories are always in equilibrium under the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Because each transaction has a **dual effect**, increases or decreases on one side must be balanced by changes on the other.

Illustrative examples trace how common transactions—issuing stock to investors, obtaining bank loans, acquiring equipment, prepaying rent, and purchasing supplies on credit—affect total assets, liabilities, and equity while maintaining overall balance. By analyzing these transactions, students learn how financing, investing, and operating activities interconnect and how the balance sheet captures an organization’s financial structure at any point in time.
