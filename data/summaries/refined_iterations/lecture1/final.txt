The lecture introduces **financial accounting** as the process of recording, measuring, and communicating a company’s economic activities. Described as the *language of business*, accounting conveys a company’s financial story—its performance, opportunities, and risks—in monetary terms. It serves two key functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies and records economic events, and **communication**, which provides financial information to external and internal decision‑makers.

A wide range of users rely on accounting information: investors, creditors, suppliers, managers, boards of directors, employees, customers, and others such as regulators and analysts. Financial accounting focuses on three major types of business activities—**financing** (transactions with owners and lenders), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of productive resources), and **operating** (the daily production and sale of goods and services). These activities generate the data summarized in four interrelated **financial statements**: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**, supplemented by disclosure notes. Public companies communicate this information through required filings: the **10‑K** (annual, audited), **10‑Q** (quarterly, unaudited), and **8‑K** (reports of major events).

Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, which ensure that information is comparable across firms. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees reporting for public companies and delegates standard‑setting authority to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)**. Independent **auditors**, required for publicly traded companies, evaluate whether statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements. Their opinions—unqualified, unqualified with explanatory paragraph, qualified, or adverse—enhance the credibility of financial reports.

The lecture concludes with the **balance sheet**, which presents a company’s financial position at a specific date. It reports **assets** (resources with future benefit), **liabilities** (obligations to creditors), and **stockholders’ equity** (owners’ residual interests). These elements must satisfy the **accounting equation**, *Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity*, meaning every transaction has a dual effect that keeps the statement balanced. Step‑by‑step examples using a start‑up company illustrate how issuing stock, borrowing from a bank, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and buying supplies on account all alter resources and claims while maintaining equality. Through these examples, students see how business transactions are systematically captured to depict a firm’s overall financial condition.
