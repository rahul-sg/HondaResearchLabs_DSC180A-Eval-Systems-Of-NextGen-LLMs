The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces financial accounting as the foundation for recording and communicating a company’s economic activities. Accounting is presented as the language of business—a means of telling an organization’s financial story that captures its successes, challenges, and risks. It serves two essential functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to decision makers through financial statements.

The lecture outlines the many **users of financial information** and their goals. **Investors** evaluate management performance and financial condition; **creditors** and **suppliers** assess a firm’s ability to repay or maintain reliable relationships; **management** and the **board of directors** rely on financial data to plan strategy and assess results; while **employees**, **customers**, and other groups—such as analysts, regulators, and competitors—use financial reports to make informed employment, purchasing, or oversight decisions.

Financial accounting measures three broad types of business activity:  
1. **Financing**, encompassing transactions with investors and lenders that provide capital;  
2. **Investing**, involving acquisition or disposal of resources used to produce and sell goods and services; and  
3. **Operating**, which includes the revenue‑generating activities of daily business such as production, promotion, and sales.  

These processes lead to the preparation of four periodic **financial statements**—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—that communicate performance and financial position. Reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, the framework of rules ensuring comparability and consistency. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** holds legal authority for establishing accounting standards for public companies but delegates standard‑setting responsibility to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. International standards are issued by the **IASB** under **IFRS**. Independent **auditors** add credibility by determining whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements, issuing one of four opinions—unqualified, explanatory, qualified, or adverse.

A central topic is the **balance sheet**, a snapshot of a firm’s financial position at a given date. It reflects the **accounting equation—Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**, which must always remain balanced because every transaction has a dual effect. Assets provide future benefit, liabilities are obligations, and stockholders’ equity represents owners’ residual claims. Step‑by‑step examples from “Klein, Inc.” show how issuing stock, obtaining loans, buying equipment, paying rent in advance, or purchasing supplies on account each alter accounts while preserving this balance—the core logic underlying all financial reporting.
