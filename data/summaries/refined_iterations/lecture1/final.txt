The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the foundations and purpose of financial accounting—how organizations record, summarize, and communicate business activities to support informed decisions. Accounting is described as the **“language of business”**, capturing a company’s financial story of performance, opportunities, and risks. It serves two essential functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which provides this information to external users through financial statements.  

The lecture emphasizes the diverse **users of financial information** and how they rely on accounting data. Investors judge company performance and value; creditors evaluate repayment ability; suppliers and customers assess reliability; management and boards of directors use reports to monitor operations and strategic goals; and employees, regulators, analysts, and competitors use financial statements to inform decisions.  

Financial accounting measures three types of **business activities**:  
- **Financing activities**, which include transactions with investors and creditors such as issuing stock or borrowing funds.  
- **Investing activities**, involving the acquisition or disposal of assets like equipment, land, and inventory.  
- **Operating activities**, relating to daily transactions for producing and selling goods or services.  

The results of these activities are summarized in four key **financial statements**—the *Balance Sheet*, *Income Statement*, *Statement of Stockholders’ Equity*, and *Statement of Cash Flows*. These reports communicate a company’s financial position (resources and claims), performance, and cash movements to external users.  

Financial reporting must follow **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** to ensure consistency and comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** enforces reporting for public firms and delegates standard-setting authority to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues the IFRS framework. Independent **auditors** evaluate whether statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements, providing one of four opinions: unqualified, unqualified with an explanatory paragraph, qualified, or adverse—each affecting the credibility of financial disclosures.  

A central focus is the **Balance Sheet**, which presents a company’s financial position at a point in time, governed by the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets represent resources with future benefits; liabilities, obligations to creditors; and equity, owners’ residual claims. Through examples using the fictional company *Klein, Inc.*, the lecture illustrates how issuing stock, securing loans, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, or buying supplies on credit each affect the equation while maintaining its balance.
