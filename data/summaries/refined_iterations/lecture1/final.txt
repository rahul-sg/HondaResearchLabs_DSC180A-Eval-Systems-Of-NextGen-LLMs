The lecture introduces **financial accounting** as the *language of business*—a system for recording and communicating a company’s economic activities and financial condition. Accounting conveys a firm’s story of performance, challenges, and opportunities so that users can make informed decisions. It performs two main functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records business events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which provides relevant information to a wide range of **decision makers** including investors, creditors, managers, employees, customers, boards of directors, and regulators.  

Financial accounting captures three key types of activities:  
- **Financing activities**—transactions with owners and lenders that raise or repay capital.  
- **Investing activities**—the acquisition and disposal of resources needed to operate, such as buildings, equipment, or inventory.  
- **Operating activities**—day‑to‑day transactions tied to producing and selling goods or services.  

The results of these activities appear in four primary **financial statements**: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**. These reports work together to show how a company obtains and uses resources over time.  

All financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, which promote consistency and comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees standards for publicly traded firms and delegates standard setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. Global practices are guided by the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** under **IFRS**. Independent **auditors** review financial statements to determine whether they comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements, issuing opinions that add credibility for external users.  

A focal point of the lecture is the **balance sheet**, which presents a company’s financial position at a specific date. It reports:  
- **Assets**—resources owned that provide future economic benefit (e.g., cash, equipment, land).  
- **Liabilities**—obligations owed to others (e.g., notes payable, salaries payable).  
- **Stockholders’ equity**—owners’ residual interest after liabilities.  

These categories are governed by the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**, which must remain balanced in every transaction. Examples using *Klein, Inc.* demonstrate this logic: issuing stock or obtaining a bank loan increases both cash and claims on the firm; purchasing equipment or paying prepaid rent shifts amounts among asset accounts; and buying supplies on credit raises assets and liabilities equally. Every event affects at least two accounts but keeps the overall equation intact.  

Collectively, these concepts outline the **financial accounting framework**—a structured approach to measuring, recording, and communicating business activities. Understanding how transactions flow through this system enables more accurate interpretation of financial statements and more informed business decisions.
