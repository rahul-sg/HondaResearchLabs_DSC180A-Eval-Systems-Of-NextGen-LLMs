The first *Principles of Accounting* lecture introduces accounting as the **language of business**, describing how it records and communicates a company’s financial story—its performance, resources, and obligations—to support sound decisions. Accounting serves two main purposes: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records transactions in monetary terms, and **communication**, which reports this information through financial statements.

The lecture reviews the wide range of **users** who rely on accounting data: **investors**, **creditors**, **suppliers**, **management**, **boards of directors**, **employees**, **customers**, and others such as **analysts, regulators, and competitors**. Each uses financial information to assess profitability, credit risk, organizational health, or strategic opportunities.

Financial accounting measures three categories of business activity:  
- **Financing** – obtaining resources from and repaying investors and creditors,  
- **Investing** – acquiring and disposing of assets needed for production and sales, and  
- **Operating** – day‑to‑day business functions like production, marketing, and administration.  

These activities feed into the **financial reporting process**, which summarizes economic events in accordance with **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees standard setting for public companies and delegates that role to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)** for global comparability.

Companies communicate results through four key **financial statements**—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—along with footnotes and disclosures. Public firms file annual (**10‑K**), quarterly (**10‑Q**), and current (**8‑K**) reports to share this information.  

Independent **auditors** examine whether statements comply with GAAP and are free from material misstatement, providing credibility for decision makers. Their opinions can be unqualified, unqualified with explanatory note, qualified, or adverse.

The lecture focuses on the **balance sheet**, which presents a company’s **financial position** at a specific date, showing resources (**assets**) and claims to those resources (**liabilities** and **stockholders’ equity**) under the **accounting equation**:  
**Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity.**  
Assets provide future benefit (e.g., cash, land, equipment, supplies); liabilities represent obligations (e.g., payables, loans); and stockholders’ equity is the owners’ residual interest after liabilities are settled. Every transaction has a dual effect that keeps this equation in balance.

Step‑by‑step examples—issuing stock, borrowing from a bank, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and buying supplies on credit—demonstrate how **financing**, **investing**, and **operating** activities change assets, liabilities, and equity while maintaining equilibrium. The completed balance sheet then summarizes these relationships as a snapshot of the company’s resources and their financing sources, establishing the foundation for understanding how accounting information supports effective business analysis and decision making.
