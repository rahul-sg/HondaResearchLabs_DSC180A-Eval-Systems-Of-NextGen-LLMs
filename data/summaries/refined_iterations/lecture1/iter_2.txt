The first lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the purpose and structure of financial accounting and the framework it uses to describe business performance. Accounting is presented as the **language of business**, telling a company’s financial story—its successes, risks, and opportunities—through recorded and communicated financial information. It performs two central functions: **recordkeeping**, which involves identifying, measuring, and recording economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to decision makers through financial statements.

The course begins with the **financial accounting framework**, emphasizing how accounting information supports decision‑making by various users. Investors and shareholders evaluate management performance and financial condition; creditors and suppliers assess a firm’s ability to repay or maintain business relationships; management and boards use reports to guide strategy; and others—such as employees, customers, analysts, and regulators—draw on these reports for their own assessments.

Financial accounting organizes business activity into three categories. **Financing activities** involve transactions with investors and creditors, such as stock issuance or borrowing. **Investing activities** include acquiring or disposing of assets needed for operations, like land, buildings, or inventory. **Operating activities** encompass the daily production, promotion, and sale of goods or services. Together, these activities generate information summarized in four key reports: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**.

Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, which ensure consistency and comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** requires public companies to report under GAAP and delegates standard setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**; internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS**. Independent auditors, required for public firms, examine financial statements to determine whether they comply with GAAP and are free from material misstatements. Their resulting opinions—unqualified, qualified, adverse, or explanatory—add credibility for external users.

The lecture ends by illustrating the **balance sheet** and the **accounting equation**—**Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**—which must always remain in balance. Assets represent economic resources, liabilities are obligations to creditors, and equity is the residual claim of owners. Through examples of issuing stock, taking loans, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and buying supplies on credit, students see how each transaction affects multiple accounts but preserves the equality that underlies all financial reporting.
