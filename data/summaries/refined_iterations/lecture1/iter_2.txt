The first lectures introduce accounting as the **language of business**, described as the system that identifies, measures, and communicates a company’s economic activities. Accounting tells the firm’s financial story—its successes, challenges, opportunities, and risks—to inform decisions by investors, creditors, managers, suppliers, customers, employees, directors, and other stakeholders. Its two primary functions are recordkeeping of operations and communication of results through financial reports.

Financial accounting captures three main business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of productive resources), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production and selling). These activities are summarized in four core **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—which together convey a company’s financial position and performance.

All reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, providing consistency and comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** requires public companies to issue financial statements and delegates standard‑setting authority to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**; globally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** establishes International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS). Independent **auditors** add credibility by assessing whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements, issuing opinions that may be unqualified, qualified, include emphasis paragraphs, or—rarely—be adverse. Public companies communicate results through regular filings such as the annual **10‑K**, quarterly **10‑Q**, and event‑specific **8‑K** reports.

The lecture then focuses on the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific date and must satisfy the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**.  
- **Assets** are resources expected to generate future benefits, such as cash, equipment, land, supplies, or patents.  
- **Liabilities** represent obligations to creditors, including payables and notes payable.  
- **Stockholders’ equity** is the residual interest of owners after liabilities are met, typically shown as common stock and retained earnings.  

Each transaction affects at least two accounts but keeps the equation in balance, demonstrating the **dual effect principle**. Examples of a new company illustrate this flow: issuing stock and taking a bank loan provide financing; purchasing equipment and prepaying rent are investing activities; acquiring supplies on credit increases both assets and liabilities. The resulting balance sheet organizes assets and liabilities by liquidity or maturity and confirms equality between total resources and total claims. Together, these concepts establish how accounting systematically records, summarizes, and communicates financial information to support sound business decision‑making.
