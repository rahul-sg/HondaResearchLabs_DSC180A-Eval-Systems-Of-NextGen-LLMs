The first lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the foundations of financial accounting and how it conveys a company’s economic story. Accounting is presented as the **language of business**, depicting an organization’s successes, challenges, and risks in financial terms. It serves two main purposes: **recordkeeping**, which involves identifying, measuring, and documenting economic events, and **communication**, which summarizes that information in financial statements for external decision makers.

The **financial accounting framework** explains why accounting matters and who uses its information. **Investors** and **shareholders** evaluate management performance and financial condition, **creditors** assess repayment ability, and **suppliers** gauge reliability before extending credit or expanding relationships. **Management** and the **board of directors** use results to plan strategy and measure performance, while **employees**, **customers**, **analysts**, and **regulators** rely on the same reports to judge security, service reliability, or compliance.

Financial accounting organizes activity into three categories: **financing** (transactions with investors and creditors such as loans or stock issues), **investing** (acquiring or selling productive resources like buildings, land, and equipment), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production, marketing, and sales). These activities generate the four core **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—that describe the business’s financial position and performance.

Public companies report periodically through the **financial reporting process**. They file **Form 10‑K** annually with audited statements and disclosure notes, **Form 10‑Q** quarterly with unaudited figures, and **Form 8‑K** after major events such as acquisitions or executive departures. These reports follow **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** to ensure consistency and comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** enforces these standards, while delegating rule‑making authority to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**; internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS**. Independent **auditors** enhance credibility by verifying compliance and expressing one of four opinion types: unqualified, explanatory, qualified, or adverse.

The lecture concludes with the **balance sheet** and the **accounting equation**—**Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Through simple examples, each transaction’s dual effect is illustrated: raising funds from stockholders or banks increases both assets and financing sources; purchasing equipment or prepaid rent shifts assets without changing totals; buying supplies on credit raises both assets and liabilities. Each example reinforces how accounting maintains balance and links resources to the claims against them.
