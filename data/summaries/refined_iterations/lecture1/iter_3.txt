Accounting is introduced as the **language of business**—a system that identifies, measures, and communicates a company’s economic activities to tell its financial story and support decision‑making. It reveals successes, challenges, risks, and opportunities, helping stakeholders such as investors, creditors, suppliers, employees, managers, customers, and directors evaluate a firm’s condition and performance. Accounting serves two main roles: **recordkeeping** of economic events in monetary terms and **communication** of results to users through formal reports.

Financial accounting tracks three categories of activities: **financing** (transactions with owners and lenders), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of productive resources), and **operating** (daily production, marketing, and administration). Information from these activities is summarized in four key **financial statements**: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. Together they provide a picture of financial position and performance that underlies informed business decisions.

Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** to ensure consistency and comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** enforces reporting requirements for public firms and delegates standard‑setting authority to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**; internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)**. Independent **auditors**, required for public companies, enhance credibility by evaluating whether statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatement, issuing one of four opinions: unqualified, unqualified with explanatory emphasis, qualified, or (rarely) adverse. Public companies communicate results mainly through **10‑K** annual reports, **10‑Q** quarterly filings, and **8‑K** current reports announcing significant events.

The main statement introduced is the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific point in time and must satisfy the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**.  
- **Assets** are resources expected to yield future benefits, such as cash, equipment, land, supplies, or patents.  
- **Liabilities** represent obligations—amounts owed to creditors like notes or accounts payable.  
- **Stockholders’ equity** reflects owners’ residual claims on resources after liabilities, appearing mainly as common stock or retained earnings.  

Every transaction has a **dual effect** that keeps the equation balanced. Lecture examples trace how a new business finances operations by issuing stock and obtaining loans, invests in equipment and prepaid rent, and buys supplies on credit. These actions show how financing and investing activities create resources while operations change their form without altering total assets. The resulting balance sheet organizes assets and liabilities by liquidity or maturity and confirms the equality of total resources and total claims. Collectively, these concepts establish how accounting systematically records, summarizes, and communicates information for sound economic decisions.
