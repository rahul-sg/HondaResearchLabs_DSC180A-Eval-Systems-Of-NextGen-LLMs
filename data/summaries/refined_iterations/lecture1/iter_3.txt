The first lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the purpose and structure of financial accounting and how it communicates a company’s financial story. Accounting is described as the **language of business**, revealing a firm’s successes, risks, and opportunities through recorded information. It serves two main functions: **recordkeeping**, in which economic events are identified, measured, and recorded in value terms, and **communication**, in which this information is organized into financial statements for decision makers.

The lecture begins with the **financial accounting framework**, explaining why accounting matters and who uses it. Investors and shareholders use reports to assess management performance and financial condition; creditors and suppliers evaluate a company’s ability to repay or sustain business relationships; management and boards rely on results to guide strategy and evaluate operations. Employees, customers, analysts, and regulators also interpret these reports to judge financial health, reliability, or compliance.

Financial accounting groups business activity into three categories. **Financing activities** involve transactions with investors and creditors, such as issuing stock or taking loans. **Investing activities** cover acquiring and disposing of resources that support operations, including buildings, land, or inventory. **Operating activities** consist of daily production, promotion, and sales. These activities feed into the four major **financial statements**: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows.

Reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, ensuring consistency and comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** requires public firms to report under GAAP and delegates standard setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**; globally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS**. Independent **auditors** examine financial statements to confirm GAAP compliance and absence of material misstatements, providing one of four opinion types—unqualified, explanatory, qualified, or adverse—to strengthen credibility for users.

The lecture concludes with the **balance sheet** and the **accounting equation**—**Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**—showing that each transaction has a dual effect. Through numerical examples, students see this equality preserved: a company raising \$1,000,000 from stockholders and \$500,000 from a bank increases both assets and sources of financing; purchasing \$240,000 of equipment or \$180,000 of prepaid rent simply shifts asset categories; buying \$14,000 of supplies on credit increases both assets and liabilities. The result is a balanced financial snapshot linking resources to the claims against them.
