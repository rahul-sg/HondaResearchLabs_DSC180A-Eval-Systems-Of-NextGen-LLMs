The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces financial accounting as the language of business—a systematic way of telling a company’s financial story that conveys its successes, failures, opportunities, challenges, risks, and uncertainties. Accounting rests on two key functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which reports this information through financial statements for use by internal and external decision makers. The class objective is to understand how financial statements are created and how accounting information supports sound business decisions across all fields.

The lecture highlights the broad range of **users of financial information** and their different purposes. **Investors** assess management performance and financial condition; **creditors** and **suppliers** evaluate repayment ability and the stability of relationships; **management** and the **board of directors** use financial data to plan strategy and review results; and **employees**, **customers**, analysts, regulators, and competitors rely on financial reports to judge an organization’s reliability or value.

Financial accounting measures and reports three categories of business activity:  
1. **Financing activities**—transactions with investors and creditors such as issuing stock or borrowing funds.  
2. **Investing activities**—acquisition and disposal of resources like buildings, land, or equipment.  
3. **Operating activities**—day‑to‑day transactions related to production, promotion, and sales.  

These activities feed into the preparation of the four major **financial statements**: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**. All are produced under **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, which ensure consistent and comparable reporting. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees public companies and delegates standard setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**, while the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues global standards known as **IFRS**. Independent **auditors** enhance credibility by judging whether statements comply with GAAP and are free of material errors, issuing unqualified, explanatory, qualified, or adverse opinions.

A major focus is the **balance sheet**, which presents a company’s financial position at a single point in time. It follows the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**, demonstrating that every transaction has a dual effect and the equation must always balance. Assets represent resources providing future benefit, liabilities reflect obligations to creditors, and stockholders’ equity shows owners’ residual claims. The step‑by‑step “Klein, Inc.” examples illustrate how transactions such as issuing stock, obtaining loans, acquiring equipment, prepaying rent, or buying supplies on credit change account balances while keeping total assets equal to total claims—a foundation for all financial reporting.
