The lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed, shaping identity, opportunity, and schooling. It began with reflection questions prompting students to consider whether they had been labeled by teachers or administrators, how those labels affected their self‑views, and whether such labels reflected or distorted their full selves. This activity, paired with discussion of “Silence, Fear, and Denial,” emphasized that while society and schools often avoid conversations about race or cultural difference, genuine understanding requires courage to face how upbringing and environment shape one’s perspectives.  

Drawing on Sonia Nieto, the lecture explained that society classifies people by visible and invisible traits, forming exaggerated stereotypes that advantage some and disadvantage others. Scientists and social scientists widely accept that race is not biologically determined but developed through historical, political, and economic forces that made racial categories appear “common sense.” The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated scientific efforts to dispel racial myths.  

Legal scholar Ian Haney López showed that law has been instrumental in defining race. “Law as coercion” described how naturalization, immigration, and marriage laws shaped racial categories by determining who could access citizenship and rights. For instance, Armenians were granted whiteness through court approval, while Japanese and Mexican individuals were legally positioned as outsiders. Laws linked racial classification to social status—Irish and Italians became “White” to legitimate their citizenship. The U.S. Census institutionalized these evolving categories, forcing individuals to identify within limited, often problematic, options. James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” echoed how legal and social coercion constructed whiteness. “Law as ideology” explained how race continues to be normalized through seemingly neutral systems—such as criminal justice, schooling, and media—that sustain inequalities without explicit mention of race.  

The lecture then shifted to the social construction of culture. Nieto defined culture as the ever‑changing values, relationships, and worldviews created and transformed by human interaction, not inherited genetically. Culture is complex, dynamic, hybrid, and multifaceted; everyone has culture and selects or rejects aspects shaped by family, peers, class, language, geography, and other mediating factors. Small et al. (2010) added that culture operates through “values,” “frames,” and “repertoires” guiding interpretation and action. Schools were analyzed as distinctive cultural environments whose routines, tracking, and beliefs about fixed ability reflect dominant assumptions that influence students’ engagement, learning, and visions of their futures.
