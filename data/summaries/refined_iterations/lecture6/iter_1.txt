This lecture focused on nested SQL queries—subqueries embedded inside other queries—and their logical meaning, practical forms, and optimization through unnesting. Building on previous coverage of projections, selections, joins, aggregates, and grouping, it explained that subqueries may appear in the SELECT, FROM, or WHERE clauses and can return either single values or entire relations. While sometimes necessary to express conditions that depend on computed tables, nested queries generally add complexity and can often be replaced by equivalent join or grouping formulations.

Subqueries in the **SELECT clause** were illustrated through examples that derive values such as a movie’s genre, actor counts, and average salaries. The slides highlighted that correlated subqueries (those referencing outer query variables) might return multiple values, creating ambiguity. Equivalent results are often achieved using joins (“subquery unnesting”), thus improving efficiency. However, the lecture cautioned that these rewritings are not always fully equivalent—particularly when outer joins are introduced, as demonstrated in slide 10 comparing inner and left outer join variants. 

Subqueries in the **FROM clause** serve as intermediate relations or temporary tables. The lecture demonstrated both inline subqueries and refactoring using common table expressions (WITH clauses), showing that these can clarify multi-step filtering without changing semantics. Such subqueries are non-correlated and can often be simplified further through unnesting.

Subqueries in the **WHERE clause** were tied to logical quantifiers. **Existential quantifiers**—implemented with `EXISTS` or `IN`—test whether related records satisfy specific conditions and naturally correspond to join queries. **Universal quantifiers**, expressing “for all” or “every,” are more difficult because SQL lacks direct syntax to represent them. They were emulated using `NOT IN` or `NOT EXISTS` to identify actors who worked only in action films or movies whose actors all earned above \$100K. The lecture also noted that the `ALL` operator is not supported in SQLite, motivating use of `NOT EXISTS` as a practical replacement.

The session concluded with the concept of **monotone and non-monotone queries**. Monotone queries—those that do not lose results when input data grows—can typically be unnested safely. In contrast, queries involving negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates are non-monotone and cannot always be transformed equivalently. This distinction links logical properties of queries to database optimization strategies such as subquery unnesting.
