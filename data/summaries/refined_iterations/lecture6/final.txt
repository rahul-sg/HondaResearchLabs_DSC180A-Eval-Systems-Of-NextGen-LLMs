This lecture expanded on **nested SQL queries (subqueries)**—queries embedded within others—and analyzed when they can or cannot be **unnested** into equivalent join forms. Building on prior SQL concepts (projections, selections, joins, aggregates, grouping, and updates), the lecture emphasized that subqueries can appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, or **WHERE** clause and may return either single values or full relations. Though nested queries help express conditions that rely on computed tables, the **rule of thumb** is to avoid them when an equivalent join or grouped query can achieve the same result.

In the **SELECT clause**, examples showed *correlated subqueries* used to retrieve information such as movie genres, actor counts, or average salaries. These were compared to equivalent join-based expressions through **subquery unnesting**, which often improves efficiency. However, Slide 10 demonstrated that equivalence breaks under **LEFT OUTER JOIN** semantics, since outer joins preserve unmatched rows, altering query meaning.

Subqueries in the **FROM clause** served as **derived tables** or **inline views**. The lecture illustrated refactoring a query into steps—first filtering movies with rating > 8 and then those < 9—using either subquery aliasing or a **common table expression (CTE)**. These are **non‑correlated subqueries**, and while the example could be unnested equivalently, this property depends on the specific case rather than being universally safe.

In the **WHERE clause**, subqueries expressed **quantifiers**. **EXISTS** and **IN** implement **existential quantifiers**, true when matching tuples exist, and are equivalent to join forms. **Universal quantifiers** (“for all”) are more complex. The lecture constructed such logic stepwise—for example, finding actors who acted *only in Action movies* by excluding those who appeared in any non‑Action movie. Similarly, queries retrieving movies where **all actors earned > $100K** showed use of `< ALL` (not supported in SQLite) and an equivalent **NOT EXISTS** version expressing the same condition.

The final section introduced **monotone vs. non‑monotone** queries. Using nested‑loop semantics, simple **SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE** queries without aggregates are **monotone**: adding tuples cannot remove existing results, so their subqueries can be safely unnested. In contrast, queries involving **negation**, **universal quantifiers**, or **aggregates** are **non‑monotone**, meaning unnesting would change their output. These properties explain why existential subqueries behave predictably under joins, while universal or negated ones resist unnesting.
