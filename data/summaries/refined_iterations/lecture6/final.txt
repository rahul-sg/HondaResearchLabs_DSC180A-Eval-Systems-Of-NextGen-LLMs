This lecture examined nested and subqueries in SQL, focusing on their structure, usage across different clauses, and their relationship to query monotonicity and optimization. Building on earlier SQL operations—projections, selections, joins, grouping, and aggregation—it introduced subqueries as queries embedded within other queries. These can appear in the SELECT, FROM, or WHERE clauses and may return either scalar values or full relations. Although powerful, nested queries are typically less efficient; thus, subquery unnesting, or rewriting them into equivalent join queries, is encouraged when possible.

Subqueries in the **SELECT** clause were illustrated with examples involving movies and actors: retrieving the genre of each actor’s movie, computing the number of actors per movie, or calculating the average salary of actors in highly rated films. These correlated subqueries depend on outer query variables and can often be replaced with equivalent join and GROUP BY queries, improving performance. However, the slides also showed that apparent alternatives are not always equivalent—particularly when outer joins (LEFT OUTER JOIN) are introduced—highlighting the need for care in comparing differently structured queries.

Subqueries in the **FROM** clause act as temporary tables or inline views. Using constructs such as `WITH` (common table expressions), one can refactor queries to improve readability while maintaining equivalent logic. The lecture demonstrated converting a subquery that filters movies by rating range into an unnested form with a standard WHERE condition, establishing that these two are equivalent.

Subqueries in the **WHERE** clause expressed logical quantification. **Existential quantifiers** (“there exists”) were represented using `EXISTS` and `IN`, enabling tests for presence or membership, such as finding actors who appeared in Sci‑Fi films. These queries can be rewritten as joins. **Universal quantifiers** (“for all”) were addressed next and presented greater difficulty, as SQL lacks built‑in universal operators. Examples included finding actors who only acted in action movies and movies whose actors all earned above $100K. Slides showed two equivalent formulations for the latter—using `$100K < ALL (...)` and `NOT EXISTS (...)`—and noted that the first is not supported in SQLite.

The lecture concluded by introducing **monotone and non‑monotone queries**. Monotone queries (those without negation, aggregation, or universal quantifiers) do not lose results when data is added and can usually be unnested. In contrast, non‑monotone queries—those involving negation, universals, or aggregates—cannot be safely unnested, linking logical expressiveness with practical optimization limits.
