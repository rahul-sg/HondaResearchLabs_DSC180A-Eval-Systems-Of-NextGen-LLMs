This lecture extended prior SQL topics—projections, selections, joins, grouping, and aggregation—by introducing **nested SQL queries (subqueries)**: queries embedded within another query. It examined where subqueries occur (SELECT, FROM, and WHERE clauses), what results they can return (single values or relations), and when they are necessary or can be simplified through **unnesting**.

In the **SELECT clause**, subqueries were shown to derive computed attributes such as a movie’s genre, actor count, or average salary. These examples illustrated **correlated subqueries**, which reference outer query variables and may return multiple values, potentially causing ambiguity. The lecture demonstrated that equivalent results often arise from rewriting the query using joins or group-by aggregation—a process called *subquery unnesting*. However, slide 10 emphasized that unnesting is not always semantically identical: rewriting nested queries as joins can change results when outer versus inner joins are used, so equivalence must be verified carefully.

Subqueries in the **FROM clause** act as intermediate results. The lecture illustrated inline subqueries and refactoring using **common table expressions (WITH clauses)**, showing that such non‑correlated subqueries can simplify multi-step filtering. These can also be flattened into equivalent single-level queries through unnesting without changing meaning.

Subqueries in the **WHERE clause** introduced logical quantifiers. **Existential quantifiers**, expressed with `EXISTS` or `IN`, test for the presence of related records and correspond naturally to join operations. **Universal quantifiers**, meaning “for all,” require more complex constructs because SQL lacks built-in support. They were expressed using `NOT IN` or `NOT EXISTS` (and theoretically `ALL`, which SQLite does not support) to capture statements such as “actors who acted only in action films” or “movies where every actor’s salary exceeds \$100K.” Stepwise examples showed how to identify non-qualifying tuples first, then invert the selection to express universal conditions.

The lecture concluded with the notion of **monotone versus non‑monotone queries**. A query is monotone if adding data never removes results, and slide 33 provided a formal theorem: *A SELECT–FROM–WHERE query without subqueries or aggregates is monotone.* Such queries can be safely unnested. In contrast, queries containing negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates are **non‑monotone**, meaning their unnesting may not preserve results. This logical property connects query semantics directly to optimization strategies in SQL.
