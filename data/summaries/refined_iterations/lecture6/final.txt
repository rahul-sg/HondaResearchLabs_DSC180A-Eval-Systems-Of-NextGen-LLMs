This lecture explored **nested SQL queries**—queries written inside other queries—and how their placement and structure affect meaning and optimization. Building on earlier SQL features (projection, selection, joins, and aggregation), it defined **subqueries** as inner queries that supply values or relations needed by an outer query. Subqueries can appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, or **WHERE** clauses and may return a single value or a full table.  

A key guideline is to **avoid nesting when possible**, since equivalent results are often obtainable through joins or grouping. Slides illustrated this “subquery unnesting” for queries such as finding each actor’s movie genre or computing the average actor salary for highly rated films. These examples compared correlated subqueries with their join-based counterparts and showed that small changes—like using an outer join—can alter semantics and make queries non‑equivalent.  

In the **FROM** clause, subqueries serve as **derived tables**, allowing complex conditions to be isolated and reused. The lecture demonstrated “subquery refactoring” using the **WITH** clause (common table expression) to improve readability without changing results; these can often also be unnested.  

Subqueries in the **WHERE** clause introduced **logical quantifiers**. Constructs such as **EXISTS** and **IN** capture *existential quantification* (“there exists” or “for some”), which align closely with join operations and are relatively straightforward. The lecture then covered **universal quantifiers** (“for all” or “every”), which SQL lacks direct syntax for. The slides broke this logic into a clear two‑step method:  
1. Identify actors who have participated in at least one non‑Action movie.  
2. Retrieve those **not in** that result—actors who acted *only* in Action movies.  
Additional examples used **NOT EXISTS** or comparison forms like `$100K < ALL(...)` to check conditions true for every tuple (e.g., all actors in a movie earning above \$100K). These formulations illustrated that universal quantifiers often require **negation** and thus differ fundamentally from existential ones. A related case study—finding actors appearing in at most two Action movies—showed how aggregate conditions use **counting** rather than universal quantification.  

The lecture concluded with **query monotonicity**: a query is *monotone* if adding tuples to input relations never removes output tuples. A basic SELECT–FROM–WHERE query without subqueries or aggregation is provably monotone under nested‑loop semantics. The final theorem linked logic to optimization:  
- If a query is monotone, it **can be unnested**.  
- If it includes **universal quantifiers, negation, or aggregates**, it becomes **non‑monotone** and **cannot be unnested** without changing meaning.
