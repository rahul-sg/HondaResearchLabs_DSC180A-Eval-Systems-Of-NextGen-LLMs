This lecture explains how natural selection, genetics, and molecular inheritance together account for evolutionary change. Evolution is defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population across generations—a process observable only at the population level. Natural selection acts on variation among individuals competing for limited resources, favoring heritable traits that improve survival and reproduction; these advantages gradually shift allele frequencies. Darwin’s questions about variation, blending, and inheritance were answered through genetics. Artificial selection, seen in breeding of dogs and pigeons, illustrates how deliberate selection can rapidly alter trait frequencies.  

Evolutionary change can proceed gradually or rapidly depending on environmental and selective pressures; for instance, structural adaptations in aquatic organisms show selection’s shaping influence. Mendel’s pea plant experiments demonstrated that inheritance follows consistent, particulate patterns rather than blending. Organisms carry two alleles per gene, which combine to form genotypes that produce observable phenotypes. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, though dominance does not imply greater fitness. Punnett squares and related tools predict consistent genotype (1:2:1) and phenotype (3:1) ratios when heterozygotes are crossed.  

Genes are located on chromosomes, which contain DNA and associated proteins. Humans have 46 chromosomes organized in homologous pairs, while species differ widely in chromosome number. Mitosis generates identical somatic cells, whereas meiosis forms gametes with half the chromosome number, maintaining stability across generations and enhancing diversity through independent assortment and crossing over. Linkage and recombination of genes produce new allele combinations that increase variation.  

DNA’s double-helix structure, composed of complementary nucleotide pairs, stores and replicates genetic information. Mutations introduce new variation, providing the raw material for evolution. Mitochondrial DNA, found in mitochondria and inherited maternally without recombination, is especially useful for tracing ancestry. Molecular analyses of DNA support both practical and evolutionary applications—such as ancestry testing and forensics, as well as estimating divergence times, reconstructing phylogenetic relationships, and identifying common ancestors. Molecular clocks, which rely on relatively constant mutation rates, help estimate when evolutionary lineages diverged. Overall, the lecture shows that evolution results from genetic change within populations, driven by natural selection acting on heritable, molecularly based variation.
