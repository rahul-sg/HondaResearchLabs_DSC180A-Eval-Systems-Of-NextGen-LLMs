This lecture explores how natural selection, genetics, and molecular inheritance together explain evolutionary change. Evolution is defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population across generations—a process measurable only at the population level. Natural selection acts on variation among individuals competing for limited resources, favoring traits that enhance survival and reproduction. Heritable traits passed to offspring gradually shift allele frequencies. Darwin’s questions about variation, blending, and inheritance were later resolved through genetics. Artificial selection, such as the breeding of dogs and pigeons, demonstrates how selective pressures can quickly reshape trait frequencies.  

Evolutionary change can occur over long or short timescales depending on environmental and selective forces; for example, structural adaptations in aquatic species show how selection shapes form and function. Mendel’s experiments with pea plants revealed particulate inheritance and showed that traits are transmitted discretely rather than blended. Organisms carry two alleles per gene, and allele combinations determine genotype and phenotype. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, though dominance does not imply higher fitness. Predictive tools such as Punnett squares yield consistent genotype (1:2:1) and phenotype (3:1) ratios in heterozygote crosses.  

The discovery that genes reside on chromosomes explained Mendelian patterns. Mitosis produces identical somatic cells, while meiosis forms gametes with half the chromosome number, preserving stability across generations and generating diversity through independent assortment and crossing over. Gene linkage and recombination further create new allele combinations.  

DNA’s double-helix structure, composed of nucleotide base pairs, encodes genetic instructions and replicates faithfully. Mutations introduce new variation—the raw material for evolution. Mitochondrial DNA, inherited maternally and lacking recombination, assists in tracing ancestry. Applications of DNA analysis include estimating evolutionary divergence times, reconstructing phylogenetic relationships, identifying common ancestors, and supporting forensic and ancestry testing. Molecular clocks, based on neutral mutation rates, are valuable though imperfect tools for estimating divergence timing. Together, these principles show that evolution results from genetic change in populations, with natural selection acting on heritable, molecularly based variation.
