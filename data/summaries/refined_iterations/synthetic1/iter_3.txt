This lecture explains how natural selection, genetics, and molecular inheritance together account for evolutionary change. Evolution is defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over generations, measurable only at the population level. Natural selection acts on individual variation within populations competing for limited resources. Because heritable traits affect survival and reproduction differently, these differences alter allele frequencies over time. Darwin’s unresolved questions about variation and inheritance were later answered by genetics, showing that traits are passed discretely rather than blended. Artificial selection in domesticated animals and plants demonstrates how selective pressures can rapidly shift trait frequencies, providing observable evidence for the power of selection.  

Evolution can proceed slowly or rapidly depending on environmental and selective pressures, as seen in structural adaptations among aquatic organisms. Mendel’s experiments with pea plants revealed the consistent, particulate rules of inheritance—each organism carries two alleles per gene, which combine to form genotypes producing observable phenotypes. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, though dominance does not necessarily imply greater fitness. Predictive tools such as Punnett squares yield expected genotype (1:2:1) and phenotype (3:1) ratios when heterozygotes are crossed.  

Genes are located on chromosomes composed of DNA and associated proteins. Humans have 46 chromosomes arranged in homologous pairs, and Mendelian segregation follows from their behavior during meiosis, when chromosomes and genes separate into gametes. Meiosis also generates variation through independent assortment and crossing over, while recombination and linkage further shape new allele combinations.  

DNA’s double-helix structure, with complementary base pairing, ensures faithful replication and storage of genetic information. Mutations introduce new variation, forming the molecular basis for evolutionary change. Molecular genetics thus reinforces classical inheritance by revealing DNA as the material mechanism governing heritable traits. Mitochondrial DNA, inherited only from the mother and lacking recombination, is particularly valuable for tracing maternal ancestry. DNA analysis supports practical and evolutionary uses—such as reconstructing phylogenetic relationships, estimating divergence times with molecular clocks, identifying common ancestors, and applying genetic tools in forensics and ancestry testing.  

Overall, the lecture integrates natural selection, Mendelian inheritance, and molecular biology to show that evolution results from genetic change within populations, driven by selection acting on heritable molecular variation.
