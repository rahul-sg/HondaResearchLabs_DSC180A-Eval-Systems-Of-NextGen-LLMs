Within-subjects designs offer an efficient alternative to between-groups designs by comparing each participant’s performance across multiple conditions rather than comparing separate groups. In between-groups designs, individual differences add nuisance variability (“noise”) that can obscure true effects, requiring larger samples to reach adequate statistical power—for example, 40 participants to obtain 20 data points per condition. In within-subjects designs, each participant provides data in every condition, comparison is made within the same person, and much of this participant-related noise is removed. With 20 participants tested under all conditions, researchers can still achieve 20 usable data points per condition, producing a more powerful and efficient test.

Three main forms of within-subjects designs were emphasized. **Pretest–posttest designs** measure participants before and after an intervention. **Repeated-measures designs** include two or more manipulated conditions within a session. **Longitudinal designs** involve repeated measurements across long time spans, yielding highly informative data but requiring substantial time investment.

While these designs reduce noise, they also introduce unique challenges. Participants who experience all phases of a study may discern its purpose (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects**, where one condition influences later performance—either improving it (**practice effects**) or worsening it (**fatigue effects**). In addition, other threats may co-occur but are conceptually distinct from order: **history effects** (external events during the study), **maturation effects** (developmental or biological change), and **testing effects** (performance shifts from repeated exposure to the same test). Each can confound interpretation if not properly controlled.

**Order effects** highlight why condition sequence matters—performance may appear better simply because a condition was run first or second. To manage this, researchers use **counterbalancing**, varying the order of conditions across participants. **Complete counterbalancing** includes every possible order (e.g., ABBA for two conditions or all six orders for three conditions), but the number of orders increases factorially (n!), which becomes impractical with more conditions. **Incomplete counterbalancing** methods, such as random rotation or **Latin-square designs**, ensure balanced exposure while requiring fewer participants. In cognitive science, researchers sometimes randomly intermix trials instead of counterbalancing when many short items are available, though this is avoided when tasks are lengthy or confusing. Some manipulations cannot be counterbalanced at all—participants cannot “un-take” a drug, “unlearn” a skill, or repeat a one-time experience.
