Within-subjects designs compare each participant’s performance across multiple conditions rather than comparing separate groups. In between-groups designs, each participant contributes data in only one condition, and individual differences introduce nuisance variability (“noise”) that can obscure real effects and require larger samples (e.g., 40 participants to yield 20 data points per condition). Within-subjects designs instead measure each participant in all conditions, comparing each person to themselves and minimizing participant-related noise—so 20 participants can yield 20 data points per condition. This efficiency makes within-subjects designs powerful and sample‑efficient.

Several common forms were outlined. A **pretest–posttest design** measures each participant before and after an intervention. A **repeated‑measures design** tests participants under two or more conditions, such as varying stimulus difficulty. A **longitudinal design** follows participants over time, providing rich information but taking much longer to execute. Illustrative studies included one examining whether children understand speech better in their own accent and a dyslexia experiment comparing reading with regular versus widely spaced text, prompting students to consider whether these were within‑ or between‑subjects designs and whether they used **complete** or **incomplete** condition orders.

Despite their advantages, within‑subjects designs introduce challenges. When participants experience every condition, **demand characteristics** can make them guess the purpose of the study, and **carryover effects** may distort results—performance in later conditions may improve with practice or worsen from fatigue. Other time‑related threats include **history effects** (external events influencing results), **maturation effects** (natural change over time), and **testing effects** (familiarity from repeated testing). Including a pretest–posttest **control group** can help separate these influences from the intervention itself.

**Order effects** occur when condition order alone drives differences. To manage them, researchers can either switch to a **between‑subjects design** or apply **counterbalancing**, which varies the order of conditions. **Complete counterbalancing** uses all possible orders (e.g., ABBA for two conditions or all six for three), while **incomplete counterbalancing**—through **rotation** or a **Latin‑square design**—balances positions with fewer participants. In cognitive studies with many short trials, random intermixing can also cancel order effects. Some manipulations, however, cannot be reversed or counterbalanced (e.g., drug intake or learning a new skill).
