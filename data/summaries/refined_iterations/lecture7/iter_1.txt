Within-subjects designs offer a methodological alternative to between-groups approaches by comparing each participant to themselves across conditions rather than comparing one group of participants to another. In between-groups designs, individual differences between participants introduce nuisance variability (“noise”) that can obscure true effects. Because each participant is measured in only one condition, relatively large samples are needed—for example, 40 people to obtain 20 data points per condition. In contrast, within-subjects designs collect data from the same individuals under all conditions, reducing participant-related variability and requiring fewer total participants (e.g., just 20 people to yield 20 data points per condition). This approach produces more powerful statistical tests with greater efficiency.

Several specific forms of within-subjects designs were introduced. Pretest–posttest designs expose each participant to both a pretest and posttest surrounding an intervention. Repeated-measures designs involve two or more systematically varied conditions within a session, while longitudinal designs measure the same individuals at multiple time points over months or years. Each type takes advantage of comparing participants to themselves to detect change or differences more sensitively.

Despite these benefits, within-subjects designs face certain challenges. Participants may guess a study’s purpose (demand characteristics) or experience carryover effects, where performance in one condition influences later ones. Carryover effects include fatigue, practice, history, maturation, and testing effects. Each can distort results if the order of conditions coincides with these changes. Researchers combat order effects using counterbalancing—varying or randomizing condition order across participants. Complete counterbalancing methods, such as ABBA and block randomization, include all possible condition orders; however, the number of orders grows quickly with added conditions. Incomplete counterbalancing methods like random rotation or Latin-square designs provide practical solutions by ensuring each condition appears equally often in each position and follows every other condition equally often. In cognitive science, conditions are sometimes intermixed randomly within many trials to minimize order bias, unless doing so would confuse participants or overly lengthen the task. Nonetheless, counterbalancing has practical limits: irreversible manipulations, such as drug administration, learning experiences, or surgeries, cannot be undone. Overall, within-subjects designs efficiently reduce noise and increase statistical power but require careful attention to order and carryover control.
