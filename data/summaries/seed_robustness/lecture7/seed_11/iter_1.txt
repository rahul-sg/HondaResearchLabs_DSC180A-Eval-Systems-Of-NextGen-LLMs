This lecture introduces within-subjects designs as an alternative to between-groups designs for controlling participant-related variability. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data in only one condition; differences across individuals add noise and may obscure real effects. Within-subjects designs, by contrast, test every participant under all conditions and compare each person to themselves across those conditions. This structure removes much of the nuisance variability due to individual differences, increases statistical power, and allows researchers to achieve the same number of data points with fewer participants.

Three common types of within-subjects designs illustrate different applications. A **pretest–posttest design** measures each participant before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more conditions within a single experiment, such as testing performance at 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% coherence in a random-dot motion task. A **longitudinal design** assesses the same individuals across multiple time points, revealing change over time but often requiring long data collection periods. For example, the “extra-large letter spacing improves reading in dyslexia” study offers a case to consider whether the design is within- or between-subjects and whether participants experience all conditions or order combinations.

While within-subjects designs are efficient and powerful, they also pose validity challenges. **Demand characteristics** can arise when participants receive all conditions and infer the experimental purpose. **Carryover effects** occur if performance in one condition influences another—through fatigue, practice, or learning. Additional threats include **history effects** (external events affecting outcomes between measurements), **maturation effects** (changes due to aging or development rather than the manipulation), and **testing effects** (performance changes simply from prior test exposure).

A major concern is **order effects**, where the sequence of tasks alters results. Researchers can mitigate them through **counterbalancing**, systematically varying order across participants. In **complete counterbalancing**, participants experience all possible condition orders—using methods like ABBA sequences or block randomization. When this becomes impractical, **incomplete counterbalancing** methods such as random order with rotation or a **Latin-square design** ensure each condition appears in every position and follows each other condition equally often.

In cognitive science studies with many short trials, conditions may be randomly intermixed to cancel order effects unless such mixing confuses participants. However, counterbalancing cannot undo irreversible manipulations—like drug administration, surgery, or one-time experiences. Overall, within-subjects designs provide efficient, low-variability tests of effects when researchers carefully manage order and carryover influences (398 words).
