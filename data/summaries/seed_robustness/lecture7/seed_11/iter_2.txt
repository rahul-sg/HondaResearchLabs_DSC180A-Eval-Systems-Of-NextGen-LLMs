This lecture introduces within-subjects designs as a counterpart to between-groups designs for managing participant-related variability. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so natural differences among individuals add noise and can obscure effects. By contrast, a within-subjects design tests each participant in **all** conditions and compares them to themselves across those conditions. This approach removes at least some of the nuisance variability due to individual differences, enhances statistical power, and yields the same number of data points with fewer participants.

Several specific types illustrate how within-subjects designs are used. A **pretest–posttest design** measures each participant before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more experimental conditions within the same study (e.g., varying coherence levels in a random-dot motion task). A **longitudinal design** tracks the same individuals across multiple time points, providing valuable developmental or change data but often requiring lengthy data collection. The dyslexia study on **extra-large letter spacing** demonstrates how researchers must decide whether participants experience all conditions and orders, determining if a design is complete, incomplete, within-, or between-subjects.

The efficiency of within-subjects designs comes with potential validity threats. **Demand characteristics** may emerge as participants discern the study’s purpose from repeated exposure. **Carryover effects** can alter performance across conditions due to fatigue, practice, or learning. Additional concerns include **history effects** (outside events influencing outcomes), **maturation effects** (natural developmental or biological change), and **testing effects** (performance altered by prior test exposure).

A common challenge is **order effects**, where the sequence of tasks influences results. These are managed through **counterbalancing**, which systematically varies the order of conditions. In **complete counterbalancing**, participants experience all possible condition orders—using techniques such as ABBA sequences or block randomization. When full counterbalancing is impractical, **incomplete methods** like random order with rotation or a **Latin-square design** ensure that each condition appears in every position and follows each other condition equally often. In cognitive science studies with many short, noisy trials, researchers may instead **intermix** conditions randomly, which cancels order effects unless such mixing would confuse participants. Counterbalancing, however, has limits: irreversible manipulations (e.g., taking a drug, undergoing surgery, or learning a memory technique) cannot be undone.
