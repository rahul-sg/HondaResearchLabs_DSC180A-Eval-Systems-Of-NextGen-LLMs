Within-subjects designs provide an efficient way to reduce nuisance variability by testing the same participants in all conditions. In a between-groups design, each person contributes data to only one condition, and individual differences introduce variability that can obscure true effects. In contrast, within-subjects designs compare each participant to themselves across conditions, removing participant-related noise and increasing statistical power. Because each person serves as their own control, researchers can obtain the same number of data points with fewer participants.

Several common forms were discussed. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more conditions, such as varying a perceptual difficulty level. A **longitudinal design** follows the same individuals over time, offering valuable insight but requiring patience and extended data collection. These approaches enhance precision and efficiency rather than necessarily producing “stronger” causal inferences—they must still address validity threats.

Examples illustrated the design logic. In the dyslexia **letter-spacing study**, children read texts with normal versus wide spacing. Researchers had to decide whether each child would see both conditions (a within-subjects approach) or whether different groups would see different versions (between-subjects). The study also raised questions about **complete** versus **incomplete** within-subjects structures—in which everyone gets all conditions but not all possible orders.

Potential drawbacks include **demand characteristics**, where participants may guess the purpose after multiple conditions, and **carryover effects**, where one condition influences later ones through **fatigue** or **practice**. Time-based threats include **history effects** (outside events during the study), **maturation effects** (natural developmental change), and **testing effects** (being influenced by having taken the test before). Including a control pretest–posttest group can help isolate these influences.

**Order effects**—changes in performance due solely to sequence—pose a central challenge. Researchers can counter them by running a between-subjects design or by **counterbalancing** the order of conditions. In **complete counterbalancing**, every possible order is used: the **ABBA** design controls gradual practice effects, and **block randomization** organizes multiple conditions in all possible sequences. When that is impractical, **incomplete designs** such as **random rotation** or a **Latin-square design** ensure each condition appears in each position equally often. In cognitive tasks with many short trials, intermixing condition types can cancel order effects, but counterbalancing is favored when tasks are long or confusing to alternate. Some manipulations—like taking a drug or unlearning a skill—cannot be counterbalanced at all.
