This lecture introduced within-subjects designs as a methodological alternative to between-groups designs. In a between-groups design, each participant experiences only one condition, so individual differences contribute additional noise that can obscure true effects. In contrast, a within-subjects design tests the same participant under every condition, allowing comparisons of each person to themselves. This structure removes much participant-related variability, increases statistical power, and requires fewer participants to reach the same precision of measurement.

Several forms of within-subjects designs were described. A **pretest–posttest design** measures each participant before and after an intervention, typically to assess change following treatment or instruction. A **repeated-measures design** exposes each participant to two or more stimulus conditions—such as differing levels of difficulty or stimulus clarity—and compares performance across them. A **longitudinal design** extends measurement across multiple time points to capture developmental or long-term change, though it can take considerable time to complete.

Within-subjects designs are efficient and powerful but bring particular risks. **Demand characteristics** can arise when participants, seeing all conditions, infer the experiment’s purpose. **Carryover effects** occur if one condition influences behavior in later ones, such as through fatigue or practice. Separate, time-related confounds include the **history effect** (external events occurring during the study), **maturation effect** (changes due to natural development or aging), and **testing effect** (improvement due to prior exposure to a test).

Slides also illustrated these principles with an applied example: research on how children with dyslexia read text that is spaced versus regular. Because each child could read both text formats, researchers could compare each participant’s performance across conditions—an example of a within-subjects setup.

**Order effects** arise when performance depends on the sequence of conditions; for instance, doing Condition A first might produce different results than doing B first. To control this, experiments often use **counterbalancing**, which systematically varies or randomizes condition order. Complete counterbalancing includes methods such as **ABBA designs** and **block randomization** (presenting all possible orders of conditions). When the number of conditions or participants makes this impractical, **incomplete counterbalancing**—like random order with rotation or **Latin-square designs**—ensures each condition appears equally often in each position.

In cognitive science studies with many short trials, researchers may instead **intermix** conditions randomly to cancel order effects. However, counterbalancing cannot undo irreversible manipulations such as surgeries, training, or drug administration. Overall, within-subjects designs provide a powerful, efficient way to detect effects while requiring careful management of time- and order-related confounds.
