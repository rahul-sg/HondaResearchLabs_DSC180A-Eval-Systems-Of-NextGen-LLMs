Within-subjects designs were introduced as an alternative to between-groups designs. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data in only one condition, so individual differences add variability (“noise”) that can obscure real effects. Within-subjects designs instead have each participant experience all conditions, allowing direct comparison of each person to themselves. This approach reduces participant-related variability, increases statistical power, and yields the same number of observations with fewer participants.

Several forms of within-subjects designs were discussed. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** exposes participants to multiple conditions (e.g., varying motion coherence levels). A **longitudinal design** measures the same individuals across extended time points—highly informative but time-intensive. All share the advantages of efficiency and sensitivity through reduced nuisance variability.

The lecture emphasized potential drawbacks that accompany these advantages. **Demand characteristics** can emerge when participants exposed to all conditions guess the study’s purpose. **Carryover effects**, in which earlier conditions influence later ones, pose additional threats: **fatigue effects** (worse performance from tiredness or boredom) and **practice effects** (improvement due to learning the task). Other confounding influences include:  
- **History effects**—external events (e.g., exams, campus celebrations, world events) that coincide with the study and confound results.  
- **Maturation effects**—changes due to natural development or aging (e.g., vocabulary gains, hearing decline) rather than the manipulation.  
- **Testing effects**—participants improving or adjusting because they have taken the same test before.

**Order effects** occur when performance differences depend on the sequence of conditions rather than the manipulation itself. To address these, researchers use **counterbalancing**, distributing condition orders across participants. Complete counterbalancing methods include the **ABBA design** (for two conditions) and **block randomization** (for three or more). **Incomplete counterbalancing**—such as **random order with rotation** or a **Latin-square design**—achieves balanced exposure with fewer participants. The lecture also illustrated a developmental example comparing **4‑year‑olds and 7‑year‑olds** understanding speech in their own versus other accents, highlighting how design choice affects interpretation.

In cognitive science tasks with many trials, researchers often **randomly intermix conditions** to minimize order effects, though counterbalancing is preferable when intermixing would confuse participants or items are lengthy. Finally, some interventions (e.g., surgery, drug administration, or once-seen stimuli) cannot be reversed, defining the **limits of counterbalancing**. Overall, within-subjects designs offer a statistically powerful, efficient framework when researchers carefully manage order, carryover, and time-related confounds.
