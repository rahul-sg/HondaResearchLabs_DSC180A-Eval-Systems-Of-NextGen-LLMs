This lecture focused on **within-subjects designs**, where each participant experiences all experimental conditions and is compared to themselves across those conditions. This contrasts with **between-groups designs**, in which different participants are tested in separate conditions. Because within-subjects designs remove much of the participant-related “noise” that arises from individual differences, they produce more powerful tests and require fewer participants to obtain the same number of data points per condition.

The lecture outlined several common forms. A **pretest–posttest design** measures each participant before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** involves two or more task levels (for example, motion coherence set at 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40%). A **longitudinal design** follows participants across extended periods, capturing developmental or time-based change but demanding more time to complete.

Although efficient, these designs can introduce specific confounds. **Demand characteristics** arise when participants recognize the manipulation after receiving all conditions. **Carryover effects** occur when earlier conditions influence later ones through fatigue or practice. Time-related problems include the **history effect**, when outside events—such as midterms, major games, or the Sun God Festival—affect participant performance; the **maturation effect**, in which natural growth or aging (like children’s vocabulary gains or adult hearing loss) changes outcomes; and the **testing effect**, when prior exposure to a measure alters later performance or expectations.

**Order effects** are a central challenge: performance may differ simply because one condition came first. The lecture highlighted **counterbalancing** as a solution. In **complete within-subjects designs**, every participant experiences all possible orders of conditions. Examples include the **ABBA design**, which counters gradual practice effects, and **block randomization**, which uses all permutations of conditions. When testing all orders is impractical, **incomplete designs**—such as **random order with rotation** or the **Latin-square design**—ensure that each condition appears equally often in each position.

In cognitive science research, where tasks often involve many rapid trials, conditions may instead be randomly intermixed to cancel order effects. However, some manipulations—like administering a drug, performing surgery, or teaching a permanent skill—cannot be reversed, making counterbalancing impossible. Overall, within-subjects designs are an efficient way to control participant variability but require careful handling of order, time, and carryover effects.
