This lecture introduced **within-subjects designs** as an alternative to between-groups designs. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data in only one condition, so individual differences among participants add noise that can obscure experimental effects. Within-subjects designs instead have each participant experience all conditions, allowing each person’s scores to be compared to their own performance across conditions. This reduces participant-related variability, increases power, and requires fewer participants to achieve the same number of data points per condition.

Several forms of within-subjects designs were highlighted. A **pretest–posttest design** involves two measurements per participant—before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** exposes participants to multiple task levels or stimuli (for example, varying motion coherence levels such as 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%). A **longitudinal design** measures change over extended time spans; although informative, it can take much longer to complete.

These designs offer practical and statistical benefits but can introduce confounds. **Demand characteristics** may arise if participants deduce the study’s purpose from experiencing every condition. **Carryover effects** occur when one condition influences later ones through fatigue, practice, or learning. Time-related issues include the **history effect** (outside events affecting results), **maturation effect** (changes due to normal development or aging), and **testing effect** (participants performing differently on a retest because of prior exposure or familiarity).

**Order effects** occur when performance depends on the sequence of conditions. To manage these, researchers use **counterbalancing**, which systematically varies order. In **complete within-subjects designs**, every subject receives all possible condition orders. Examples include the **ABBA design**, where the sequence repeats (A–B–B–A) to offset gradual practice effects, and **block randomization**, which presents all possible permutations of the conditions (e.g., for three conditions, six orders: ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA). When all orders cannot be tested, **incomplete designs** such as **random order with rotation** or the **Latin-square design** ensure each condition appears equally often in each position.

In cognitive science, researchers sometimes **intermix** trial types randomly, which can cancel order effects when many short trials are used. However, certain manipulations—like surgery, drug administration, or learned techniques—cannot be undone, limiting counterbalancing options. Overall, within-subjects designs provide an efficient, powerful approach that demands attention to order and time-dependent confounds.
