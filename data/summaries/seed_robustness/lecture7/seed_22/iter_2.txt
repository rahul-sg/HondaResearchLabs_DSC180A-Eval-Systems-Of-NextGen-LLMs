This lecture introduced within-subjects designs as an alternative to between-groups designs for reducing nuisance variability. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data in only one condition, so individual differences in ability or behavior add “noise” that can obscure real effects. Because each participant is tested only once, researchers must recruit large samples to achieve stable estimates. In contrast, within-subjects designs test every participant in all conditions, allowing each person to be compared to themselves. This removes at least some participant-related noise, increases statistical power, and typically requires fewer participants.

Several common forms of within-subjects designs were presented. A pretest–posttest design measures participants before and after an intervention. A repeated-measures design gathers responses across two or more conditions, such as varying stimulus difficulty levels. A longitudinal design collects data at multiple time points to track change over time—highly informative but often slow to complete. These designs efficiently reduce variability, making them powerful tools for detecting effects.

However, the lecture emphasized that within-subjects designs can introduce demand characteristics and carryover effects. Participants who experience all conditions may infer the study’s purpose or perform differently due to prior exposure. Carryover effects include fatigue (worse on later trials), practice (improvement through task familiarity), history (external events disrupting performance), maturation (changes over time such as aging or development), and testing effects (familiarity with tests or items from earlier sessions).

Order effects, where performance depends on condition sequence, pose another concern. A key remedy is counterbalancing—systematically varying condition order across participants. In complete within-subjects designs, every possible order is experienced. Examples include the ABBA sequence, which controls for gradual practice effects but not rapid ones, and block randomization, which randomizes complete sets of conditions—but is only practical when conditions are very short. When full counterbalancing is unrealistic, incomplete designs reduce order bias using methods like random order with rotation or Latin-square arrangements, ensuring each condition appears in each position and follows every other equally often.

In cognitive science, where tasks often involve many quick trials, researchers sometimes intermix conditions randomly, which effectively cancels out order effects unless the task is too complex or lengthy. Nonetheless, counterbalancing has clear boundaries—some manipulations, like taking a drug or learning a skill, cannot be undone. Overall, within-subjects designs provide an efficient, statistically powerful framework when carefully managed to control order and carryover effects.
