This lecture introduced within-subjects designs as an alternative to between-groups designs for reducing nuisance variability in experiments. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data in only one condition, so individual differences in ability or behavior add noise that can obscure real effects. Because each participant is tested only once, researchers must recruit more participants to reach reliable statistical power. In contrast, within-subjects designs have each person experience all conditions, allowing comparisons of each participant to themselves. This approach removes at least some participant-related noise, increases power, and requires fewer participants overall.

Several key forms of within-subjects designs were highlighted. A pretest–posttest design measures participants before and after an intervention. A repeated-measures design gathers responses across multiple conditions, such as varying stimulus difficulty. A longitudinal design collects measurements over time to examine change—very informative but often slow to execute. These approaches are efficient and statistically powerful because they lower variability within and between conditions.

The lecture emphasized that within-subjects designs are not flawless—but sometimes they are simply good experimental design. Potential issues include demand characteristics, where participants who see all conditions might infer the manipulation, and carryover effects, where earlier experiences influence later performance. Types of carryover effects include fatigue (worse on later trials), practice (improvement through familiarity), and broader influences such as history (external events), maturation (changes due to time or aging), and testing effects (improved performance from prior exposure to the same test).

Order effects—when performance depends on the sequence of conditions—are another concern. One major solution is counterbalancing, which varies the order of conditions across participants. In complete within-subjects designs, every possible order is used. Common methods include the ABBA sequence, which balances gradual practice effects, and block randomization, which works well for short conditions. When full counterbalancing is impractical, incomplete approaches like random order with rotation or Latin-square designs ensure each condition appears equally across positions and follows others equally often.

In cognitive science, researchers may instead intermix trials from different conditions, which can cancel out order effects for many short, repeated tasks. However, counterbalancing has limits—certain manipulations, such as taking a drug or learning a skill, cannot be undone. Overall, within-subjects designs are powerful and efficient when their possible carryover and order effects are thoughtfully controlled.
