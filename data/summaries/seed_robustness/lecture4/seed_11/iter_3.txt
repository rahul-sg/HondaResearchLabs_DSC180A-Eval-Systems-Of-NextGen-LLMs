This lecture traced how natural selection and genetics together explain evolutionary change. Evolution is the change in allele frequencies within a population over time and operates under Darwin’s three postulates: populations face a struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and these variations are heritable. Darwin’s uncertainty about how variation was maintained led to later discoveries in genetics. Artificial selection in plants and animals, such as breeding of dogs and pigeons, showed how deliberate selection could cause rapid trait shifts, while evolution in nature can range from quick physiological adaptations, like placenta-like structures in minnows, to slow, stepwise developments such as the evolution of the vertebrate eye across about one million years.

Gregor Mendel’s experiments with pea plants (1856–1863) demonstrated that inheritance follows predictable patterns based on discrete hereditary “particles,” or genes, which preserve variation rather than blending it. Rediscovery of his work around 1900 and advances in cell biology connected inheritance to physical structures inside cells. Walther Flemming described chromatin and mitosis, and Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri independently established that hereditary material is carried on chromosomes—the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Chromosomes occur in homologous pairs: humans have 46 total, including one pair of sex chromosomes. Understanding how they behave during cell division clarified inheritance. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells, whereas meiosis forms haploid gametes, halving chromosome number so that fertilization restores the diploid set. Critically, meiosis introduces genetic diversity through both mutation and recombination.

Mendel’s crosses revealed how allele combinations (genotype) determine observable traits (phenotype) under dominant–recessive relationships. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but those located close together are linked and may segregate non‑independently. Crossing-over during meiosis can reshuffle alleles, and the recombination rate measures how frequently such exchanges occur.

At the molecular level, James Watson, Francis Crick, and Rosalind Franklin’s work in 1953 revealed DNA’s double‑helix structure, a sugar‑phosphate backbone with paired bases (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional sequences of DNA, while mitochondria contain their own DNA (mtDNA), a small, highly conserved genome of 37 genes inherited only from mothers and lacking recombination. Because it mutates slowly, mtDNA serves as a molecular clock used to estimate divergence times, including identifying “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common maternal ancestor of all humans about 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.
