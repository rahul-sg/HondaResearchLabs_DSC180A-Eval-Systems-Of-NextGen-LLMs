This lecture connected natural selection with modern genetics to explain how evolutionary change occurs. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time, following Darwin’s postulates that (1) organisms struggle for existence, (2) there is heritable variation in traits linked to survival and reproduction, and (3) those variations are passed from generation to generation. Darwin recognized limits in his understanding of inheritance—he questioned whether traits might blend and how variation could persist or new forms arise. Artificial selection, such as the selective breeding of dogs or pigeons, demonstrated how strong selection can rapidly shift traits within only a few generations. Evolutionary rates vary widely: minnows evolved placenta-like structures in a short time, while complex organs like the eye developed gradually through about 1,800 small steps over roughly one million years.

Gregor Mendel’s experiments with pea plants (1856–1863) revealed that inheritance involves discrete “particles,” later called genes, that maintain variation across generations. His work was revisited in the early 1900s as cell biology advanced. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri proposed that hereditary material resides in chromosomes—forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Understanding cell division clarified inheritance: mitosis produces identical diploid cells, while meiosis forms haploid gametes and introduces variation through mutation and recombination.

Mendel’s crosses showed how genotype (the alleles possessed) determines phenotype (the observable trait) through dominant and recessive relationships. Recombination and linkage explain how genes are transmitted together or separately: genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but segregation may not be independent when loci lie close to one another. Crossing-over in meiosis can produce new combinations of alleles, and recombination rate describes how often such exchanges occur.

The discovery of DNA’s double-helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x-ray evidence, identified the molecular basis of heredity. DNA consists of paired nucleotide bases (A–T and C–G) along a sugar-phosphate backbone. Genes are functional DNA sequences, and mitochondria contain their own maternally inherited genome (mtDNA), which accumulates changes slowly and without recombination. mtDNA provides a molecular clock that allows estimation of divergence times, such as identifying “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common maternal ancestor of all humans, living about 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.
