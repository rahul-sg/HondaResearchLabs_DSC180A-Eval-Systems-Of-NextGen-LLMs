This lecture traced how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change, linking Darwin’s early ideas to the discovery of DNA. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time, following Darwin’s postulates that (1) organisms struggle for existence, (2) traits vary in ways affecting survival and reproduction, and (3) those variations are heritable. Darwin recognized, however, that he lacked a clear mechanism for how variation arises and is maintained. Artificial selection—human-driven breeding of plants and animals—illustrated that strong selection can produce dramatic change in only a few generations. Rates of evolutionary change can vary widely: some traits, such as minnows’ placenta-like structures, can evolve rapidly, while complex traits like the eye can develop through roughly 1% improvements accumulated over about a million years.

Gregor Mendel’s experiments with pea plants between 1856 and 1863 established the “particulate” nature of inheritance, showing that traits are controlled by discrete units (later termed genes) passed intact from parents to offspring. His work resurfaced in the early 1900s, inspiring studies of cellular structures. Walther Flemming discovered chromatin and mitosis, and Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri independently proposed the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, identifying chromosomes as the vehicles of hereditary material.

Understanding cell division clarified how traits are transmitted. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells, while meiosis produces haploid gametes, introducing variation through mutation and recombination. Mendel’s crosses demonstrated how genotype (allele combination) determines phenotype (trait expression) through dominance and recessiveness. Crossing heterozygotes predicts trait distributions as illustrated by Punnett squares. Recombination and linkage further refine inheritance: genes on different chromosomes assort independently, while tightly linked genes may segregate together. The recombination rate measures the frequency of crossover events that create new gene combinations.

The molecular basis of inheritance was revealed with the discovery of DNA’s double-helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x-ray work. DNA has a sugar-phosphate backbone and complementary nucleotide base pairs (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional DNA sequences, and mitochondria contain their own small, maternally inherited genome (mtDNA). Because mtDNA accumulates changes slowly and does not recombine, it enables evolutionary timekeeping—such as identifying “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common female ancestor of all humans, living roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.
