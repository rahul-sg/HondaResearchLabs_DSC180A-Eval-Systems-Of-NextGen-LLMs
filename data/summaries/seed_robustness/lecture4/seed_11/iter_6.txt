This lecture explained how natural selection and genetics together account for evolutionary change. Evolution is defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time and operates through three postulates: there is a struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and this variation is heritable. Darwin recognized these conditions but faced difficulties explaining how variation persisted if selection removed it or how novel traits arose. Artificial selection in domesticated species such as dogs and pigeons demonstrates that rapid change can occur across just a few generations. In nature, evolutionary rates vary—minnows have evolved placenta‑like structures quickly, while the eye developed gradually through 1,800 incremental steps over about one million years—showing that such changes are empirically observed phenomena.

Gregor Mendel’s pea‑plant experiments (1856–1863) revealed that inheritance relies on discrete “particles” contributed by each parent that preserve, rather than blend, variation. Rediscovery of his work around 1900 spurred research connecting these particles to chromosomes. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri—working independently with grasshopper chromosomes and roundworm embryos—showed that hereditary material is carried on chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans possess 46 chromosomes (23 homologous pairs, including one pair of sex chromosomes), though other species differ in number. Understanding chromosomes clarified how inheritance underlies evolution. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells, while meiosis yields haploid gametes, maintaining chromosome number across generations and introducing variation through mutation and recombination.

Mendel’s crosses revealed that genotype (the allelic composition) determines phenotype (the observable form), with dominant alleles masking recessive ones. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but those close together may be linked. During meiosis, crossing‑over produces new allele combinations—measurable as the recombination rate—helping preserve genetic diversity.

At the molecular level, James Watson and Francis Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images, described DNA’s double‑helix structure in 1953. DNA consists of a sugar‑phosphate backbone with paired bases—A with T and C with G. Genes are functional segments of DNA, and mitochondria contain distinct mtDNA coding for 37 genes inherited maternally without recombination. Because it changes slowly, mtDNA functions as a molecular clock, enabling researchers to estimate divergence times and trace lineages to “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of all humans about 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
