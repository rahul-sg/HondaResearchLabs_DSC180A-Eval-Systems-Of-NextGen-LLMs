This lecture explained how natural selection and genetics together account for evolutionary change. Evolution is defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time and operates under three postulates: there is a struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits related to survival and reproduction, and this variation is heritable. Darwin recognized these conditions but had difficulty explaining how variation was maintained if natural selection eliminated it or where new traits originated. Artificial selection, such as the domestication and breeding of dogs and pigeons, demonstrated that rapid change can occur within a few generations. In nature, some traits evolve quickly—like minnows developing placenta‑like structures—while others, such as the eye, change gradually through many small steps.

Gregor Mendel’s experiments with pea plants (1856–1863) showed that inheritance involves discrete “particles” passed from each parent, which preserve rather than blend variation. His work laid the foundation for understanding heritability. When rediscovered around 1900, it led to studies linking genes with chromosomes. Walther Flemming discovered chromatin and mitosis, while Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri independently proposed the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance: genetic material passed from parent to child is contained in chromosomes. Humans have 46 chromosomes, arranged as 23 homologous pairs, including one pair of sex chromosomes. Understanding chromosomes was key to understanding inheritance, which in turn is key to understanding evolution. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells, whereas meiosis produces haploid gametes, halving chromosome number so fertilization restores the diploid state. Variation can be introduced through mutation and recombination.

Mendel’s crosses revealed that a genotype (the specific alleles) determines a phenotype (the observable trait) and that dominant alleles can mask recessive ones. Genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but genes located near each other can be linked. Crossing‑over in meiosis allows recombination, producing new gene combinations and maintaining genetic variation; its frequency is defined by the recombination rate.

At the molecular level, James Watson and Francis Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, described the double‑helix structure of DNA in 1953. DNA consists of a sugar‑phosphate backbone with paired bases (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional DNA sequences, and mitochondria contain separate mtDNA—37 genes inherited only from mothers without recombination. Because mtDNA is relatively conserved, it serves as a molecular clock, allowing estimates such as “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of all humans about 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
