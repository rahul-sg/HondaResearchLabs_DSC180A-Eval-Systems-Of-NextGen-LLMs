This lecture connected natural selection with the genetic mechanisms that explain inheritance and evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, following Darwin’s postulates that organisms struggle for existence, vary in traits influencing survival and reproduction, and transmit this variation to offspring. However, Darwin himself faced difficulties explaining how variation arises and persists—questions later answered through genetics. Artificial selection in domesticated species such as dogs and pigeons demonstrated how directed breeding can cause rapid changes in a few generations, while other examples, from minnows evolving placenta‑like structures to the gradual development of the eye in roughly 1,800 small steps over about one million years, showed that evolutionary rates can vary widely.  

Mendel’s 19th‑century pea plant experiments revealed that traits are inherited as discrete “particles,” or alleles, passed from each parent without blending, preserving variation. Rediscovery of Mendel’s work in the early 1900s led researchers such as Walther Flemming, Walter Sutton, and Theodor Boveri to link those hereditary units to chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans possess 46 chromosomes (23 homologous pairs: 22 autosomal and one sex pair), although other species differ greatly in number. Karyotypes visually arrange these homologous pairs, helping show how chromosome structure underlies inheritance and thus evolutionary change.  

Cell division occurs through mitosis, which produces identical somatic cells, and meiosis, which forms haploid gametes so that fertilization restores the species’ chromosome number. Variation is introduced during meiosis by mutation, recombination, and independent assortment. Independent assortment—one of Mendel’s principles—refers to random segregation of genes on different chromosomes. When genes are physically near each other on the same chromosome, their segregation may not be independent (linkage). Crossing‑over can break this linkage, creating new gene combinations; the recombination rate measures how often such exchanges occur.  

At the molecular level, Watson and Crick, building on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images, described DNA’s double‑helix structure composed of sugar‑phosphate backbones and paired bases (A‑T, C‑G). Genes are functional DNA sequences, and humans have about 20,000–25,000 of them. Mitochondrial DNA, inherited only from mothers and coding for 37 genes, was the first human DNA sequenced and is used to trace “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common matrilineal ancestor who lived about 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa. Comparisons of DNA at neutral sites serve as a molecular clock to estimate when species diverged.
