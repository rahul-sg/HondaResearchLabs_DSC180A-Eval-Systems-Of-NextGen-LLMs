This lecture explained how natural selection and genetics together account for evolutionary change. Evolution is defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, and it operates according to three postulates: existence involves competition, traits affecting survival and reproduction vary among individuals, and this variation is heritable. Darwin recognized these conditions but could not explain how variation was maintained when natural selection seemed to remove it or how new traits arose. Artificial selection by humans—such as selective breeding of dogs and pigeons—demonstrated that rapid changes can be produced within just a few generations. In nature, rates of change vary: minnows evolved placenta‑like structures rapidly, whereas the vertebrate eye developed through small, incremental 1% steps—about 1,800 in total, taking roughly one million years.

Gregor Mendel’s pea experiments (1856–1863) showed that inheritance follows consistent patterns governed by discrete “particles,” or genes, passed from each parent. These particles preserve variation instead of blending. The rediscovery of Mendel’s work around 1900 and later cell studies linked genes to chromosomes. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri proposed that hereditary material resides in chromosomes—the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans possess 46 chromosomes (23 homologous pairs, including one pair of sex chromosomes). Understanding their behavior in cell division clarified inheritance: mitosis produces identical diploid cells, while meiosis generates haploid gametes, halving chromosome number so fertilization restores the diploid state. Genetic variation arises through mutation and recombination during meiosis.

Mendel’s crosses distinguished genotype (the allele combination) from phenotype (the observable trait) and revealed dominance relationships, where one allele can mask another. Independent assortment applies to genes on different chromosomes, whereas genes located near each other are linked and may segregate together. Crossing‑over in meiosis can create new allele combinations, with the recombination rate measuring how often this occurs.

At the molecular level, the double‑helix structure of DNA, described by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953 and based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, clarified how inheritance operates. DNA consists of a sugar‑phosphate backbone with paired bases (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional DNA sequences, and mitochondria contain their own mtDNA—37 genes inherited solely from mothers without recombination. Because mtDNA is relatively stable, it provides a molecular clock used to estimate divergence times, including identifying “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of humans about 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
