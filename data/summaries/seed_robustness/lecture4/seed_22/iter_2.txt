This lecture traced how natural selection and inheritance mechanisms together explain evolutionary change, progressing from Darwin’s postulates to modern molecular genetics. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time, supported by three postulates: competition for existence, heritable variation, and variation in survival and reproduction. While Darwin identified these conditions, he faced difficulties explaining how variation was inherited, maintained, and how new traits arose—questions that later research in genetics helped clarify rather than fully resolve.  

Artificial selection in domesticated species, such as dogs and pigeons, demonstrated how selective breeding can produce rapid changes over only a few generations, illustrating the power of selection. Empirical examples further highlighted rates of evolutionary change: minnows evolved placenta‑like structures quickly, while a complex eye could evolve through small incremental steps over about a million years.  

Mendel’s nineteenth‑century experiments with pea plants established that traits are controlled by discrete hereditary “particles” (genes) passed intact from each parent. His rediscovered work around 1900 linked heredity to chromosomal behavior. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, while Sutton and Boveri independently proposed the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance—that chromosomes carry the genetic material transmitted across generations. Humans have 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 homologous pairs (22 somatic and one sex‑determining pair), although species differ widely—chimpanzees have 48, mosquitoes 6, and some butterflies over 400.  

Understanding how chromosomes divide clarified inheritance patterns. Mitosis produces identical diploid somatic cells; meiosis forms haploid gametes and introduces variation through mutation and recombination. Mendel’s crosses distinguished genotype (genetic makeup) from phenotype (observable traits) and demonstrated dominant and recessive allele behavior. Linkage and recombination expanded these principles: genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but genes close together may be inherited jointly unless separated by crossing‑over during meiosis, with recombination rates measuring how often exchanges occur.  

The mid‑twentieth‑century discovery of DNA’s double‑helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick, informed by Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images, revealed the molecule’s molecular basis. DNA’s sugar‑phosphate backbone and paired bases (A–T, C–G) encode genetic information. Genes are functional sequences of DNA, and some DNA exists separately as maternally inherited mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). Because mtDNA is short, conserved, and lacks recombination, scientists use it to trace evolutionary timing, including identifying “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all humans about 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.
