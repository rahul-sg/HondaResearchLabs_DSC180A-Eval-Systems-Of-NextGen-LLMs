This lecture connected natural selection with the genetic mechanisms that enable evolutionary change, moving from Darwin’s foundational postulates to modern DNA-based understanding. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Natural selection operates under three conditions: individuals struggle to survive and reproduce, there is heritable variation among them, and some variants confer advantages that allow more successful reproduction. Darwin recognized these principles but was unable to explain how variation was inherited or maintained—questions later resolved by discoveries in genetics.  

Artificial selection in domesticated species such as dogs and pigeons illustrated how targeted breeding can rapidly alter traits within only a few generations, demonstrating the effectiveness of selection. The lecture also compared rates of evolutionary change: minnows evolved placenta‑like structures rapidly, while the complex eye could arise through modeled incremental steps of about one percent change per stage—an example showing how gradual modification over roughly a million years can produce complexity.  

Mendel’s experiments with pea plants showed that traits are governed by discrete hereditary “particles,” now known as genes, passed intact from each parent and preserving variation. When his work resurfaced around 1900, it advanced the link between genes and chromosomes. Walther Flemming observed chromatin and mitosis, and Sutton and Boveri independently established the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance—that chromosomes carry inherited material. Humans have 46 chromosomes in 23 homologous pairs (22 somatic and one sex‑determining pair), though counts differ widely across species.  

Understanding cell division clarified inheritance. Mitosis produces identical diploid body cells; meiosis forms haploid gametes, introducing variation through mutation and recombination. Mendel’s crosses established genotype (genetic makeup) and phenotype (expressed traits) distinctions, as well as dominant and recessive allele relationships. Linkage and recombination extend these ideas: genes on separate chromosomes assort independently, while those close together may co‑inherit unless crossing‑over during meiosis separates them. Recombination frequency measures how often such exchanges occur.  

The mid‑twentieth‑century discovery of the double‑helix DNA structure by James Watson and Francis Crick, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray work, revealed the molecule’s chemical basis. DNA’s sugar‑phosphate backbone and nucleotide pairing (A–T, C–G) encode heredity. Genes are functional DNA sequences, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) appears separately in mitochondria, inherited only from mothers and not recombining. Because mtDNA is short and conserved, researchers use its divergence to estimate evolutionary timescales, including the approximate age—100–200 thousand years ago—of “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all living humans.
