This lecture traced the development of ideas linking heredity and evolution, from Darwin’s postulates of natural selection to Mendel’s particulate inheritance and the discovery of DNA’s molecular structure.  

Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Natural selection operates when organisms struggle for existence, individuals vary in heritable traits affecting survival and reproduction, and those traits are passed to their offspring. Darwin recognized the importance of variation but could not explain how it was maintained or inherited. Artificial selection—humans selectively breeding plants and animals such as dogs and pigeons—demonstrated that substantial change can occur within a few generations. Quantitative examples, such as minnows evolving a placenta‑like structure and the eye developing through about 1,800 small, 1 % optical improvements over roughly one million years, illustrated measurable and incremental evolutionary change.  

Mendel’s pea plant experiments (1856–1863) established the particulate nature of inheritance: each parent contributes discrete “particles,” now known as genes, which do not blend but remain intact across generations. Rediscovery of his work in the early 1900s linked genetics to cell biology. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and later, Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri proposed that chromosomes carry the hereditary material—the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans possess 46 chromosomes—22 pairs of somatic chromosomes and one pair of sex chromosomes—though chromosome counts vary by species.  

Cell division occurs by mitosis, producing identical diploid body cells, and by meiosis, forming haploid gametes. During meiosis, variation arises through mutation, recombination, and independent assortment. Mendel’s distinctions between genotype (genetic composition) and phenotype (observable expression) and the roles of dominant and recessive alleles explain predictable inheritance patterns. Linkage and recombination further show that genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but closely located genes may be inherited together; crossing over during meiosis can create new allele combinations, generating additional variation.  

The discovery of DNA’s double‑helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, revealed the molecular basis of heredity. DNA consists of a sugar‑phosphate backbone and paired bases (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional sequences of DNA, while mitochondrial DNA—shorter, maternally inherited, and not subject to recombination—provides evidence for a common matrilineal ancestor, “Mitochondrial Eve,” who lived about 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
