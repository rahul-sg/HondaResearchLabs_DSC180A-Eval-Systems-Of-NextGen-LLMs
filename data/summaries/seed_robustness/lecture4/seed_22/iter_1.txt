This lecture traced how natural selection and mechanisms of heredity jointly explain evolutionary change, moving from Darwin’s postulates to the molecular understanding of DNA. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time, supported by three conditions: competition for existence, heritable variation, and variation in survival and reproduction. Darwin recognized these principles but struggled to explain how variation was inherited and maintained—questions later resolved by developments in genetics. Artificial selection in plants and animals, such as dogs and pigeons, illustrated how targeted breeding can produce dramatic evolutionary change within just a few generations, highlighting how selection can act rapidly on variation.

Examples of evolutionary rates further demonstrated this principle: minnows evolved placenta-like structures in a short period, while the complex eye could evolve in about 1,800 incremental steps over roughly one million years. Mendel’s 19th-century pea-plant experiments revealed that traits are controlled by discrete “particles,” later called genes, inherited from each parent and not blended together. His rediscovered work around 1900 connected heredity to cellular structures. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and Sutton and Boveri proposed the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, demonstrating that chromosomes carry genetic material passed between generations.

Understanding how chromosomes divide clarified inheritance. Mitosis produces identical diploid somatic cells, whereas meiosis generates haploid gametes, introducing variation through mutation and recombination. Mendel’s crosses distinguished genotype (genetic makeup) from phenotype (observable traits) and showed dominant and recessive allele behavior. Punnett squares and event diagrams illustrated predictable inheritance patterns. Linkage and recombination expanded Mendel’s principles by showing that genes on different chromosomes assort independently, while nearby genes may be inherited together unless separated by crossing-over during meiosis. Recombination rates quantify how often such exchanges occur.

The mid-twentieth century discovery of DNA’s double-helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images, revealed the molecule’s physical basis—information crucial for understanding replication and protein coding. DNA is a double helix with a sugar‑phosphate backbone and paired bases (A–T, C–G). Genes are sections of DNA encoding functions, and some DNA exists separately as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), inherited maternally and lacking recombination. mtDNA’s stability allows estimation of evolutionary timing, including identification of “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all living humans about 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.
