This lecture examined how natural selection and genetic inheritance jointly explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as changes in allele frequencies within populations over time, grounded in Darwin’s postulates: organisms face a struggle for existence, show heritable variation in traits related to survival and reproduction, and differential survival leads to evolutionary change. Darwin could not explain how variation persisted or new traits arose—questions later answered through Mendelian genetics.

Artificial selection in domesticated species, such as dogs and pigeons, demonstrates how selection on heritable variation can cause substantial change over a few generations, revealing the same basic mechanism operating in nature. Evolution can proceed at different rates: some traits evolve rapidly, as in minnows that developed placenta‑like structures, while complex features such as the eye may arise through many small, functional steps accumulated over long timescales.

Mendel’s experiments with pea plants (1856–1863) showed that traits are inherited as discrete particles rather than blending together. These hereditary particles—genes—occur as alternative forms called alleles. Homozygous individuals possess identical alleles, while heterozygotes carry different ones. Genotype refers to the allelic combination, and phenotype to the expressed trait. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, and during meiosis alleles segregate so each gamete carries one copy. Recombination, or crossing‑over, can generate new allele combinations, while independent assortment ensures genes on different chromosomes are inherited separately except when close together (linkage reduces independence).

Cytological research connected Mendel’s findings to physical structures within cells. Walther Flemming identified chromosomes and mitosis, and Sutton and Boveri proposed the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, recognizing chromosomes as carriers of genetic material. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells, while meiosis forms haploid gametes, preserving chromosome number and introducing variation through mutation and recombination.

The molecular basis of inheritance was revealed when Watson and Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, described DNA’s double‑helix structure composed of sugar‑phosphate backbones and paired bases (A with T, C with G). Genes are functional segments of DNA, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)—inherited only from mothers—provides a tool for tracing ancestry. Its stability allows estimation of divergence times using molecular clocks that compare neutral DNA regions. Analyses of mtDNA support the concept of a “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all humans, living roughly 100–200 thousand years ago in East Africa.
