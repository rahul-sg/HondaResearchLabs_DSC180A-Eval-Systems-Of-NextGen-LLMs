This lecture explained how natural selection and genetic inheritance together account for evolutionary change from Darwin’s postulates to modern molecular genetics. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Darwin proposed that organisms compete for existence, vary in traits tied to survival and reproduction, and pass those traits to offspring. While he recognized the importance of inherited variation, he lacked a mechanism for how it was maintained. Artificial selection in species such as dogs and pigeons demonstrated that targeted breeding can cause rapid change, revealing the potential power of selection. Evolution can proceed at different tempos—minnows developed placenta‑like structures quickly, whereas the complex eye evolved incrementally in about 1,800 steps over roughly one million years.

Mendel’s pea plant experiments (1856–1863) established that inheritance is particulate: discrete “particles” from each parent maintain variation across generations. Rediscovery of his work around 1900 spurred investigation into the physical carriers of these particles. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and later Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri demonstrated that genetic material passed from parent to child resides in the chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Understanding cell division clarified that mitosis preserves diploid chromosome pairs in somatic cells, while meiosis produces haploid gametes and introduces variation via recombination and independent assortment.

Mendel also showed that genotype (allelic composition) underlies phenotype (observable expression). Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, and predictable offspring ratios result from allele segregation, modeled by Punnett squares. Linkage and recombination explain how genes on different chromosomes assort independently, while closely positioned loci may remain linked unless crossing‑over generates new combinations. The recombination rate measures how frequently such genetic exchanges occur, contributing to variation within populations.

The molecular basis of heredity became clear with the 1953 description of DNA’s double‑helix structure by Watson and Crick, drawing on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data. DNA consists of a sugar‑phosphate backbone and paired nucleotide bases (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional segments of DNA, and humans possess about 20,000–25,000. Mitochondrial DNA, inherited only maternally and not subject to recombination, helped identify “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all living humans, who lived roughly 100–200 thousand years ago in East Africa.
