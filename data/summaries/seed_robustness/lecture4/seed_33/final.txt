This lecture explained how natural selection and genetic inheritance together shape evolutionary change. Evolution is defined as a change in allele frequencies within populations over time, grounded in Darwin’s postulates that there is a struggle for existence, variation in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and heritable transmission of those traits. Darwin, however, lacked a clear mechanism for how variation was preserved or how new traits appeared—questions later clarified through genetics rather than resolved at the time.

Artificial selection in domesticated animals and plants—such as dogs and pigeons—provides a concrete example of how targeted breeding can produce rapid changes across few generations, illustrating the same principles that natural selection operates on more gradually. The pace of change can vary: some species, like minnows, show rapid evolution (e.g., a placenta‑like structure), while complex features such as eyes can evolve through many small, functional steps (the slide example estimated around 1,800 incremental changes over about one million years).

Mendel’s pea plant experiments (1856–1863) demonstrated that traits are inherited through discrete “particles” rather than blending, maintaining variation within populations. These hereditary units—genes—occur in alternative forms called alleles. An organism’s genotype refers to its allele combination, while phenotype describes the expressed trait. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, and during meiosis alleles segregate so each gamete carries one from each pair. Recombination or crossing‑over creates new allele combinations, while independent assortment ensures genes on different chromosomes inherit separately, except when closely linked loci reduce independence.

Cytological studies connected these hereditary “particles” to cellular structures. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and later Sutton and Boveri established the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, recognizing chromosomes as the carriers of genetic material. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells, whereas meiosis forms haploid gametes, maintaining chromosome numbers and enabling genetic variation through recombination and mutation.

The discovery of DNA revealed the molecular basis of inheritance. Watson and Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray evidence, described DNA’s double‑helix structure of sugar‑phosphate backbones and base pairs (A with T, C with G). Genes are functional DNA sequences, while mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) occurs separately in mitochondria and is passed only through the maternal line without recombination. Because mtDNA evolves slowly and predictably, it helps estimate divergence times using molecular clocks—supporting concepts such as “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all humans, inferred to have lived roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
