This lecture traced how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Darwin’s postulates emphasized that organisms struggle for existence, vary in traits influencing survival and reproduction, and pass this variation to offspring. While Darwin recognized the importance of variation, he could not explain its origin or persistence. Later discoveries in genetics provided those mechanisms. Artificial selection in domesticated species, such as dogs and pigeons, illustrated how targeted breeding can rapidly alter traits over just a few generations, demonstrating evolution in action. Similarly, natural processes can drive both rapid and gradual change—some minnows evolved placenta-like structures relatively quickly, whereas the complex eye likely evolved through about 1,800 small steps over roughly one million years.

Gregor Mendel’s pea plant experiments established the particulate nature of inheritance, in which discrete “particles” (genes) from each parent maintain variation across generations. Rediscovery of Mendel’s work around 1900 refocused attention on heredity’s physical basis. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and later Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri connected chromosomes to Mendelian factors, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Understanding mitosis and meiosis clarified how somatic cells maintain diploid chromosome pairs, while gametes carry haploid sets. Meiosis introduces variation through mutation, recombination, and the independent assortment of chromosomes.

Mendel’s results also distinguished genotype (allelic composition) from phenotype (observable traits) and showed that dominant alleles mask recessive ones. Crossing heterozygotes produces predictable offspring ratios illustrated by Punnett squares. Linkage and recombination further refine inheritance patterns: genes on separate chromosomes assort independently, while those close together can remain linked unless crossing-over generates new allele combinations. Recombination rate measures how often this exchange occurs, explaining much of the genetic diversity seen in populations.

The molecular foundation of inheritance emerged with the discovery of DNA’s double-helix structure by Watson and Crick, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images. DNA’s sugar‑phosphate backbone and complementary base pairing (A–T, C–G) store genetic information, forming genes that code for specific functions. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), inherited solely from mothers, provides a powerful evolutionary tool because it is short, highly conserved, and not recombined. It has helped identify “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all living humans—one lineage among many within a continuously interconnected human population.
