This lecture traced how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change, from Darwin’s postulates to the molecular understanding of DNA. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies over time within a population. Darwin proposed that organisms face a struggle for existence, vary in traits influencing survival and reproduction, and pass these variations to offspring. His main challenge was explaining how variation persists if selection removes it and how new forms arise. Examples of artificial selection, such as human breeding of dogs and pigeons, demonstrated that targeted selection can cause rapid changes across a few generations. Observations of minnows and the gradual evolution of the eye illustrated that evolutionary change can occur both rapidly and incrementally.

Mendel’s pea plant experiments (1856–1863) revealed that inheritance is particulate: “particles” are passed from each parent, preserving variation across generations. When his work resurfaced around 1900, it prompted searches for the physical basis of inheritance. Walther Flemming discovered chromatin and described mitosis, and later Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri showed that hereditary material is carried in chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Understanding cell division clarified that mitosis produces identical diploid cells, while meiosis produces haploid gametes and introduces variation through mutation, recombination, and independent assortment during meiosis.

Mendel distinguished between genotype, the allelic composition of a trait, and phenotype, its observed expression. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, and predictable ratios in offspring result from allele segregation, illustrated by Punnett squares. Linkage and recombination further explain how traits are inherited: genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but those located close together may be inherited together unless crossing‑over during meiosis separates them. The recombination rate quantifies how frequently such exchanges occur, helping maintain genetic diversity.

The molecular foundation of heredity was established when Watson and Crick described the DNA double helix in 1953, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data. DNA has a sugar‑phosphate backbone with complementary base pairs (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional sequences of DNA, and humans possess about 20,000–25,000 of them. Mitochondrial DNA, inherited only from mothers and lacking recombination, provided evidence for “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all living humans, who lived roughly 100–200 thousand years ago in East Africa.
