This lecture examined **nested and subqueries in SQL**, their various forms, logical interpretations, and implications for optimization and query monotonicity. Building on prior topics such as projections, selections, joins, and aggregates, it introduced how queries can contain other queries and how nesting affects both expressiveness and efficiency.

A **subquery** is a query embedded within another and can appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, or **WHERE** clauses. A **nested query** often refers to a subquery correlated with its parent, meaning it depends on outer query values. Although SQL allows such constructs, the lecture’s rule of thumb (Slide 3) stressed: *avoid nested queries when possible—but sometimes it is impossible.* Subqueries can return a single value or an entire relation. When equivalent results can be obtained through simple joins or grouping, **subquery unnesting** (rewriting them as joins) improves clarity and performance.

Examples in **SELECT clause** subqueries included finding each actor’s movie genre, counting how many actors appear per movie, or computing the average salary for movies rated above nine. Paired queries demonstrated that such subqueries are frequently equivalent to join‑based formulations, though outer joins may alter equivalence. In the **FROM clause**, subqueries can act as temporary tables or **common table expressions (CTEs)**, supporting cleaner logic refactoring, as shown in filtering movies by rating ranges.

The **WHERE clause** cases introduced **quantifiers**. Using `EXISTS` or `IN` implements **existential quantifiers** (“there exists”) to check for the presence or membership of related tuples and can generally be replaced with join formulations. In contrast, **universal quantifiers** (“for all”) are trickier because SQL lacks direct syntax. The lecture illustrated how to handle them indirectly—for example, selecting actors who only acted in action movies or movies whose actors all earn above $100 K—by combining `NOT IN`, `NOT EXISTS`, or multi‑step exclusion logic. Certain universal‑form queries (e.g., `$100 K < ALL (...)`) were noted as **not supported in SQLite**, underscoring limitations of real systems.

Finally, the lecture connected these ideas to **query monotonicity**. A **monotone query** (without negation, aggregates, or universal conditions) never loses results when data is added and can therefore be unnested safely. **Non‑monotone queries**, which include negation or universal quantification, cannot be unnested this way. This conceptual link between logic, optimization, and expressiveness concluded the discussion of nested SQL queries.
