This lecture focused on **nested SQL queries (subqueries)**—queries embedded within other queries—and their links to logical quantifiers and query monotonicity. Building on prior SQL topics like projections, selections, joins, aggregates, and grouping, it examined subqueries appearing in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, and **WHERE** clauses.

A **subquery** can return either a single value or a full relation and may reside in different clauses depending on the logic required. The guiding principle was to **avoid nested queries when possible**, though some conditions necessitate them.  

In **SELECT** clauses, examples such as finding each actor’s movie genre or computing the average salary for highly rated movies illustrated **correlated subqueries**, which depend on outer‑query values. These were compared with equivalent **join or aggregation‑based statements** through **subquery unnesting**, showing that many nested forms can be rewritten more efficiently. However, the slides cautioned that unnesting may not always preserve results, particularly when **outer joins** are involved.

For subqueries in the **FROM** clause, the lecture demonstrated how a subquery can define a **derived table** or temporary view. Using the **WITH clause** introduced **common table expressions (CTEs)**, enabling *subquery refactoring*—naming an intermediate result to clarify multi‑step logic. This differs from unnesting, which removes nesting entirely; both were shown to produce equivalent results in certain cases.

In the **WHERE** clause, subqueries represent **logical quantifiers**. **Existential quantifiers** (“there exists”) appear through `EXISTS` or `IN`, testing whether any tuple satisfies a condition—for example, identifying actors who acted in at least one Sci‑Fi film. These mirror join semantics. **Universal quantifiers** (“for all”) require more complex reasoning: the lecture presented a **two‑step process**—(1) select actors who participated in a non‑Action film, and (2) retrieve others not in that set—to find actors who acted **only in Action movies**. The same logic extended to queries such as finding movies where every actor’s salary exceeded $100K, expressible via `<ALL` or equivalently by `NOT EXISTS`.

The session concluded with the notion of **monotone versus non‑monotone queries**. A query is *monotone* if adding tuples to input tables cannot remove outputs. Slides proved that plain SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE queries without subqueries or aggregates are monotone under nested‑loop semantics. The lecture related this property to **unnesting** theoretically: **if a query is monotone, unnesting is possible**, whereas **non‑monotone queries**—those involving **negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates**—cannot be unnested in principle.
