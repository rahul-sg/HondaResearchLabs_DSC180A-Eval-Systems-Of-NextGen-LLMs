This lecture covered **nested SQL queries (subqueries)**—queries embedded within others—and how they relate to logical quantifiers and query transformation. It built on previous SQL features such as projections, selections, joins, aggregates, grouping, and updates, then examined three major contexts for subqueries: in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, and **WHERE** clauses.

A **subquery** may return either a single value or a relation and can appear in different clauses depending on need. The rule of thumb emphasized was to **avoid nested queries when possible**, though sometimes nesting is required to compute intermediate conditions.  

In **SELECT** clauses, slides illustrated **correlated subqueries** that depend on outer query values—for example, retrieving each actor’s movie genre or the average salary per high‑rated movie. These examples were compared with equivalent **join or aggregation formulations** through **subquery unnesting**, revealing that many nested queries can be flattened without changing results. However, Slide 10 questioned true equivalence when **outer joins** are involved, cautioning that structural rewriting may yield different outcomes in those cases.

Subqueries in the **FROM** clause served as **derived tables** or temporary results. Using **CTEs (common table expressions)** clarified multi‑step logic—an approach called *subquery refactoring*—while subquery unnesting again showed that equivalent results can sometimes be expressed by a single‑level query.

In the **WHERE** clause, subqueries expressed **logical quantifiers**. **Existential quantifiers** (“there exists”) appeared through `EXISTS` and `IN`, testing whether at least one tuple satisfies a condition, as when identifying actors who performed in any Sci‑Fi movie. These queries corresponded closely to join operations. **Universal quantifiers** (“for all”) required more complex reasoning: the lecture explicitly presented a **two‑step method**—(1) find actors who appeared in a non‑Action movie, and (2) retrieve those *not in* that set to yield actors who acted **only in Action movies**. The same logical pattern underpinned examples like identifying movies where every actor earned above $100K, implementable via `< ALL` or, equivalently in SQLite, `NOT EXISTS`.

The lecture ended by examining **monotone versus non‑monotone queries**. A query is *monotone* if adding tuples to input tables cannot remove existing results. Plain SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE queries without subqueries or aggregates are monotone under nested‑loop semantics. The slides concluded that **monotone queries can be safely unnested**, whereas non‑monotone queries—those involving **negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates**—cannot.
