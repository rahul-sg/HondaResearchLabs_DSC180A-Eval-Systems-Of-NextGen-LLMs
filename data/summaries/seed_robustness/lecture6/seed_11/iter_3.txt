This lecture examined **nested and subqueries in SQL**, focusing on their forms, uses, optimizations, and logical properties related to **quantifiers** and **monotone behavior**. Building on earlier material—projections, selections, joins, and aggregates—it defined a **subquery** as a query embedded inside another that can appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, or **WHERE** clause. Subqueries are used when a condition depends on data that must first be computed. The slides emphasize a guiding rule: *avoid nested queries when possible,* though some tasks require them.

In the **SELECT clause**, subqueries compute derived values such as a movie’s genre, number of actors, or actors’ average salary. These are often **correlated subqueries** that reference outer query variables. Examples showed how such queries can often be rewritten using joins or aggregations—a process called **subquery unnesting**—to improve efficiency. Slide comparisons highlighted that multiple formulations (nested, grouped, or LEFT OUTER JOIN versions) can yield the same result, though output differences may appear when tuples are missing, especially with outer joins.

**Subqueries in the FROM clause** demonstrate how intermediate results can be defined as temporary tables or **common table expressions (CTEs)**. These **non‑correlated subqueries** can simplify queries into logical steps—an example of subquery refactoring. The lecture also showed that these can be unnested to equivalent single‑query forms.

**Subqueries in the WHERE clause** connected SQL conditions to **logical quantifiers**. **Existential quantifiers**, expressed using `EXISTS` or `IN`, check for the presence of at least one matching record (e.g., actors who appeared in a Sci‑Fi movie). These queries can be converted to joins without changing meaning. **Universal quantifiers** express “for all” conditions, such as actors who acted only in action movies or movies where every actor’s salary exceeds $100K. Implementations used constructs like `$100K < ALL` and `NOT EXISTS`. The slides noted that the `ALL` operator is *not supported in SQLite*, highlighting a practical system limitation. Stepwise reasoning was used to derive universal conditions by excluding counterexamples (e.g., actors not appearing in non‑action films).

The lecture closed by linking **monotonicity** and **unnesting**. A formal theorem stated that SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE queries **without subqueries or aggregates** are **monotone**, meaning adding data cannot remove existing results. **Non‑monotone queries**, which include negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates, cannot be safely unnested—defining a theoretical boundary for SQL query transformation.
