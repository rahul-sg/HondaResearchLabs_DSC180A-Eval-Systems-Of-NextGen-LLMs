This lecture focused on **nested and subqueries in SQL**, exploring their structure, use cases, optimization, and their logical connection to **quantifiers** and **monotone behavior**. Building on prior topics—projections, selections, joins, and aggregates—it defined a **subquery** as a query embedded inside another. Subqueries may appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, or **WHERE** clauses, returning either a single value or an entire relation. They are often necessary when a condition depends on data that must first be computed, though the slides emphasize a rule of thumb: *avoid nested queries when possible*, but recognize that some cases require them.

In the **SELECT clause**, subqueries can compute derived information such as a movie’s genre, actor count, or average salary per movie. These are often **correlated subqueries** that reference outer queries. The lecture illustrated that such queries can frequently be rewritten using joins or aggregations—a process known as **subquery unnesting**—to improve readability and efficiency. However, the slides ask “Are these equivalent?” when comparing nested, grouped, and left‑join forms, signaling that equivalence is not guaranteed under all conditions, not just when NULLs are involved.

**Subqueries in the FROM clause** demonstrated how intermediate results can be defined as virtual tables or **common table expressions (CTEs)**, enabling step‑by‑step query construction. These are examples of **non‑correlated subqueries**, since their results do not depend on outer query variables.

**Subqueries in the WHERE clause** introduced **quantifiers**. **Existential quantifiers**, expressed with `EXISTS` or `IN`, test for the presence of satisfying tuples (e.g., actors appearing in some Sci‑Fi film) and can often be flattened into equivalent join queries. **Universal quantifiers** represent “for all” conditions, which are harder to express directly. Examples included identifying actors who acted only in action movies or movies whose actors all earn above a given salary. These queries are typically written using `NOT IN` or `NOT EXISTS`, often requiring stepwise reasoning.

The lecture concluded with the link between **query monotonicity** and **unnesting**. A formal statement specified: *If Q is a SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE query with no subqueries and no aggregates, then Q is monotone.* Monotone queries never lose results when data are added, whereas **non‑monotone queries**—those involving negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates—cannot be safely unnested. This theoretical boundary defines when nested queries can or cannot be transformed for optimization.
