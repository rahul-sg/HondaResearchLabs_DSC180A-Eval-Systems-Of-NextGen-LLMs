This lecture explored **nested SQL queries**, focusing on their forms, use cases, and when they can be transformed or “unnested” into equivalent flat queries. After reviewing core SQL capabilities such as projections, selections, joins, aggregates, and grouping, the lecture introduced the **subquery**—a query embedded within another—which can appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, or **WHERE** clauses. Subqueries may return either single values or entire relations. The guiding principle is to **avoid nested queries when possible**, since they often can be rewritten using joins or grouping for efficiency—but some conditions inherently require nesting.

For **subqueries in the SELECT clause**, examples used correlated subqueries to find related information, such as each actor’s movie genre, the number of actors per movie, or average salaries for movies with rating > 9. The slides demonstrated how such queries can often be **unnested** into equivalent joins or group-by queries. However, one slide explicitly asked, “But are these equivalent?” to emphasize that equivalence depends on semantics: switching between inner and **LEFT OUTER JOINs** can yield different results, so unnesting must preserve meaning.

In **subqueries within the FROM clause**, the lecture showed how nested results can define intermediate tables through derived tables or **WITH** clauses (common table expressions). This **subquery refactoring** supports modular design and can usually be flattened without changing output.

Subqueries in the **WHERE clause** introduced **logical quantifiers**. Constructs like **EXISTS** and **IN** express **existential quantifiers** (“there exists at least one”), which correspond closely to joins that test the presence of related tuples and are generally straightforward. Slide 17 contrasted these with **universal quantifiers** (“for all”), which **pose a challenge**, since standard SQL cannot directly express them. To represent universals, the lecture used negation-based rewrites—typically with **NOT IN** or **NOT EXISTS**—to find actors who acted only in action movies, those with at most two action roles, or movies whose actors all earn more than $100K. These examples highlighted that expressing universality often requires multi-step logic and can quickly become complex.

The lecture concluded by linking query structure to **monotonicity**. A **monotone query** (one whose results never decrease as data is added) can generally be unnested safely. In contrast, **non‑monotone queries**—those involving negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates—cannot be unnested without altering semantics. Recognizing which queries are monotone clarifies both the possibilities and limitations of subquery unnesting.
