This lecture examined nested queries and subqueries in SQL, emphasizing how they are formed, used, and optimized, and introduced the concepts of existential and universal quantifiers along with monotone query behavior. Building on prior SQL foundations—projections, selections, joins, and aggregates—the session defined a **subquery** as a query embedded within another, often necessary when a condition depends on a value or table that must itself be computed. Subqueries can appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, or **WHERE** clauses, returning either single values or entire relations. A key principle was stated early: **avoid nested queries when possible**, since they tend to be less efficient and more complex, but recognize that some situations make them unavoidable.

Examples of **subqueries in the SELECT clause** showed how to compute derived attributes such as a movie’s genre, actor counts, or average salaries. The lecture demonstrated that many nested forms can be rewritten as equivalent join or aggregation queries—an optimization known as **subquery unnesting**—but highlighted a subtle distinction: a left outer join is not always strictly equivalent to the nested version when NULL-matching cases are present.  

**Subqueries in the FROM clause** were presented as a way to define intermediate results or “temporary tables,” including through **common table expressions (CTEs)**. This supports modular query design or multi-step filtering while maintaining readability and correctness.  

**Subqueries in the WHERE clause** introduced **quantifiers**. Using `EXISTS` or `IN`, SQL can express **existential conditions** (“there exists”), determining whether related tuples meet certain criteria—for example, finding actors who have appeared in some Sci‑Fi film. These constructs can also be flattened into equivalent join queries. **Universal quantifiers** (“for all”) are more challenging, since SQL lacks direct syntax for them. Examples included finding actors who acted only in action movies and movies whose actors all earn above a salary threshold. Such logic can be implemented with `NOT IN` or `NOT EXISTS`, often requiring multi-step reasoning.

The lecture concluded with **monotone and non‑monotone query behavior**. A **monotone query** never loses results when more data are added—typical of straightforward `SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE` statements without aggregates or negation—and can thus be safely unnested. Queries involving **negation**, **universal quantifiers**, or **aggregates** are **non‑monotone**, meaning unnesting is generally **not possible**. This connection between monotonicity and unnesting forms the theoretical boundary for SQL query optimization.
