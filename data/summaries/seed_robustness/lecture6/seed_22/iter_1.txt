This lecture examined nested SQL queries—subqueries appearing inside other queries—and the theoretical notion of monotonicity that determines when such queries can be simplified. Building on prior SQL topics such as projections, joins, and groups, the lecture first defined subqueries as queries that may occur in the SELECT, FROM, or WHERE clauses. They can return single values, full relations, or sets of values to be compared in conditions. A key rule of thumb emphasized early on was: **avoid nested queries whenever possible**, since they can often be replaced by equivalent join‑based queries; yet for some logical conditions, nesting is unavoidable.

Subqueries in the **SELECT clause** were demonstrated through examples computing attributes such as a movie’s genre, an actor’s count of roles, and average salaries. These queries illustrated *correlated subqueries*, where the inner query references attributes from the outer query, and *subquery unnesting*, where equivalent results can be obtained using joins and group‑by operations. The lecture showed that unnesting can affect results depending on join type (inner versus outer) and filtering conditions.

Subqueries in the **FROM clause** acted as derived tables or temporary results. Examples used inline views and common table expressions (CTEs) to first restrict movies by rating and then apply further filters, highlighting “subquery refactoring” for improved readability without changing semantics.  

Subqueries in the **WHERE clause** illustrated logical quantifiers. *Existential quantifiers* (“there exists”) were expressed through EXISTS or IN, testing whether a related tuple satisfies some property—for example, finding actors who appeared in at least one Sci‑Fi movie. Such expressions can usually be flattened into joins. By contrast, *universal quantifiers* (“for all”) were simulated using NOT IN or NOT EXISTS. Examples included selecting actors who acted only in action movies or movies whose actors all earn above $100K. These queries required multi‑step or negated conditions, showing that expressing universality in SQL is more difficult.

The lecture concluded with **monotone versus non‑monotone queries**. A query is monotone if adding tuples to the input tables cannot remove results. Monotone queries, such as simple SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE forms without aggregates or negation, can in theory be unnested. Non‑monotone queries—those involving negation, aggregation, or universal quantifiers—cannot. This theoretical relationship clarifies why some nested SQL constructions resist simplification.
