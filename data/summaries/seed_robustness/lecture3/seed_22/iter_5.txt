This lecture deepened discussion of Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **Coloniality of Power**, tracing how colonial domination reshaped global systems of race, labor, and knowledge that still organize life in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist whose work spans dependency theory, political economy, and decolonial epistemology—showed that the colonial order did not end with independence. Instead, its logic endured as a worldwide structure of racialized capitalism and Eurocentric thought. Within the **Modernity / Coloniality / Decoloniality (MCD)** framework, the lecture emphasized that modernity and coloniality were **co‑constituted**: Western “progress” and white supremacy grew together as parts of the same historical process, not as separate or parallel modernities.  

**Point 1 – Coloniality of Power.** The conquest of the Americas created two intertwined formations: (1) new racial categories that separated conquerors and conquered, and (2) systems of labor control—slavery, encomienda, and mita —that extracted wealth while tying race to economic role. These relations produced a **new model of global power** combining economic exploitation with epistemic domination. Racial difference became a mental category of modernity, naturalizing whiteness as a universal standard of civilization and humanity.  

**Point 2 – Eurocentrism.** European dominance in economy and knowledge was not inevitable but historically forged through racialized exploitation. Early colonizers linked unpaid labor to “inferior” races, embedding hierarchy within capitalism itself. **Eurocentrism** functions as both worldview and method of knowledge‑making that privileges Europe as the universal reference point while casting others as “pre‑capital,” “developing,” or “Third World.” This perspective formed the ideological foundation for linear narratives of progress. Connections to Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest” highlighted how cultural images and discourses reproduce those assumptions—illustrated in analyses of films like *Roma*, which expose contemporary layers of colonial power.  

**Point 3 – Eurocentrism and Historical Experience in Latin America.** After independence, elites constructed national imaginaries that romanticized a distant Indigenous past yet excluded living Indigenous peoples. Rebecca Earle termed this “**Indianesque nationalism**”: symbolic pride used by non‑Indigenous creoles to claim deep roots while maintaining colonial hierarchies. Subsequent **mestizaje** ideologies reframed mixture as unity but continued to center whiteness, revealing continuity between colonial and national projects of identity.  

The lecture concluded by contrasting Europe’s linear vision of history with Aymara ideas of *pachakuti*, underscoring diverse, cyclical understandings of change. Quijano’s **call for decoloniality** urged freeing ourselves from the **Eurocentric mirror** that distorts self‑understanding, affirming plural, non‑Western ways of knowing and being.
