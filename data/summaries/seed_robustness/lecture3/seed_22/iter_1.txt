This lecture traces how Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **“coloniality of power”** explains enduring structures of domination that link race, labor, knowledge, and identity in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist who moved from dependency theory to decolonial thought—argues that colonialism produced more than political rule: it established a lasting matrix of power that organizes global modernity itself. His work is situated within the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project**, which contrasts three intertwined dimensions.  
- **Modernity**: a Western narrative portraying progress as uniquely European, challenged here through “counter‑histories” that reveal modernity’s roots in conquest.  
- **Coloniality**: the continuing hierarchies of knowledge and power that privilege European worldviews while silencing Indigenous and other epistemologies.  
- **Decoloniality**: efforts to center non‑Western languages, cosmologies, and modes of being as legitimate sources of knowledge.

The first portion of the lecture examines how the **coloniality of power** emerged through two historical processes: (1) the creation of racial distinctions separating “West” and “Rest,” and (2) the formation of new systems of labor control tied to global capitalism. In the Americas, slavery, encomienda, and mita labor organized production for European markets, embedding racial hierarchies into economic and political life. Quijano shows that these relations became naturalized through **Eurocentrism**, where Western superiority appeared as historical “common sense.” This Eurocentric lens shaped who held authority and what counted as progress.

Building on this analysis, the lecture connects Quijano’s ideas to cultural examples. The film *Roma* provides an illustration of how colonial hierarchies of class, race, and gender persist within contemporary representations. The discussion then transitions to national identity formation and the ideology of **mestizaje**. Historian Rebecca Earle’s notion of **“Indianesque nationalism”** describes how early independence leaders glorified a romanticized Indigenous past to mark distance from Spain while excluding actual Indigenous peoples from the new nations. Later, mestizaje celebrated racial mixing as a symbol of unity but continued to privilege whiteness and marginalize Afro‑descendant and Indigenous identities. Paintings such as *The Redemption of Ham* highlight these racialized ideals of family and progress.

The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality**: to free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts identity and to recognize the heterogeneity of histories and knowledges that have shaped the Americas. Decolonial thinking, he insists, opens space to imagine plural modernities and dismantle colonial patterns that still define power, culture, and self‑understanding.
