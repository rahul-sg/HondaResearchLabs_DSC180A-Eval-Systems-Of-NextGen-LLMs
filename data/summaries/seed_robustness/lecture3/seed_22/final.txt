This lecture centers on Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the *coloniality of power* as a framework for understanding how structures created during colonialism continue to shape modernity, capitalism, and identity in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist whose work evolved from dependency theory and political economy to cultural and decolonial studies—argued that colonialism persisted beyond formal independence through enduring hierarchies of knowledge, labor, and race.

Within the Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project, Quijano and scholars such as Walter Mignolo, Arturo Escobar, and Catherine Walsh challenge Western modernity’s claim to universal progress. They show that modernity and coloniality were *co‑constituted*: Western advancement was built on colonial domination that defined the “West” in contrast to the “Rest.” The lecture is structured around three core points.  

**Point 1 – Coloniality of Power:** Quijano identifies two intertwined processes in the making of “America”: (1) the creation of racial distinctions between conquerors and conquered, and (2) the establishment of new global labor systems—slavery, encomienda, and mita—that organized production for European markets. This produced a *systemic racial division of labor* linking whiteness to authority and associating non‑European peoples with unpaid or coerced work. These hierarchies became the foundation of global capitalism and persist through dependency and racialized inequality.

**Point 2 – Eurocentrism:** Colonial domination also shaped epistemology. Eurocentrism naturalized European superiority by presenting its worldview as universal knowledge and by portraying other societies as “underdeveloped.” The resulting linear narrative of progress obscures the historical violence that enabled European expansion. Quijano connects these ideas with Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest” discourse, showing how representations of difference continue to reproduce colonial hierarchies.

**Point 3 – Eurocentrism and Historical Experience in Latin America:** Eurocentric national ideologies after independence—such as “Indianesque nationalism” and later *mestizaje*—romanticized Indigenous pasts while excluding living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant peoples. These myths sustained whiteness as an ideal and masked inequality within nation‑building.

The lecture concludes by underscoring heterogeneity and discontinuity in history, rejecting linear European models of change, and calling for *decoloniality*: centering non‑Western languages, cosmologies, and worldviews. Quijano’s appeal to free ourselves from the “Eurocentric mirror” invites a reimagining of plural modernities and more equitable forms of knowing and being.
