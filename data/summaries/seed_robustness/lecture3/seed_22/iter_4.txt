This lecture extends Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power**, showing how colonial domination outlives formal empire through racial, economic, and epistemological hierarchies that continue shaping Latin America and the modern world. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist, moved from dependency theory to decolonial thought, shifting his focus from political economy to the interrelation of knowledge, power, and identity. His ideas form a core of the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project**, which argues that modernity and coloniality were **co‑constituted**: Western modernity emerged through conquest, exploitation, and the silencing of other histories.  

Within this framework:  
- **Modernity** is revealed not as a neutral or universal epoch of progress, but as a historical process grounded in colonial expansion and the belief in Western superiority. Counter‑narratives challenge this “great march of progress” that obscures violence and racial hierarchy.  
- **Coloniality** refers to the persistent logic that privileges Eurocentric ways of knowing and ordering the world, naturalizing social categories first produced under colonial rule. It links knowledge, race, and labor in a **matrix of power** that continues to structure global capitalism.  
- **Decoloniality** centers non‑Western language, culture, and cosmology, seeking to value alternative epistemologies and plural modernities suppressed under Eurocentrism.  

According to Quijano, two intertwined historical processes produced what he calls the “coloniality of power”: (1) the **constitution of difference** between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), which racialized human division; and (2) the **constitution of new structures of labor control** that organized the world economy around extraction and forced work systems such as slavery, the encomienda, and the mita. Over time, **Eurocentrism** legitimated these hierarchies, constructing linear narratives of development (“Third World”/“First World”) that place Europe as universal model.  

Cultural and national examples illustrate these legacies. Cuarón’s *Roma* exposes layered inequalities rooted in race, class, and gender. Historian **Rebecca Earle** traces “Indianesque nationalism,” where post‑independence elites celebrated a symbolic Indigenous past while excluding living Indigenous peoples. Later, the ideology of **mestizaje** promoted racial mixing as unity but continued to equate advancement with whiteness—visibly captured in Broccos’s *The Redemption of Ham*.  

Slides on **heterogeneity and discontinuity** call for rejecting linear European histories and recognizing complex, situated transformations. Quijano’s closing appeal urges detachment from the “Eurocentric mirror” that distorts identity, advancing a decolonial horizon where diverse worldviews and experiences define modernity on their own terms.
