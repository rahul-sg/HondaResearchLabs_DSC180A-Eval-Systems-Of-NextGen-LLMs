This lecture deepens Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power**, explaining how colonial domination persists through interconnected systems of race, labor, knowledge, and identity in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist who moved from dependency theory to decolonial thought—argues that colonialism created not only political subjugation but a durable matrix that structures global modernity itself. His ideas contribute to the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project**, which analyzes how these dimensions were **co‑constituted**, not sequential: modernity arose through colonial expansion, and coloniality remains the hidden logic sustaining modern progress.  

- **Modernity**: a self‑affirming Western story of progress and reason, challenged here by counter‑histories revealing that European “advancement” depended on conquest and the extraction of American resources.  
- **Coloniality**: the ongoing epistemic and social hierarchies that privilege Eurocentric categories while erasing Indigenous and other knowledges.  
- **Decoloniality**: attempts to center non‑Western languages, cosmologies, and forms of being as valid sites of knowledge and politics.  

The lecture traces how the **coloniality of power** emerged through two historical processes: (1) defining a racial divide between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) creating new global systems of labor control such as slavery, encomienda, and mita. These practices linked racial hierarchy to capitalism, establishing Europe’s economic dominance. Over time, **Eurocentrism** naturalized these inequalities by presenting Western superiority as universal truth.  

Examples from contemporary and historical culture illustrate these legacies. Alfonso Cuarón’s *Roma* exposes enduring hierarchies of race, class, and gender in everyday life. Historian Rebecca Earle’s notion of **“Indianesque nationalism”** shows how early Latin American elites celebrated an imagined Indigenous past to distance themselves from Spain while excluding real Indigenous peoples. The later **mestizaje** ideal claimed national unity through racial mixing but continued to privilege whiteness, as visualized in works such as *The Redemption of Ham* (1895).  

Slides on **heterogeneity and discontinuity** emphasize that a “crisis” in European knowledge opens space for plural ways of understanding history and change. Decolonial thought rejects linear, Eurocentric notions of progress, recognizing that societies evolve through overlapping, context‑specific processes. Quijano’s final call for **decoloniality** urges breaking from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts identity and acknowledging the diverse historical experiences and epistemologies that constitute the Americas.
