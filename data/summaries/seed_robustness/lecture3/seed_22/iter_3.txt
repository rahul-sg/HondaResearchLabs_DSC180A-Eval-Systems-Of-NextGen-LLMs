This lecture expands on Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power**, explaining how colonial domination endures beyond formal empire through interlinked structures of race, labor, knowledge, and identity in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist whose work evolved from dependency theory to decolonial thought—argues that colonialism produced not just political rule but a continuing **matrix of power** shaping global modernity itself. His analysis anchors the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project**, which contends that modernity and coloniality were **co‑constituted**: Europe’s modern progress was built through conquest, resource extraction, and racial hierarchy.  

- **Modernity**: framed by the West as a universal path of progress, yet historically dependent on colonial expansion. Counter‑histories expose how this “great march of civilization” silenced its violent origins.  
- **Coloniality**: the enduring logic that privileges Eurocentric categories of knowledge and social order, casting Indigenous and non‑Western ways of knowing as inferior. It links racial classification to economic control, as seen in slavery, the encomienda, and the mita.  
- **Decoloniality**: more than appreciation of non‑Western culture, it is an active **praxis** aimed at dismantling epistemic dependence and validating multiple cosmologies and worldviews.  

Quijano identifies two foundational processes: the racial division between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and the creation of a capitalist world economy organizing labor through racialized exploitation. **Eurocentrism** later normalized these hierarchies by portraying European dominance as the natural course of history.  

Cultural and national examples demonstrate how this ideology persists. Alfonso Cuarón’s *Roma* reflects overlapping legacies of race, class, and gender formed under coloniality. Historian **Rebecca Earle’s “Indianesque nationalism”** reveals how post‑independence elites romanticized an Indigenous past while marginalizing actual Indigenous people. Later, the **mestizaje** ideal promoted racial mixing as national unity yet continued to measure advancement by proximity to whiteness—visualized in **Modesto Broccos’s *The Redemption of Ham***, which naturalizes whiteness and assimilation as societal progress.  

Slides on **heterogeneity and discontinuity** highlight that breaking the Eurocentric view requires rejecting linear, dualistic histories and recognizing diverse, situated experiences of change. Quijano’s closing call for **decoloniality** urges societies to free themselves from the “Eurocentric mirror” that distorts identity, affirming plural modernities and the knowledge systems long suppressed within the Americas.
