This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **“coloniality of power”** and its ongoing influence on modernity, capitalism, and national identity in Latin America. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist, moved from dependency theory and cultural analysis to develop **decolonial theory**, linking political economy and epistemology. His work is central to the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** project, which reinterprets global history through counter‑narratives from the “Rest” that challenge the notion of Western modernity as the sole pathway to progress.

According to Quijano, two intertwined historical processes constitute the coloniality of power: first, the creation of racial distinctions separating the European conquerors from the colonized peoples; second, the establishment of new systems of labor control to manage the extraction of wealth from the Americas. These processes founded a *new model of global power* in which racial hierarchies became embedded in capitalism through slavery, the encomienda, and the mita systems. Race thus functioned as both an organizing category of difference and a justification for labor exploitation. Eurocentrism naturalized this order, linking whiteness to modernity and positioning non‑European peoples as backward, “underdeveloped,” or pre‑modern.

As the lecture highlights, this Eurocentric worldview extended beyond economics into knowledge, culture, and politics. Linear theories of history portrayed Europe as the measure of civilization, shaping who defines “development,” “democracy,” and national success. Yet, crises in the European perspective of knowledge have opened intellectual space for **heterogeneity and discontinuity**—recognizing overlapping, locally situated processes of change rather than a universal path of progress. This shift parallels Aymara notions such as *pachakuti* and aligns with Stuart Hall’s critique of the “West and the Rest.”

Decoloniality, for Quijano, requires **centering non‑Western modernities**—valuing suppressed cosmologies, languages, and epistemologies. Cultural applications, such as the film *Roma*, reveal how coloniality persists through race, gender, and class. Similarly, the lecture connects Eurocentrism to nation‑building: Rebecca Earle’s concept of **“Indianesque nationalism”** shows how early republican symbols romanticized an Indigenous past while excluding Indigenous peoples from full nationhood, later transformed into the ideology of **mestizaje**, which claimed unity through racial mixing but often reaffirmed whiteness. Quijano’s call to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror” captures the lecture’s central message—to recognize the co‑constitution of coloniality and modernity and to envision plural, decolonial ways of knowing, being, and belonging.
