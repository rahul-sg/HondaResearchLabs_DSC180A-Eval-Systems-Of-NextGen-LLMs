This lecture develops Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** within the larger **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework. A Peruvian sociologist whose work spans dependency theory, cultural identity, and epistemology, Quijano argues that **modernity and coloniality were co‑constituted**, not sequential. The MCD project identifies three dimensions: **modernity**, the Eurocentric narrative portraying Western progress as universal; **coloniality**, the long‑lasting structure of domination that privileges Western knowledge and rationality; and **decoloniality**, the effort to recentre non‑Western languages, cultures, and ways of being.

**Point 1 – Coloniality of Power:**  
Quijano traces two entwined historical processes that defined the formation of “America”: (1) the construction of differences between “conquerors” (the West) and the “conquered” (the Rest); and (2) the establishment of new forms of labor control tied to the Americas’ resources. Systems such as slavery, the encomienda, and the mita organized production for global markets and institutionalized a **racialized division of labor**. Race became a mental category of modernity, naturalizing hierarchies that structured political economy and global capitalism.

**Point 2 – Eurocentrism:**  
European domination of the world market was not inevitable but arose through colonial exploitation. **Eurocentrism** became both ideology and knowledge system, linking whiteness to superiority and defining the West as the exclusive agent of progress. This perspective produced dualisms—developed/underdeveloped, civilized/primitive—that framed world history as a linear path toward European modernity. Quijano’s analysis complements Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest” discourse, revealing how such representations continue to shape global hierarchies and ideas of success.

**Point 3 – National Identity and Historical Experience:**  
In post‑independence Latin America, Eurocentric thought persisted in projects of nation building. As historian **Rebecca Earle** shows, “Indianesque nationalism” romanticized an Indigenous past while excluding living Indigenous peoples from citizenship. Later **mestizaje** ideologies promoted racial mixing and unity yet often reaffirmed whiteness and social inequality. Media such as *Roma* illustrate how these colonial hierarchies endure in everyday life.

Quijano’s **call for decoloniality** (Slide 19) urges a step beyond the “Eurocentric mirror” that distorts self‑understanding. Decoloniality means freeing knowledge and identity from colonial frameworks and acknowledging the **heterogeneity and discontinuity** of historical processes. In doing so, Latin American thought can recover multiple modernities rooted in diverse epistemologies and lived experiences.
