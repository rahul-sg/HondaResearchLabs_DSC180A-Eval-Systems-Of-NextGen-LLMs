This lecture examines **Aníbal Quijano’s** concept of the **“coloniality of power”**—a framework explaining how colonial domination endures beyond formal colonialism through racialized labor, knowledge, and culture. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist whose work spans dependency theory, cultural analysis, and decolonial thought, argues that coloniality and modernity were **co‑constituted**: the rise of Western modernity depended on the exploitation and classification of conquered peoples in the Americas. His work forms part of the broader **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** project, alongside scholars such as **Arturo Escobar, Walter Mignolo, and Catherine Walsh**, which challenges the notion that “modernity” naturally belongs to the West.  

According to Quijano, two intertwined historical processes formed this modern global order: (1) the creation of racial distinctions separating European conquerors from colonized peoples, and (2) the construction of new systems of **labor control**—slavery, the *encomienda*, and the *mita*—to channel wealth from the Americas into a world market. In this way, race became both an ideological and structural tool for organizing capitalism. Over time, **Eurocentrism** naturalized this hierarchy, presenting white Europe as the universal model for civilization and relegating others to categories of “underdeveloped” or “pre‑modern.”  

The lecture connects Eurocentrism not only to economics but also to epistemology: Western ways of knowing became dominant, marginalizing indigenous cosmologies and non‑Western perspectives. Slides on **Eurocentrism and historical experience** emphasize that linear narratives of progress—“pre‑capital” to “developed,” “Third World” to “First World”—sustain global inequality and define whose knowledge counts. Yet, as Quijano and later decolonial thinkers note, crises in this European epistemic model reveal **heterogeneity and discontinuity** in history, echoing ideas like the Aymara *pachakuti* that view transformation as cyclical and locally situated.  

Applications such as the film *Roma* show how colonial hierarchies of race, gender, and labor persist in cultural representation. The lecture then turns to **nation formation**, highlighting Rebecca Earle’s concept of **“Indianesque nationalism.”** Post‑independence elites used romanticized Indigenous imagery to distance themselves from Spain while still excluding living Indigenous peoples—a tension that later produced the ideology of **mestizaje**, or racial mixing, which claimed inclusivity yet reinforced whiteness.  

Quijano ultimately calls for **decoloniality**: freeing ourselves from the “Eurocentric mirror” to recognize multiple modernities and value suppressed epistemologies. This entails reimagining history, culture, and identity beyond Western universals toward more plural and equitable ways of knowing and being.
