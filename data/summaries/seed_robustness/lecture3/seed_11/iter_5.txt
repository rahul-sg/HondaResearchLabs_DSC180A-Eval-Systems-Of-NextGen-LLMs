This lecture deepens the study of Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power**, showing how colonial domination endures as a global matrix that organizes knowledge, labor, identity, and representation in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist whose work spans dependency theory, political economy, and decolonial thought—traced how the hierarchies founded during conquest shaped modernity itself rather than disappearing with political independence.  

Within the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework, modernity and coloniality are presented as **co‑constituted**. Western modernity’s story of progress was enabled by conquest and racial classification, not independent of them. “Coloniality” refers to the lasting hierarchies of race and knowledge that sustain this order, while “decoloniality” aims to recover suppressed perspectives, languages, and cosmologies, opening space for plural modernities.  

The **coloniality of power** rests on two intertwined processes: (1) the racial differentiation of peoples into conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) the creation of global systems of labor control that tied race to economic exploitation. Enslavement and forced‑labor regimes such as the encomienda and mita systems linked race to work status, producing a **racialized division of labor** that structured emerging capitalism.  

Slides on **Eurocentrism** show how these material hierarchies were reinforced by intellectual ones—dualistic, linear narratives that positioned Europe as the sole agent of progress (“First World/Third World”). Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest” framing helped reveal how Enlightenment discourses naturalized Western superiority and silenced alternative histories.  

Subsequent sections examined **nation‑building and identity**. Post‑independence elites constructed *Indianesque nationalism*—celebrating an Indigenous past to distance themselves from Spain yet excluding Indigenous peoples from the modern nation. Later, the ideology of **mestizaje** presented racial mixing as harmony while upholding Eurocentric ideals of progress, visualized in works like Modesto Broccos’s *A Redenção de Cã*.  

The lecture also introduced **heterogeneity and discontinuity** as ways to rethink historical process—drawing on Aymara notions of *pachakuti* to stress that change occurs through overlapping, situated experiences rather than a single European path.  

Portfolio tasks invited students to select and interpret key passages from Quijano to connect text and theory, emphasizing active engagement with his arguments rather than memorization.  

Finally, Quijano’s **call for decoloniality** urged freeing thought from the Eurocentric mirror and affirming diverse ways of knowing as the foundation for building new, decolonial modernities.
