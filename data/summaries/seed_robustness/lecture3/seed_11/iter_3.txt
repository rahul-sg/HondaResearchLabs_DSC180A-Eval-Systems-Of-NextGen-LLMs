This lecture explores **Aníbal Quijano’s** concept of the **“coloniality of power,”** a framework explaining how structures of domination created during colonization continue to organize race, labor, and knowledge long after formal colonial rule. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist whose work ranges from dependency theory to decolonial thought, argues that modernity and coloniality were **co‑constituted**: Western modernity emerged through the conquest and exploitation of the Americas. His ideas align with the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** project—developed with scholars such as **Walter Mignolo, Arturo Escobar, and Catherine Walsh**—which challenges the Western claim to be modernity’s origin and sole model.  

Quijano identifies two intertwined processes behind the modern global order:  
1) the construction of differences between European conquerors (“the West”) and colonized peoples (“the Rest”), and  
2) the creation of new systems of **labor control**—slavery, the *encomienda*, and the *mita*—that funneled American resources into a global capitalist system.  
Through these, **race became a mental category of modernity**, a hierarchy that justified inequality and linked racial identity to one’s position in the labor system.  

Over time, these hierarchies solidified into **Eurocentrism**, a worldview that treated European culture as universal, defining others as undeveloped or pre‑modern. The lecture notes that Eurocentrism shaped not only capitalism but also ways of knowing, privileging Western epistemologies while obscuring Indigenous and other non‑Western perspectives. Quijano critiques linear, dualistic narratives—“pre‑capital / industrial,” “Third World / First World”—that claim social progress as Europe’s achievement. The slides suggest that questioning such narratives opens space for understanding the **heterogeneity** of history and the possibility of multiple, situated ways of transformation.  

Applications illustrate how these logics endure. The film *Roma* demonstrates the persistence of racialized and gendered labor hierarchies in modern Mexican society. Rebecca Earle’s concept of **“Indianesque nationalism”** shows how post‑independence elites celebrated a symbolic Indigenous past while excluding living Indigenous peoples. The subsequent ideology of **mestizaje** purported to unify the nation through racial mixing but instead reproduced whiteness and Eurocentric ideals—an outcome that, from Quijano’s perspective, reflects the continuing **coloniality of power** within national identity formation.  

Quijano’s closing call for **decoloniality** urges freeing ourselves from the “Eurocentric mirror” to recognize diverse modernities and value the knowledge systems and worldviews long suppressed by colonial legacies.
