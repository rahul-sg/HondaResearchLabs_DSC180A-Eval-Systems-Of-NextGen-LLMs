This lecture expands on Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power**, showing how colonial domination persists as a global matrix that organizes knowledge, labor, and identity in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist who moved from dependency theory and political economy to decolonial thought—argues that colonial hierarchies were not erased by independence but absorbed into modern global systems.  

Within the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework, modernity and coloniality are shown to be **co‑constituted**. Western modernity’s narrative of progress arose directly from conquest and exploitation, not apart from them. “Coloniality” refers to the enduring hierarchies of race and knowledge underpinning this order, while “decoloniality” emphasizes the recovery of plural modernities grounded in non‑Western languages, epistemologies, and cosmologies.  

The **coloniality of power** derives from two historical processes: (1) the racial classification of peoples into conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) the creation of global labor systems that tied racial difference to economic control. Practices such as slavery, encomienda, and mita established a **systemic racial division of labor** that persists within capitalism. Slides on **Eurocentrism** highlight how these material hierarchies were reinforced by intellectual ones—linear and dualist theories of development (“Third World/First World”) that positioned Europe as the agent of history. Stuart Hall’s concept of the “West and the Rest” reveals how Enlightenment and colonial discourses naturalized Western superiority as universal truth.  

Later slides situate these dynamics in **nation‑building and identity formation**. Post‑independence elites adopted *Indianesque nationalism*, idealizing an Indigenous past while excluding Indigenous peoples from the nation’s modern image. The ideology of **mestizaje** later reframed racial mixing as national harmony but maintained aspirations toward whiteness and Eurocentric progress, a continuing expression of coloniality in cultural and visual forms such as Modesto Broccos’s *A Redenção de Cã*.  

The lecture then underscored **heterogeneity and discontinuity** as alternatives to a single European historical path. Drawing on ideas like the Aymara *pachakuti*, it presented history as overlapping, locally situated, and open to transformation—aligning with the decolonial turn within the MCD framework.  

Quijano’s closing **call for decoloniality** urges us “to learn to free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror in which our image is always, and necessarily, distorted”—to stop being defined by what we are not and to affirm diverse, situated ways of knowing as the basis for a decolonial modernity.
