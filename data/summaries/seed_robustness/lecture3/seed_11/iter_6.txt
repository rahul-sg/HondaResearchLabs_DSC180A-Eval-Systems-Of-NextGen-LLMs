This lecture examined Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power**, showing how colonial domination endures as a global matrix organizing knowledge, labor, and identity in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist whose work spans dependency theory, political economy, and decolonial thought—traced how the hierarchies created during conquest shaped the very formation of modernity rather than disappearing with independence.  

Within the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework, modernity and coloniality are presented as **co‑constituted**. The claimed universality of Western modernity was built upon conquest and classification, not apart from them. “Coloniality” names the continuing hierarchies of race and epistemology that sustain this order, while “decoloniality” seeks to recover non‑Western languages, cosmologies, and knowledge to reveal *multiple modernities*.  

The **coloniality of power** emerged through two linked processes: (1) the differentiation between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) new global systems of labor control that bound race to economic exploitation. Colonial structures such as slavery, the encomienda, and the mita organized labor for European markets. As Quijano states, from the beginning of colonization Europeans associated nonpaid or nonwage labor with the “dominated races,” establishing a **racialized division of labor** that underpinned global capitalism.  

Slides on **Eurocentrism** explained how these material hierarchies were reinforced through knowledge systems. Eurocentrism functioned as a mode of producing knowledge from a Western perspective, promoting dualistic, linear histories—“underdeveloped/developed,” “Third World/First World”—that naturalized Europe as the driver of progress. Drawing on Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest,” the lecture connected this to Enlightenment and capitalist discourses that privileged whiteness and obscured colonial histories.  

In discussions of **nation and identity**, post‑independence elites used *Indianesque nationalism* to celebrate a remote Indigenous past while marginalizing Indigenous peoples in the present. The later ideology of **mestizaje** framed racial mixing as national unity but reproduced Eurocentric ideals of superiority, visible in cultural symbols and paintings like Modesto Broccos’s *A Redenção de Cã*.  

The lecture also addressed **heterogeneity and discontinuity**: the “crisis of the European perspective of knowledge” opens space to grasp complex, overlapping histories. Aymara concepts such as *pachakuti* illustrate that social change is contingent and plural, not linear.  

Portfolio activities guided students to link these themes to passages from Quijano’s text. The session closed with his call for **decoloniality**—to free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror and affirm diverse ways of knowing as the basis for building new, decolonial modernities.
