This lecture explores Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **“coloniality of power”** as a key framework for understanding how race, labor, and knowledge became intertwined through colonial domination and continue to shape global hierarchies. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist central to Latin American thought—developed this idea across successive projects: **dependency theory** (political economy), cultural studies of **modernity and identity**, and later **decolonial theory** focusing on epistemology and the relation between knowledge and power. Within the broader **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project**, modernity is reinterpreted not as a natural Western achievement but as historically co‑constituted with colonialism.

The lecture outlines two formative processes underlying the coloniality of power: (1) the racialized division between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) the construction of new global systems of labor control in the Americas. Through slavery, the encomienda, and the mita, colonial capitalism organized production for global markets while embedding racial hierarchy in economic relations. These patterns established enduring social and spatial orders that distributed privilege, authority, and access unequally. Quijano links this structure to the persistence of **Eurocentrism**—the worldview that situates Europe as the source and standard of knowledge, rationality, and progress.

**Eurocentrism**, the lecture emphasizes, is more than bias; it is a **mode of producing knowledge** from a particular Western perspective. It naturalizes linear and dualistic narratives—“developed/underdeveloped,” “First/Third World”—that present European history as universal progress. Yet the slides note a growing “**crisis of the European perspective of knowledge**,” which opens space for alternative epistemologies such as the **Aymara notion of pachakuti**, a cyclical understanding of upheaval and renewal that challenges linear histories and highlights heterogeneous, situational forms of agency. This move toward complexity signals the shift to **decoloniality**, or centering diverse and previously marginalized ways of knowing.

Cultural examples illuminate these dynamics. Alfonso Cuarón’s *Roma* exposes overlapping colonial legacies through its portrayal of race, class, and gender, while historian **Rebecca Earle’s** study of “Indianesque nationalism” shows how post‑independence nation‑building romanticized Indigenous ancestry yet excluded Indigenous people from belonging. Later **mestizaje** ideologies posed an unresolved question—**closing the gap or widening the divide**—between inclusive narratives of mixture and the continued privileging of whiteness. The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call to free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror and imagine plural, decolonial futures.
