This lecture examined Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** and its continuing influence on modernity, capitalism, and knowledge in Latin America. As introduced within the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework, Quijano’s work represents part of a broader Latin American intellectual current that also includes scholars such as Walter Mignolo, Arturo Escobar, and Catherine Walsh, though each approaches decolonial thought from distinct perspectives.  

**Coloniality of power** describes how two historical processes shaped the Americas and the modern global order: (1) the invention of racial distinctions separating conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) the creation of new structures of labor control around extraction and export. Systems such as slavery, the **encomienda**, and the **mita** organized production through racialized hierarchies, forming a **systemic racial division of labor** that tied social status, access, and economic roles to ideas of racial difference. Quijano argues that this linkage between race and labor became the foundation for **global capitalism**, arranging dependency and subordination that persisted long after formal colonial rule.  

In this model, **race functions as a mental category of modernity**—a social invention used to legitimate domination and economic exploitation. Over time, Eurocentric ideologies framed whiteness as civilization and constructed linear hierarchies of history: “pre‑capital” to “industrial,” “underdeveloped” to “developed,” “Third World” to “First World.” These narratives sustained the illusion that Europe was the sole driver of progress. **Eurocentrism**, therefore, is more than cultural pride; it is an **epistemological order** that privileges Western ways of knowing while silencing Indigenous and other non‑Western epistemologies.  

Slides on **heterogeneity and discontinuity** questioned this Eurocentric view, emphasizing that historical change is uneven and locally situated rather than linear. **Decoloniality** thus calls for recognizing multiple modernities and reclaiming perspectives historically dismissed as non‑rational.  

Cultural and national examples reinforced these patterns. The film *Roma* illustrates layered colonial relations through the racialized labor of its Mixtec protagonist. Historian **Rebecca Earle’s** analysis of “Indianesque nationalism” similarly showed how post‑independence elites romanticized an Indigenous past yet excluded Indigenous peoples of the present, later promoting **mestizaje** as national unity while continuing to privilege whiteness.  

In the **portfolio activity**, students connected passages from Quijano’s essay to these themes of power, race, labor, and identity. The lecture concluded with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality**—to free oneself from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts Latin American realities and to recover plural, non‑Western ways of knowing and being.
