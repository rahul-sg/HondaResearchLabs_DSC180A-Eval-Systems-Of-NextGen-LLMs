This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** and its continuing influence on modernity, capitalism, and knowledge in Latin America. A foundational figure in the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project**, Quijano—alongside thinkers like Walter Mignolo, Arturo Escobar, and Catherine Walsh—traced how colonialism shaped not only economies and societies but also enduring systems of thought. His framework links the racialization of power, control of labor, and privileging of Western knowledge as mutually constitutive legacies of conquest.  

The lecture outlines two intertwined processes defining coloniality of power: first, the invention of hierarchical racial differences distinguishing conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”); and second, the creation of new global structures of labor control. Systems such as slavery, the encomienda, and the mita organized production around racial hierarchies, embedding the belief that dominated peoples were suited for coerced or unpaid labor. These colonial formations underwrote what Quijano saw as a new global model of power and capitalism, where exploitation and dependency persisted even after formal independence.  

Crucially, coloniality also operates at the **epistemological** level—through what slide 4 calls “epistemological domination”—by elevating European categories of knowledge and obscuring Indigenous or non‑Western ways of understanding. This “Eurocentrism” naturalized Western superiority and linear notions of progress, labeling societies as “underdeveloped” or “Third World” when they diverged from European trajectories. Slides on **heterogeneity/discontinuity** highlight Quijano’s and the MCD project’s rejection of such linear historicism: politics and transformation are not uniform or sequential but arise from diverse, locally situated experiences. Concepts like the **Aymara pachakuti** symbolize the possibility of cyclical, multidirectional change that troubles Western narratives of modernization.  

The lecture then connects these ideas to **Eurocentrism and national identity** in Latin America. Drawing on Rebecca Earle’s analysis of “Indianesque nationalism,” early post‑independence elites celebrated a romanticized Indigenous past while marginalizing contemporary Indigenous peoples. Later **mestizaje** ideologies portrayed racial mixing as unity but often perpetuated whiteness as the social norm. Media examples such as *Roma* illustrate how racialized and gendered hierarchies rooted in coloniality still shape everyday life.  

The session concludes with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality**: to free ourselves from the “Eurocentric mirror” that distorts self‑perception and to center plural modernities grounded in suppressed epistemologies, cultures, and worldviews.
