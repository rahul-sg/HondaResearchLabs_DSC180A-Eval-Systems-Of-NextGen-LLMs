This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **“coloniality of power”** as a framework for understanding how modern global hierarchies of race, labor, and knowledge were formed through colonialism and persist today. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist—developed this idea through successive intellectual projects: dependency theory’s analysis of political economy, cultural approaches to identity and modernity, and, later, decolonial theory with its focus on epistemology and the links between knowledge and power. Within the broader **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project**, his work situates modernity not as an inherent Western achievement but as a historically contingent outcome of colonial conquest and exploitation.

The lecture identifies two intertwined historical processes constituting the **coloniality of power**: the creation of racial difference between “conquerors” (the West) and “conquered” (the Rest), and the establishment of new global systems of labor control in the Americas. Capitalist production—through slavery, the encomienda, and the mita—organized racialized labor to serve global markets in goods like silver, sugar, and cacao. These arrangements fused race with economic status, embedding inequality into global capitalism and shaping who could access privilege, authority, and mobility. In Quijano’s view, this logic endures through the **Eurocentrism** that positions Europe as the source and measure of knowledge, progress, and civilization.

Slides emphasize that Eurocentrism is not only a political or economic bias but a **mode of producing knowledge** from a particular Western perspective. It naturalizes linear, dualistic concepts such as “developed/underdeveloped” and frames European advancement as universal history. The coloniality of power therefore operates both materially and epistemologically, sustaining a worldview that marginalizes non‑Western ways of knowing. Quijano urges the need to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror where our image is always, necessarily, distorted,” calling for **decoloniality**—the centering of diverse, localized, and historically situated epistemologies.

The lecture applies these ideas to culture and representation. Alfonso Cuarón’s *Roma* exemplifies layered colonial legacies in class, race, and gender relations, while historian **Rebecca Earle’s** notion of “Indianesque nationalism” shows how early Latin American nations romanticized the Indigenous past to assert distinct identities yet continued to exclude Indigenous peoples from full belonging. Later **mestizaje** ideologies celebrated racial mixing as unity but often reinforced whiteness and the erasure of Afro‑descendant and Indigenous presence. Across these examples, the lecture highlights Quijano’s enduring question: how to dismantle Eurocentric structures of knowledge, power, and identity to envision truly plural and decolonial futures.
