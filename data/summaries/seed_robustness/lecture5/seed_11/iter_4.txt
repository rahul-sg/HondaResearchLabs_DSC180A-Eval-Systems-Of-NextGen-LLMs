This lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed forces shaping identity, law, and education. It began by asking students to reflect on ways teachers or administrators may have labeled them and how such labels—whether accurate or distorted—shaped self‑understanding, motivation, and perceived intelligence. Sonia Nieto’s idea that society classifies people by visible and invisible traits framed this reflection: these classifications attribute fixed characteristics and sustain systems that privilege certain groups while disadvantaging others.  

Addressing the hesitation often surrounding discussions of race, the lecture stressed that silence and denial are inadequate responses. Instead, understanding requires courage—courage to confront how social views and personal upbringing influence attitudes about racial and cultural difference. Race was presented as socially, not biologically, determined. Historical shifts in racial definitions revealed how political conflicts and economic conditions have repeatedly redefined group boundaries, making racial identity seem like “common sense.” The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated how scientific evidence debunks biological myths about race while highlighting its social reality.  

Legal scholar Ian Haney López’s framework showed that law constructs race through both coercion and ideology. As coercion, laws historically upheld racial divisions via immigration barriers, naturalization restrictions, and bans on interracial marriage—structuring who could claim citizenship or property. As ideology, law normalizes racial hierarchies by disguising them as natural or race‑neutral, evident in criminal justice and school‑discipline policies. Court cases on Armenians, Japanese immigrants, and Mexicans demonstrated how legal definitions of whiteness and belonging shifted with economic and political interests, while the changing U.S. Census made those shifts visible in official categories of identity.  

Turning to culture, Nieto described it as the continually changing set of values, traditions, and relationships created and shared by people—not inherited biologically. Culture is learned, hybrid, and multifaceted, formed through interactions among family, peers, class background, religion, language, media, and community. Schools also possess distinct cultures expressed through physical conditions, disciplinary routines, student grouping, and access to programs and resources. Underlying these structures are assumptions that ability varies among students yet is fixed and measurable. Such beliefs reinforce academic hierarchies and shape how students view their intelligence, participation, and future opportunities.
