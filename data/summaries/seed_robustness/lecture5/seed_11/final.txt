This lecture explored how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity, law, and schooling. It opened by asking students to reflect on how teachers or counselors may have labeled them and how such labels—accurate or distorted—affected their self‑perception, motivation, and sense of intelligence. This personal reflection served as an entry point to Sonia Nieto’s argument that society classifies people by visible and invisible traits and uses those categories to assign fixed characteristics. Such classifications often produce exaggerations and stereotypes that advantage some groups while disadvantaging others.  

The lecture emphasized that, although discussing race can provoke silence or fear, genuine understanding requires courage to face how upbringing and social context shape beliefs about racial and cultural difference. Race was presented as socially rather than biologically determined—its meaning historically remade through political struggle, economics, and law. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated that racial ideas are learned and appear as “common sense.”  

Legal scholar Ian Haney López added that law both constructs and legitimates race. As *coercion*, laws historically enforced racial boundaries—through immigration restrictions, exclusionary naturalization standards, and bans on interracial marriage—that defined who could gain full citizenship. As *ideology*, the law persuades people to view racial hierarchies as natural or race‑neutral, even within seemingly objective policies such as criminal justice or school discipline. Historical citizenship cases reinforced this: Armenians were admitted as “white,” a Japanese immigrant was rejected when “skin color” proved inadequate as a test, and Mexicans’ racial status shifted after U.S. territorial expansion. Changing U.S. Census categories further showed how definitions of whiteness and belonging evolved through policy decisions, conveying that whiteness itself was legally and socially made over generations.  

The lecture then turned to culture. According to Nieto, culture consists of dynamic values, relationships, and worldviews continuously created and transformed by people; it is learned, hybrid, and multifaceted—not inherited biologically or reducible to food or festivals. Cultural experience is mediated by family, peers, religion, class, community, media, and language. Schools have their own distinct cultures reflected in routines, tracking practices, and ideas about ability. A core assumption highlighted was that ability varies among students yet is fixed, unchangeable, and measurable—beliefs that reinforce hierarchies of intelligence and affect students’ participation, achievement, and sense of future possibility.
