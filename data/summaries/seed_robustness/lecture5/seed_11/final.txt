This lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape law, identity, and schooling. It began with reflection questions about labels in education—how teachers, counselors, or administrators may have categorized students in ways that shaped self‑perception, motivation, and sense of capability. These reflections linked to Sonia Nieto’s idea of “constructing others,” where society classifies people by visible and invisible traits, attaches stereotypes, and establishes policies that advantage some groups and harm others. The lecture highlighted the need for courage to discuss race and cultural difference honestly, countering silence, fear, and denial that often surround these topics.  

Race, the slides stressed, is not biologically determined but socially produced through historical, political, and economic processes that made racial categories appear “common sense.” The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project by the American Anthropological Association provided evidence dismantling myths of racial difference. Legal frameworks were shown to play a central role in building racial meanings. Ian Haney López detailed two mechanisms: **law as coercion**, where rules about immigration, marriage, and naturalization shaped who could marry, immigrate, or claim citizenship and thus defined physical and social boundaries of race; and **law as ideology**, where laws normalize race as a natural rather than socially created hierarchy. Cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans illustrated how courts negotiated racial status and belonging, while interracial marriage bans persisted until 1967. James Baldwin’s observation that “whiteness” required generations of coercion underscored that whiteness itself was legally and politically made. Shifting census categories further revealed how definitions of identity and belonging change with power and time.  

The second half focused on culture. Drawing from Nieto, culture was defined as the evolving values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews people co‑create and transform—learned rather than genetic, dynamic and hybrid, with multiple, mixed identifications. Sociologists (Small et al., 2010) added that culture can be viewed through **values**, **frames**, and **repertoires**, each shaping how people interpret and act in the world. Culture is mediated by family, peers, class, language, religion, and media. Schools themselves embody distinct cultures structured by routines, tracking, and assumptions about fixed ability and merit. These institutional norms shape how students experience belonging, learning, and opportunity. Overall, the lecture connected the construction of race and culture to the legal and educational systems that reproduce—or may challenge—social inequality.
