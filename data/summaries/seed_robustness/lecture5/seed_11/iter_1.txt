This lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity, law, and schooling. It opened with discussion questions prompting students to consider whether they had ever been labeled by teachers or administrators, and how such labels might have influenced their self-perception, motivation, or sense of capability—framing identity construction as a lived, reflective process rather than a theory alone. The lecture emphasized that silence or denial around race and cultural difference often stems from fear, yet meaningful engagement requires courage: courage to examine how personal upbringing and societal messages shape one’s views.  

Slides highlighted Sonia Nieto’s argument that society categorizes people using visible and invisible traits, linking those labels to policies that advantage some groups and disadvantage others. Race, the lecture stressed, has no biological foundation but is produced through social, political, and economic forces. Historical and scientific materials—such as the *RACE: Are We So Different?* project—demonstrate how racial categories are human-made and context-specific.  

A central segment explored law’s role in constructing race. Ian Haney López’s analysis distinguished between *law as coercion*, in which legal codes enforce racial boundaries through exclusionary acts like immigration restrictions and anti-miscegenation laws, and *law as ideology*, where law persuades people to view racial hierarchies as natural or neutral. Court cases on Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans illustrated how citizenship and “whiteness” were legally negotiated, while U.S. Census changes trace shifting official definitions of racial identity. Quoting James Baldwin, slides underscored that “whiteness” itself was historically manufactured through coercion and access to privilege.  

The latter portion shifted to culture. Drawing again from Nieto, culture was defined as dynamic, human-created, and learned rather than inherited—consisting of shared values, relationships, and worldviews that constantly evolve. Individuals participate in multiple, overlapping cultures shaped by family, peers, class, religion, community, and media. The slides also demonstrated that schools possess distinct institutional cultures expressed through layout, routines, grouping, and expectations about “ability” and “intelligence.” These cultural assumptions influence how students experience belonging, recognize potential, and imagine their futures. Overall, the lecture connected the social and legal construction of race with the dynamic, lived processes of culture in education and everyday life.
