This lecture explored how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity, privilege, and schooling. It began by asking students to reflect on labeling in schools—how teachers’ or administrators’ labels might have influenced their self-perception and whether such labels fully captured who they were. Sonia Nieto’s idea of “constructing others” framed this discussion: society classifies people by visible and invisible traits such as race, gender, and class, drawing exaggerated stereotypes that advantage some groups while marginalizing others. One slide emphasized that fear, silence, and denial often surround conversations about race, but the appropriate response is **courage**—the courage to confront how societal views and one’s own upbringing shape attitudes toward difference.  

The lecture reinforced that race is not biologically determined but socially and historically produced. Shifts in politics, economics, and legal definitions have continually redefined racial categories. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project provided scientific evidence debunking biological myths of race and showing how racial “common sense” is learned through social life. Legal scholar Ian Haney López illustrated how law itself constructs racial meaning. “Law as coercion” refers to policies that physically enforced racial boundaries—through immigration and naturalization rules, bans on interracial marriage, or court decisions defining who could be “white.” “Law as ideology” describes how law persuades society to view these boundaries as natural, seen in supposedly neutral criminal and schooling policies that still reproduce inequities. Historical cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans, along with changing U.S. Census categories, showed the shifting legal definitions of belonging and whiteness. Quoting James Baldwin, slides noted that whiteness was made—and maintained—through generations of coercion to secure privilege.  

The second part turned to culture. Nieto defined culture as the dynamic set of values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews continuously created and transformed by people; it is learned, not inherited. Everyone has culture, and individuals draw from multiple, hybrid influences shaped by family, peers, class, geography, and media. Culture also permeates schools, which have distinct routines, hierarchies, and belief systems. U.S. schooling often rests on cultural assumptions that students vary in ability, that ability is fixed and measurable, and that learning follows a strict hierarchy. These assumptions structure access to programs and profoundly affect students’ sense of ability, belonging, and future potential.  

Overall, the lecture connected the social construction of race and culture with legal, linguistic, and educational systems, urging honest reflection and courage to challenge enduring inequities.
