This lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity, social position, and schooling practices. It began with a brief reflective prompt inviting students to consider how labeling by teachers or administrators might have influenced their self-understanding—a springboard to discuss how society “constructs” others through categories of race, gender, and class. Drawing on Sonia Nieto, the lecture emphasized that such classifications are pervasive and often produce stereotypes that advantage some groups while marginalizing others.  

The slides highlighted that race is not biologically determined but socially and historically produced through shifting political, economic, and legal processes. Projects such as the American Anthropological Association’s *RACE: Are We So Different?* demonstrate through scientific evidence that perceived racial differences lack biological basis. Ian Haney López’s work explained how law has been central to constructing racial meanings. “Law as coercion” refers to legal rules that physically and materially enforced racial boundaries—for instance, immigration restrictions, bans on interracial marriage, and court cases defining who could be “white.” “Law as ideology” describes how law legitimates racial hierarchies by persuading people that these distinctions are natural rather than socially imposed, evident in criminal justice and schooling practices that appear neutral but reproduce inequality. Historical examples included the legal classification of Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans and the evolution of U.S. Census categories, all showing how legal and institutional definitions continually reshape ideas of race and belonging. James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” underscored how “whiteness” itself was constructed to secure privilege and citizenship.  

The second portion turned to culture. Nieto defined culture as an evolving set of values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews created and transformed by human groups—not inherited but learned. Culture is complex, hybrid, and multifaceted, shaped by factors such as family, peers, class, geography, and media. Everyone participates in multiple cultural systems, continually selecting and adapting elements over time. Schools also possess their own distinctive cultures, conveyed through routines, resources, and beliefs about intelligence and ability. Many U.S. schools operate on assumptions that ability is fixed and measurable, structuring opportunities accordingly. Such institutional cultures profoundly affect how students perceive learning, belonging, and future potential.  

Overall, the lecture connected the social construction of race and culture with the legal, linguistic, and educational frameworks that sustain inequality while inviting critical reflection and courage to challenge them.
