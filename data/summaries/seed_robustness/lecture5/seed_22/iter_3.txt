This lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity, belonging, and power in education and society. It opened with a reflection on labeling in schools—how teachers or administrators may have categorized students and how such labels affected students’ self‑perception. Sonia Nieto’s concept of “constructing others” framed this inquiry: societies classify people by visible and invisible characteristics such as race, ethnicity, gender, class, or religion, attach exaggerated stereotypes to those groups, and implement policies that advantage some while jeopardizing others. Silence or denial around race persists in schools and society, but the lecture emphasized that honest reflection and courage are necessary to confront how upbringing and social norms mold our views on difference.  

Race, the slides stressed, is not biologically determined but socially and historically produced. Political, economic, and legal developments have continually redefined racial categories, making race appear “common sense.” The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project was cited for showing scientific evidence that dispels biological myths of race. Legal scholar Ian Haney López demonstrated that race is also constructed through law: “law as coercion” refers to statutes that physically enforced racial boundaries—through immigration, naturalization, and marriage restrictions—while “law as ideology” describes how law persuades the public to see racial hierarchies as natural, even in seemingly race‑neutral policies on crime or schooling. Historical cases made this concrete: courts declared Armenians eligible for citizenship as “white” in 1909, rejected a Japanese immigrant’s petition in 1922 when skin color proved an inadequate racial test, and redefined Mexicans after U.S. expansion to mark them as distinct and inferior. Shifting U.S. Census categories further showed how official instruments constrain identity choices. James Baldwin’s reflection underscored that “whiteness” was produced in America through generations of coercion tied to access and privilege.  

The lecture also addressed language and culture. Changing racial and ethnic labels—such as Hispanic, Latino/a, Latinx, Negro, Black, or Asian American—illustrate that language tracks social, political, and economic shifts. Nieto defined culture as a learned, ever‑changing product of human interaction shaped by history, geography, class, and religion; everyone has culture, and identities are hybrid and multifaceted. Schools, too, possess distinct cultures, with routines, hierarchies, and fixed beliefs about ability that guide grouping, expectations, and opportunity. These cultural assumptions influence how students perceive their intelligence, participation, and future possibilities.  

Overall, the lecture linked the social construction of race, language, and culture to the shaping power of legal and educational systems, urging critical awareness and the courage to challenge enduring inequities.
