This lecture examined how both race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape social relations, access to rights, and everyday experiences, particularly within schools. It began with Sonia Nieto’s framework on the “construction” of others: society classifies people by visible and invisible traits, draws fixed conclusions about behavior and ability, and builds policies that advantage some groups while disadvantaging others. Students were asked to reflect on labeling within schools—not to analyze personal identity in depth, but to consider how institutions and categories systematically shape perception. Discussion of “silence, fear, and denial” emphasized the courage needed to confront the effects of racial and cultural beliefs on one’s thinking.

Slides reinforced that race lacks biological foundation. Rather, racial categories emerged through historical, economic, and political struggles that made racial identity seem like “common sense.” The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated this point through scientific evidence dispelling racial myths. Law served as another key mechanism of racial construction. Ian Haney López described “law as coercion,” showing how immigration, naturalization, and marriage laws literally defined racial boundaries and determined access to citizenship and resources. Cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans demonstrated how courts debated and redefined who counted as “white.” James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” captured how “whiteness” was manufactured through coercion and privilege. Later, López introduced “law as ideology,” revealing how legal systems and ostensibly neutral policies persuade people to accept racial hierarchies as natural facts, while media and institutions sustain these ideas without explicitly naming race. Shifts in census categories and public labels (e.g., Hispanic, Black, Asian American) further illustrated that such classifications and language evolve with social and political context.

The second half turned to culture. Nieto defined culture as a dynamic, learned, and hybrid process of creating and transforming shared values, relationships, and worldviews—something everyone possesses. Culture is shaped by overlapping influences including family, peers, class, gender, language, community, and media. Schools embody their own cultures through routines, grouping practices, and beliefs about intelligence and ability, often grounded in assumptions that ability is fixed and measurable. These institutional cultures profoundly influence students’ participation, motivation, and understanding of what it means to succeed. Overall, the lecture linked the social construction of race and culture to the legal, linguistic, and educational structures that make inequality appear ordinary.
