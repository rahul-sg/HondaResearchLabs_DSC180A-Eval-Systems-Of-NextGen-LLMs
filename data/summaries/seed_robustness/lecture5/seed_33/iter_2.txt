This lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity, law, and schooling. It began with reflection on how educators’ labels can influence students’ self-perception, intelligence, and motivation—inviting consideration of whether such labels reflect one’s full self or instead distort it. Sonia Nieto’s work framed how society classifies people through visible and invisible traits such as race, gender, class, religion, and culture, attaching exaggerated or stereotypical meanings that privilege some groups while disadvantaging others. The session reminded students that silence, fear, or denial around race and cultural difference are widespread but insufficient responses; progress requires the courage to confront how socialization and upbringing shape one’s own perspectives.  

The slides emphasized that race is not biologically determined. Rather, racial categories have been created and redefined through political struggles, economic conditions, and legal systems. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project demonstrated the scientific evidence against biological race and the power of “common sense” racial thinking. Ian Haney López’s analysis illustrated that law not only coerces—literally shaping who counts as which race through immigration, naturalization, and marriage laws—but also acts as ideology by making racial divisions appear natural. Historical court cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans showed how racial meanings were legally and inconsistently constructed. These cases, along with naturalization standards and bans on interracial marriage (repealed in 1967), revealed how citizenship and belonging were racially bounded. James Baldwin’s insight that whiteness itself was created through coercion underscored the social invention of racial categories. Shifting census classifications and evolving racial and ethnic terms reflected how language mirrors and records these social transformations.  

The second half turned to culture, drawing on Nieto’s definition of culture as the changing set of values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews shared and reshaped by groups. Culture is learned, dynamic, and hybrid—never reducible to celebrations or foods—and everyone possesses it. Cultural experience is multifaceted and influenced by family, peers, class, geography, gender, religion, and media. The lecture outlined how schools have their own distinct cultures shaped by routines, grouping practices, resources, and expectations. Many U.S. schools assume ability is fixed and measurable, reinforcing hierarchies that shape how students view intelligence and achievement. Understanding both race and culture as constructed exposes how educational systems can reproduce or challenge inequities. (380 words)
