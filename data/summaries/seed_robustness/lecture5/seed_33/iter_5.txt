The lecture examined how race and culture are social constructions that organize identity, privilege, and schooling. It opened with reflection on labeling in education—how teachers or administrators often categorize students, influencing how they view their intelligence, motivation, and potential. Drawing on Sonia Nieto, the class discussed how society classifies people by visible and invisible traits such as race, ethnicity, gender, class, religion, and culture, producing stereotypes that privilege some groups and harm others. Silence or denial around race was framed as fear-driven avoidance; courage is needed to confront how upbringing and social context shape personal views of difference.  

Race was explained as socially and historically constructed rather than biological. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated scientific evidence against biological ideas of race, showing how racial categories become “common sense.” Ian Haney López’s work revealed two ways law creates and maintains race. *Law as coercion* refers to legal rules that physically shape populations—through immigration restrictions, marriage laws, and definitions of citizenship that decided who could be “white.” *Law as ideology* describes how legal systems naturalize race, persuading people that inequality is inevitable. Court cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans illustrated these shifting definitions, and Baldwin’s remark that no one was white before coming to America highlighted how whiteness was made through coercion. Changing U.S. Census categories showed how government classification continues to shape identity, particularly for groups such as Middle Eastern and North African Americans.  

Language changes also trace social power, as terms from “Negro” to “Latinx” or “Asian American” mirror political and cultural shifts. The second half focused on culture as defined by Nieto: the evolving values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews created and transformed by groups. Culture is learned, dynamic, and hybrid; people actively select and reject its elements. Influences include family, peers, gender, class, religion, geography, media, and schooling. Schools themselves have distinct cultures shaped by physical environments, routines, and beliefs about ability. U.S. schooling often assumes ability is fixed and measurable, informing definitions of “smart” or “bad” students. Such assumptions influence participation, learning, and students’ sense of potential. Seeing race, law, language, and school culture as interconnected constructions highlights how education can reproduce or challenge inequality.
