This lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity, law, and schooling. It began with reflection on how educators’ labels can affect students’ self‑perception, motivation, and sense of capability—urging participants to ask whether such labels express the whole person or instead distort it. Drawing on Sonia Nieto, the slides showed that society classifies people through visible and invisible traits such as race, ethnicity, gender, social class, religion, and culture, attaching exaggerated or stereotypical meanings that privilege some groups while harming others. The class discussed how silence, fear, and denial often surround conversations about race and cultural difference, and that meaningful progress requires courage—the willingness to examine how one’s upbringing and social context have shaped personal views and biases.  

The lecture emphasized that race is not biologically determined but socially made through historical, political, and economic forces. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated scientific evidence dismantling biological myths and how racial thinking becomes “common sense.” Ian Haney López’s scholarship showed that law creates and enforces racial meaning: through naturalization, immigration, and marriage laws, legal decisions have determined who could belong and even who could marry, literally shaping physical and social boundaries. Later cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans revealed how courts inconsistently defined whiteness and citizenship. James Baldwin’s assertion that whiteness was produced in America through “generations of coercion” highlighted how racial ideologies evolved alongside legal privilege. Changing census categories and shifting racial or ethnic terms, from “Negro” to “African American” or “Latino/a” to “Latinx,” demonstrated how language records and reflects social transformation and contested power.  

The second half turned to culture. Nieto defines culture as the evolving values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews created and reshaped by groups—learned, not inherited; dynamic, hybrid, and multifaceted. Everyone has culture, influenced by family, peers, class, gender, geography, religion, and media. Schools themselves have distinct cultures shaped by structures, routines, and access to programs that convey underlying beliefs about intelligence and ability. Many U.S. schools assume that ability is fixed and quantifiable and that learning follows a rigid hierarchy. Such assumptions influence definitions of “smart” or “bad” students and affect participation, learning, and achievement. Recognizing both race and culture as constructed helps reveal how schooling can either reenforce or challenge social inequities. (382 words)
