The lecture explored how race and culture are social constructions that shape identity, belonging, and schooling. It opened with personal reflection on labeling in education—how teachers, counselors, or administrators categorize students in ways that affect how they see their intelligence, motivation, and potential. Drawing on Sonia Nieto, the class examined how society classifies people through visible and invisible traits such as race, ethnicity, gender, class, religion, and culture, creating stereotypes that privilege some groups and harm others. Silence or denial around discussions of race was described as fear-based avoidance; confronting these issues requires courage and self-reflection about how one’s upbringing influences views of difference.  

Race was presented as a social rather than biological category. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project and historical evidence show that racial categories have changed with political and economic contexts, becoming “common sense” even without explicit teaching. Ian Haney López’s framework showed two ways law constructs race. *Law as coercion* refers to how legal rules—such as immigration and naturalization restrictions or bans on interracial marriage—physically shaped populations and determined who counted as “white.” *Law as ideology* explains how legal systems make these distinctions appear natural, legitimating racial inequality through supposedly neutral policies. Court cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans illustrated shifting and inconsistent definitions of race and citizenship. James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” summarized how whiteness itself was produced through coercive and legal processes. Changing U.S. Census categories further demonstrated how government classifications continue to define racial identity.  

Language change was used to reveal broader social transformations, as evolving terms like “Negro,” “Black,” “Latino/a,” “Latinx,” or “Asian American” mirror political and cultural shifts. The second half of the lecture turned to culture, drawing on Nieto’s view of it as a learned, dynamic, and hybrid process through which people create, share, and transform values, traditions, and worldviews. Because culture is continually changing, even institutions such as schools embody distinct but evolving cultures—seen in school routines, tracking, resources, and beliefs about intelligence. U.S. schooling often assumes ability is fixed and measurable, shaping ideas of who is “smart” or “bad.” Understanding both race and school culture as dynamic constructions enables reflection on how educational environments influence students’ experiences, participation, and sense of possibility.
