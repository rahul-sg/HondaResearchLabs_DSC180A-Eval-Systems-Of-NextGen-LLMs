This lecture explored how race and culture are socially constructed systems that organize identity, privilege, and schooling. It opened with reflection on labeling in education—how teachers and administrators often categorize students in ways that shape how they see their intelligence, motivation, and potential. Drawing on Sonia Nieto, the slides explained that society classifies people through visible and invisible traits such as race, ethnicity, gender, class, religion, and culture, creating exaggerated stereotypes that advantage some groups while harming others. A central theme was that silence, fear, and denial about race are cultural habits of avoidance, not simple absence of talk. The lecture emphasized that change requires courage—to confront how social contexts and personal upbringings shape individuals’ understandings of race and difference.  

Race was presented as socially and historically constructed, not biologically determined. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project demonstrated scientific evidence debunking biological myths and showed how racial ideas become “common sense.” Ian Haney López’s scholarship explained that law both enforces and legitimates race: as *coercion*, legal rules have literally shaped who counted as white and who could marry or immigrate; as *ideology*, law persuades people to see racial inequality as natural. Cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans revealed the inconsistent legal construction of whiteness and citizenship, while Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” highlighted whiteness as a product of coercion. Changing census categories illustrated how government classification shapes self-identification—particularly for Middle Eastern and North African groups who find current categories inadequate.  

Language change further showed that terms like “Negro,” “African American,” “Hispanic,” “Latino/a,” and “Latinx” trace shifting power relations and social transformations. The second half examined culture more directly. Nieto defines culture as the evolving values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews created and reshaped by groups—a learned, dynamic, hybrid process that everyone possesses. Influences include family, peers, class, gender, geography, religion, media, and schooling. Schools themselves have distinct cultural patterns shaped by their physical environments, routines, and beliefs about ability. Many U.S. schools assume ability is fixed and measurable, producing definitions of “smart” and “bad” students that reinforce hierarchy. Understanding race and culture as ongoing social constructions reveals how educational structures can reproduce or challenge inequality. (392 words)
