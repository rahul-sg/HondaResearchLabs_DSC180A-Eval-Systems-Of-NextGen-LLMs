The Week 1 lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces financial accounting as the starting point for understanding how businesses record and communicate their economic activities. Accounting is described as the **language of business**, telling a company’s financial story—its successes, challenges, and risks—through systematically recorded data. The lecture emphasizes two major functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records a company’s economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which conveys that information through financial statements to external decision makers.  

Students are expected to develop two key abilities: to understand how financial statements are created so they can read, interpret, and use them, and to apply accounting information to business decision contexts. The class differentiates **financial accounting**, which serves external users, from **managerial accounting**, which supports internal budgeting and cost decisions, and from **financial statement analysis**, which focuses on valuing companies rather than creating the reports themselves.  

The lecture outlines who uses accounting information and for what purposes. **Investors** assess performance and financial condition; **creditors** gauge repayment ability; **suppliers** set credit terms; **management** monitors performance and compensation; **boards of directors** oversee management on behalf of shareholders; and other users include **employees**, **customers**, **analysts**, and **regulators** seeking insight into financial health and reliability.  

Financial accounting organizes information around three types of activities. **Financing activities** involve transactions with investors and creditors such as raising funds through stock or loans. **Investing activities** relate to acquiring or disposing of long‑term resources like land, equipment, or inventory. **Operating activities** capture day‑to‑day processes such as production, marketing, and sales. The results of these activities appear in four main **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows.  

Under the **Financial Accounting Framework**, all reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, established through the **FASB** under authority granted by the **SEC**, with global counterparts following **IFRS** issued by the **IASB**. Independent **auditors** provide assurance that financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements, issuing opinions that add credibility to reported results.  

The lecture concludes with the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific date and must satisfy the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Using *Klein, Inc.* examples, students see how transactions—from issuing stock and borrowing funds to purchasing equipment, paying rent, and buying supplies—each alter assets, liabilities, or equity while keeping the equation in balance.
