The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces accounting as the **language of business**, a system for recording and communicating an organization’s financial story—its successes, failures, opportunities, challenges, and risks. Accounting serves two key functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which organizes that information into financial statements that inform the decisions of various stakeholders.

The course emphasizes using accounting information to **read, understand, and apply financial statements to make business decisions**. It distinguishes **financial accounting** from related fields: *managerial accounting* (internal decision‑making such as budgeting and cost analysis) and *financial statement analysis* (evaluating firm value using reported statements).

Users of financial information include investors assessing management performance, creditors and suppliers evaluating lending or credit terms, management and boards monitoring strategy and compensation, and broader groups—employees, customers, analysts, regulators, and competitors—who rely on credible data to gauge organizational performance and reliability.

Financial accounting reflects three streams of business activity: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of resources such as buildings or equipment), and **operations** (daily production, marketing, and sales). These transactions produce data communicated in four primary **financial statements**—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—issued in standard public‑company filings: the annual **10‑K** (audited), quarterly **10‑Q** (unaudited), and event‑driven **8‑K**.

Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** to ensure consistency and comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees public reporting and delegates standard setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**; internationally, standards are issued under **IFRS** by the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)**. Independent **auditors** enhance trust by verifying whether statements conform to GAAP and are free from material misstatements. Their opinions may be *unqualified*, *unqualified with explanatory emphasis*, *qualified* (scope or GAAP issue), or *adverse* (misleading statements).

Finally, the lecture applies these principles through the **balance sheet**, which reports financial position at a point in time: **assets** (resources), **liabilities** (obligations), and **stockholders’ equity** (residual owners’ claims). Guided by the **accounting equation—Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity—**each transaction has a dual effect that maintains balance. Step‑by‑step examples of financing, investing, and operating decisions show how recording common transactions builds a coherent snapshot of a firm’s financial foundation.
