The first week of *Principles of Accounting* introduces financial accounting as the language of business—the system that records, measures, and communicates an organization’s financial story. Accounting captures key aspects of a firm’s performance, risks, and resources in measurable form to inform business decisions. It serves two core purposes: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which conveys summarized information to external users through **financial statements**.

Financial accounting focuses on three fundamental categories of activity. **Financing activities** involve obtaining resources from investors and creditors. **Investing activities** concern acquiring or disposing of assets—like buildings, equipment, or inventory—needed to operate. **Operating activities** reflect day‑to‑day transactions related to producing and selling goods and services. The outcomes of these activities are communicated through four primary financial statements: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**.

Accounting information supports a wide range of users. **Investors** and **creditors** evaluate performance, repayment ability, and risk. **Suppliers** use financial data to set credit terms and assess long‑term relationships. **Management** and the **board of directors** rely on it to monitor performance and guide strategy. **Employees** and **customers** gauge financial health and reliability, while **analysts**, **regulators**, and **other stakeholders** use it for oversight and comparison.

Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**—rules established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under authority delegated by the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS**. Public companies must have their financial statements examined by **independent auditors**, who evaluate compliance with GAAP and the presence of material misstatements. Their opinions—**unqualified**, **unqualified with emphasis**, **qualified**, and **adverse**—add credibility to reported information and guide decision‑makers.

The lecture emphasizes the **balance sheet** as a snapshot of financial position based on the equation **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. **Assets** are resources providing future benefit; **liabilities** represent obligations to creditors; and **equity** reflects owners’ residual claims. Examples such as issuing stock, borrowing funds, purchasing equipment, paying rent in advance, and buying supplies demonstrate that every transaction affects at least two accounts but always keeps the equation balanced. This framework establishes how accounting structures economic information to support transparent and informed decision‑making.
