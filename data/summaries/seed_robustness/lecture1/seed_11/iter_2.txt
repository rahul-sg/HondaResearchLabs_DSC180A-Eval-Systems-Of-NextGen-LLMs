The Week 1 lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the purpose and framework of financial accounting as the foundation for understanding business performance. Accounting is presented as the “language of business,” used to tell a company’s financial story—its successes, risks, and challenges—through recorded economic data. The lecture highlights two main functions: **recordkeeping**, which involves identifying, measuring, and recording a company’s economic events, and **communication**, which conveys that information to external decision makers through financial statements.  

Users of accounting information include **investors**, who assess management performance and financial condition; **creditors** and **suppliers**, who evaluate repayment ability and reliability; **management** and **boards of directors**, who use results to guide operations and strategic decisions; and other groups such as **employees**, **customers**, **analysts**, **regulators**, and **competitors**.  

Financial accounting measures three core types of business activity. **Financing activities** involve transactions with investors and creditors, such as obtaining loans or issuing stock. **Investing activities** concern acquiring and disposing of the resources needed to produce and sell goods or services. **Operating activities** relate to daily functions like production, marketing, and sales. The outcomes of these activities are reported in four key financial statements: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**.  

Financial reporting is governed by **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, which ensure consistency and comparability in what is reported and how. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees public reporting and delegates standard‑setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**; global guidance comes from the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** under **IFRS**. Independent **auditors**, required for public companies, examine financial statements for compliance with GAAP and issue opinions—unqualified, qualified, adverse, or explanatory—that enhance credibility for decision makers.  

A central focus of the lecture is the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific point in time and must satisfy the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets are resources with future benefits; liabilities are obligations to creditors; and equity represents owners’ residual claims. Step‑by‑step examples using *Klein, Inc.* demonstrate how transactions such as issuing stock, borrowing funds, purchasing equipment, paying rent, and buying supplies all influence the equation yet keep it balanced. This framework illustrates how financial accounting records and communicates a business’s economic story, forming the basis for later analysis and decision‑making.
