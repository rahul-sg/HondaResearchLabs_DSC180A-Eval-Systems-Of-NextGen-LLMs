The opening week of **Principles of Accounting** establishes accounting as the *language of business*—a system for recording and communicating an organization’s financial story of success, failure, risk, and opportunity. Accounting has two main roles: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys the results to external users through standardized financial statements.

Financial information serves diverse users. **Investors** and **creditors** evaluate performance and creditworthiness, **suppliers** assess reliability before extending credit, **management** and **boards of directors** monitor operations and strategy, while **employees**, **customers**, regulators, and analysts consider profitability, stability, or compliance. These perspectives highlight accounting’s role in supporting business decisions across stakeholders.

The lecture distinguishes **financial accounting** from related areas: *managerial accounting* focuses on internal cost and budget decisions, and *financial statement analysis* centers on valuing firms using existing reports. Financial accounting instead provides the underlying measurement and reporting framework governed by formal standards.

It measures and reports three core business activities: **financing** (transactions with owners and lenders), **investing** (acquiring or disposing of productive assets), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production, marketing, and sales transactions). Their results are communicated through four statements—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—prepared in accordance with **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**. GAAP rules are established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under **SEC** authority, while the **IASB** issues international equivalents (IFRS).

To enhance reliability, companies engage **independent auditors** to express professional opinions on whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements. Audit outcomes include *unqualified* (clean), *unqualified with emphasis*, *qualified*, or *adverse* opinions—with the first signaling full compliance and the last indicating serious misrepresentation. Such assurance adds credibility for decision makers.

The lecture’s central example, *Klein, Inc.*, demonstrates the logic of the **balance sheet** and the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Issuing stock and obtaining loans increase both resources and claims; investing in equipment, paying prepaid rent, or buying supplies adjust asset and liability balances yet keep the equation intact. These step‑by‑step cases connect everyday transactions to the structure of financial statements and form the conceptual bridge for all later accounting topics.
