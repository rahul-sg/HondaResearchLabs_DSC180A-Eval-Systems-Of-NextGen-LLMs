The Week 1 lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the core purpose, structure, and foundational concepts of **financial accounting**. Accounting is presented as the **language of business**, used to tell a company’s economic story through measurable data that convey its successes, challenges, and financial position. It serves two key functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records transactions in monetary terms, and **communication**, which reports that information to external decision‑makers through standardized financial statements.  

Accounting information supports a broad audience. **Investors** and **creditors** assess management performance and a company’s ability to meet obligations, while **suppliers**, **management**, and **boards of directors** use reports for evaluating operations, strategy, and financial policy. Additional users include **employees** gauging job security, **customers** judging reliability, and other stakeholders such as analysts, regulators, and competitors.  

The lecture explains that financial accounting measures and reports three types of business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders), **investing** (acquiring or disposing of resources for operations), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production, marketing, and sales). These activities are communicated through four primary **financial statements**—the **Balance Sheet**, **Income Statement**, **Statement of Stockholders’ Equity**, and **Statement of Cash Flows**—which appear in reports such as the annual **10‑K**, quarterly **10‑Q**, and event‑driven **8‑K** filings.  

All reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** to ensure comparability and consistency. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** oversees public company reporting and delegates standard‑setting authority to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS**. Independent **auditors**, required for publicly traded firms, examine statements for adherence to GAAP and issue opinions—unqualified, qualified, or adverse—on their accuracy.  

The session concludes with the **Balance Sheet**, which presents a company’s financial position at a particular date. It must always satisfy the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. **Assets** are resources providing future economic benefit, **liabilities** are obligations owed to creditors, and **equity** represents owners’ residual claims. Through the *Klein Inc.* examples—raising capital from investors, borrowing from a bank, acquiring equipment, prepaying rent, and purchasing supplies on account—students see how each transaction affects at least two accounts yet keeps the equation balanced.  

This lecture establishes the conceptual and mechanical framework of financial accounting that underpins future topics such as double‑entry recording, the accounting cycle, and financial statement preparation.
