The opening lecture in **Principles of Accounting** introduces accounting as the *language of business*—a system for recording and communicating an organization’s financial story of performance, risk, and opportunity. Accounting fulfills two essential roles: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to external users through standardized financial statements.

**Financial accounting** differs from *managerial accounting* (internal cost and budgeting decisions) and *financial statement analysis* (valuation of firms). Its purpose is to generate reliable financial reports that inform decisions across stakeholders. These users include **investors** assessing management performance, **creditors** evaluating repayment ability, **suppliers** considering credit terms, **managers** and **boards of directors** reviewing results and strategy, as well as **employees**, **customers**, **regulators**, and **analysts** judging financial health or compliance.  

Financial accounting measures three categories of business activity: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders), **investing** (acquiring or disposing of assets needed to operate), and **operating** (production, marketing, and selling). Their results appear in four core statements—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—each reflecting how resources are obtained and used.

Preparation of these statements follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**—rules ensuring consistency and comparability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** holds legal authority over reporting standards for public companies, *delegates* standard setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**, yet *retains the power to mandate changes* if necessary. Internationally, the **IASB** issues **IFRS** standards serving similar functions.

To provide credibility, companies engage **independent auditors** who assess whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements. Auditors may issue four opinions: *unqualified* (clean), *unqualified with emphasis*, *qualified*, or *adverse*—each shaping how decision makers trust the reports.

The **balance sheet** and the **accounting equation** (**Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**) form the foundation of these reports. The *Klein, Inc.* example shows how issuing stock, borrowing funds, purchasing equipment, paying rent in advance, or buying supplies all affect assets, liabilities, and equity but keep the equation balanced. This process illustrates how every transaction has a dual effect and how systematic recording links everyday events to the structure of financial statements—the conceptual base for all further accounting topics.
