The first week of **Principles of Accounting** introduces the scope and purpose of financial accounting and establishes the conceptual foundation for future topics. Accounting is presented as the *language of business*, a system that tells a company’s financial story through its recorded activities, successes, and risks. It serves two key functions: **recordkeeping**, involving identifying, measuring, and recording economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys the resulting information to external decision makers through structured reports known as financial statements.

Users of accounting information include **investors**, **creditors**, **suppliers**, **management**, and **boards of directors**, as well as **employees**, **customers**, and other stakeholders such as analysts and regulators. Investors and creditors evaluate performance and repayment ability, suppliers assess reliability, management and boards monitor operations, and employees or customers gauge organizational stability and service reliability.

The lecture distinguishes **financial accounting** from other branches: managerial accounting focuses on internal decision-making, while financial statement analysis applies existing financial reports to company valuation. Financial accounting, by contrast, centers on systematically recording and reporting activities according to established standards.

Financial accounting measures three main business activities: **financing** (interactions with owners and lenders), **investing** (acquiring productive resources), and **operating** (transactions related to providing goods and services). The outcomes are communicated through four statements—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—which together describe a firm’s financial position and performance. These reports follow **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, issued by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under the oversight of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**; global standards are guided by the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** through **IFRS**. Independent **auditors** review financial statements for compliance with GAAP and issue an opinion that enhances the credibility of reported information.

The lecture’s main focus is the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific point in time according to the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets are resources providing future economic benefit; liabilities represent amounts owed to creditors; and equity reflects owners’ residual claims. Step‑by‑step examples using *Klein, Inc.* show how financing through stock issues and loans, investing in equipment, prepaying rent, and purchasing supplies all alter component accounts yet keep the equation in balance. This foundation links everyday business transactions to the structure of financial statements and introduces the analytical thinking that underlies all accounting practice.
