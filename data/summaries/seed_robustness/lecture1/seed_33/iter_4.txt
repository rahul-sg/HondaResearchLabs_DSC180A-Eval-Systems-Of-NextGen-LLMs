The Week 1 lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the purpose, structure, and core framework of **financial accounting**. Accounting is described as the **language of business**, telling a company’s financial story through information about its operations, performance, and condition. It fulfills two main functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys that information to decision‑makers through standardized financial statements.  

Accounting information serves diverse users. **Investors** and **creditors** evaluate profitability, management performance, and repayment ability; **suppliers** use it to set credit terms; **management** and **boards of directors** rely on it to guide strategy and governance. Other users include **employees** assessing job stability, **customers** gauging reliability, and **financial analysts**, **regulators**, or **competitors** reviewing performance.  

Financial accounting measures and reports three business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of productive resources), and **operating** (production, marketing, and sales). These are communicated through four key **financial statements**—the **Balance Sheet**, **Income Statement**, **Statement of Stockholders’ Equity**, and **Statement of Cash Flows**—issued in reports such as the **10‑K** (annual, audited), **10‑Q** (quarterly, unaudited), and **8‑K** (for major events).  

All reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** to ensure comparability and reliability. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** regulates U.S. public company reporting and delegates standard‑setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**, while the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS** for global use. **Auditors**, required for publicly traded companies, act as independent reviewers who assess whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements. They issue one of four opinions: **unqualified**, **unqualified with explanatory or emphasis paragraph**, **qualified**, or **adverse**, thereby lending credibility to reports used by external stakeholders.  

The lecture concludes with the **Balance Sheet**, showing a firm’s financial position at a specific date under the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. **Assets** are resources with future benefit, **liabilities** are obligations to creditors, and **equity** represents owners’ residual claims. Through the *Klein Inc.* examples—raising capital, obtaining a bank loan, acquiring equipment, prepaying rent, and purchasing supplies on account—students see how each transaction affects multiple accounts but keeps the equation balanced.  

This session establishes the conceptual and mechanical foundation of financial accounting that supports later topics such as double‑entry recording and the accounting cycle.
