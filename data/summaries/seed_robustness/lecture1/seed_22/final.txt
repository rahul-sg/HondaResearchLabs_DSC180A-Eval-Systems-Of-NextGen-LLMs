The Week 1 lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the purpose and framework of financial accounting as the foundation for understanding business decisions. The course aims to help students comprehend how financial statements are created so they can read, interpret, and use them effectively. Accounting is presented as the “language of business,” telling a company’s financial story through its recorded economic activities—highlighting successes, failures, risks, and opportunities—and providing a basis for informed decision‑making across all fields.  

Accounting serves two main functions: **recordkeeping** and **communication**. Recordkeeping involves identifying, measuring, and recording economic events in value terms. Communication conveys this information through financial statements to external users such as investors, creditors, suppliers, management, boards of directors, employees, customers, analysts, regulators, and others. These users rely on accounting to assess financial condition, performance, and prospects, whether for investment, lending, or operational decisions.  

Financial accounting measures three fundamental business activities. **Financing activities** involve transactions with investors and creditors, such as issuing shares or obtaining loans. **Investing activities** include acquiring or disposing of long‑term and short‑term resources like buildings, inventory, and equipment. **Operating activities** comprise the production, promotion, and sale of goods and services. The results of these processes are summarized in four key financial statements: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. Together they form a company’s periodic reports and communicate performance to external parties.  

Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**—rules and procedures designed for consistent, comparable reporting—established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under the oversight of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**. Internationally, standards are set by the **IASB** under IFRS. Independent **auditors** strengthen credibility by evaluating whether a company’s financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements, issuing one of four types of opinions.  

Emphasis is placed on the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific point in time and must satisfy the accounting equation: **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity.** Assets are resources with future benefits; liabilities are obligations to creditors; equity represents owners’ residual claims. Examples of transactions—such as issuing stock for capital, borrowing from a bank, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, or buying supplies on credit—illustrate how each affects the accounting equation while keeping it balanced.  

This lecture establishes the conceptual link between business activity, financial reporting, and decision‑making, forming the first step toward understanding and using accounting information effectively.
