The opening week of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the foundations of financial accounting—its purpose, key users, and the framework underlying the financial reporting process. Accounting is presented as the “language of business” that tells a company’s financial story through recorded information about its successes, risks, and opportunities. It serves two primary functions: **recordkeeping**, which involves identifying, measuring, and recording economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which provides this information to decision makers through formal financial statements.

Users of accounting information include investors assessing performance and financial position, creditors and suppliers evaluating repayment ability and reliability, management and boards overseeing operations and strategy, and others such as employees, customers, analysts, regulators, and competitors interested in a firm’s financial health. Financial accounting captures three core business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and creditors), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of productive resources), and **operating** (activities related to producing and selling goods and services). The results of these activities are communicated through four key financial statements—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—which together describe a company’s financial position and performance.

Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, ensuring comparability across firms. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** holds authority over accounting and reporting standards for publicly traded companies, delegating standard setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. The **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues the corresponding **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS)** for global use. Public companies must file periodic reports: the **10‑K** (annual audited financials with detailed disclosures), **10‑Q** (quarterly unaudited reports), and **8‑K** (current reports for significant events).

Independent **auditors**, required for public firms, evaluate whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements. Their opinions—unqualified, unqualified with emphasis, qualified, or adverse—add credibility for decision makers.

The lecture highlights the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific date based on the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets represent resources with future economic benefit, liabilities reflect obligations to creditors, and equity shows owners’ residual claims. Through examples such as issuing stock, borrowing funds, purchasing equipment, paying rent, and buying supplies, students see how each transaction has a dual effect yet always keeps the equation in balance—providing the conceptual foundation for understanding financial health and business decision-making.
