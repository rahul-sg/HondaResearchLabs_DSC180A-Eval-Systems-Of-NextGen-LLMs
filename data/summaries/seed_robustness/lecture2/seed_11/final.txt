This lecture revisits the financial accounting framework from Week 1 and develops the mechanics of **double‑entry recordkeeping**. It begins by noting that economic activity is complex, requiring judgment and critical thinking. The four primary financial statements—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—work together to communicate how a company’s resources, obligations, and results evolve. The balance sheet reports assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time, while the income, equity, and cash‑flow statements describe performance across a fiscal period. The lecture highlights their interconnection: net income from the income statement increases stockholders’ equity, the equity statement reflects this change, and the cash‑flow statement explains variations in cash between consecutive balance‑sheet dates.  

The **accounting equation** expands from its basic form (Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity) to identify components of equity—**contributed capital** and **retained earnings**—and to show temporal flow:  
Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings (beginning + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends).  
This formulation connects revenues and expenses to net income, which then alters retained earnings and total equity.  

**Double‑entry accounting** ensures each economic event affects at least two accounts so that the equation remains balanced. A **debit** increases assets and decreases liabilities or equity, while a **credit** does the opposite. Every transaction must contain at least one debit and one credit, no negative numbers are used, and total debits must equal total credits. Through numerical examples—borrowing $100 000 from a bank, purchasing $60 000 of equipment for cash, buying $15 000 of inventory on account, paying $8 000 to a supplier, prepaying $600 for insurance, and issuing 10 000 shares for $100 000—students trace how these entries flow through T‑accounts. Each T‑account follows the relationship **BB + increases − decreases = EB**, illustrating how ending balances carry forward as beginning balances in the next period.  

The lecture then introduces **journalization**, demonstrating how transactions are first analyzed, then recorded as journal entries (debits listed first, credits indented), and finally **posted to the ledger** as T‑accounts to compute balances. The mnemonic **DEALOR** (Dividends, Expenses, and Assets → debits; Liabilities, Owners’ equity, and Revenue → credits) summarizes account behavior. Applying “**Debits = Credits**” throughout preserves the accounting equation’s integrity and ties all financial statements within a coherent reporting framework.  (≈395 words)
