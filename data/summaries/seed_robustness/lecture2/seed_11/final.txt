This lecture builds on the financial accounting framework by showing how the four financial statements connect and how double-entry recording preserves the equality of Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity. Accounting is presented as a system for interpreting complex economic activity through judgment and structured communication. The Balance Sheet reports assets, liabilities, and equity at a specific point in time, while the Income Statement, Statement of Stockholders’ Equity, and Statement of Cash Flows cover activity over a fiscal period. Together, they reveal changes between beginning and ending balance sheets: net income increases equity, net loss reduces it, and revenues and expenses drive these changes through retained earnings.

The accounting equation is expanded to show that Stockholders’ Equity includes contributed capital and retained earnings. Retained earnings evolve across periods as: beginning balance + net income – dividends, or equivalently, beginning retained earnings + revenues – expenses – dividends = ending retained earnings. This structure clarifies how profitability and distributions affect financial position over time.

The lecture then introduces the double-entry framework used to record transactions. Each economic event has two sides—a give and a take—and affects at least two accounts within the accounting equation. Debits and credits indicate direction: debits increase assets (and decrease liabilities or equity), while credits increase liabilities and equity (and decrease assets). All transactions must include at least one debit and one credit, with debits equaling credits and no negative numbers used. Sequential examples—borrowing funds, purchasing equipment or inventory, paying suppliers, prepaying insurance, and issuing stock—illustrate how entries update T‑accounts. The general form “Beginning Balance + increases – decreases = Ending Balance” determines each account’s ending value, which appears on the balance sheet and becomes the next period’s beginning balance, maintaining continuity between cycles.

Slides introduce the “Super T‑account,” aligning all account categories—Assets, Liabilities, Contributed Capital, Retained Earnings, Revenues, Expenses, and Dividends—on one framework to display their debit (Dr) and credit (Cr) orientations. The mnemonic DEALOR (Dividends, Expenses, Assets = normal Debit; Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, Revenues = normal Credit) summarizes these relationships. Later slides show how to record journal entries: list debit accounts and amounts first, indent and list credit accounts second, and ensure total debits equal total credits. The lecture concludes with the accounting process: analyze transactions, record them in journals, post to T‑accounts, and confirm that Debits = Credits maintains the fundamental equation A = L + SE.
