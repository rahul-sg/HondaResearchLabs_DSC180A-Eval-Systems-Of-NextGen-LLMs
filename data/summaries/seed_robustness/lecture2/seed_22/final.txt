This lecture deepens students’ understanding of how financial statements connect and introduces, in detail, the mechanics of the double‑entry system. It begins by stressing that accounting represents complex economic activity and requires judgment and critical thinking. Financial statements—balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—are the main communication tools describing a company’s position and performance. The balance sheet reports resources and obligations at a point in time, while the others cover a fiscal period. These statements are interlinked: the income statement shows profit or loss for the year, which adjusts retained earnings on the statement of stockholders’ equity; together with the statement of cash flows, these explain the change between two balance sheet dates. Revenues and expenses affect stockholders’ equity, so net income increases equity and a net loss decreases it.

Next, the accounting equation is expanded from its base form, Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity, to show how equity changes. Equity includes contributed capital and retained earnings, with retained earnings updated by revenues, expenses, and dividends. The extended equation—Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Beginning Retained Earnings + Revenues – Expenses – Dividends—illustrates how operating results and distributions alter the firm’s financial position across periods.

The lecture then develops the double‑entry framework: every economic event has two sides, a give and a take, and must be recorded in at least two accounts so that debits always equal credits. Debits increase assets and decrease liabilities or equity; credits do the opposite. No negative numbers are used—each transaction includes at least one debit and one credit, and the totals must balance. Worked examples show this process: borrowing funds raises both cash and notes payable; purchasing equipment or inventory either swaps or adds assets; paying suppliers or insurance reduces cash or liabilities; and issuing shares raises both cash and common stock. Using T‑accounts, students compute ending balances that also serve as beginning balances for the next period, demonstrating continuity.

The lecture concludes by formalizing the recording sequence: analyze transactions, journalize them with the debit listed first (credit lines indented), and post amounts to ledger accounts. The “DEALOR” rule—Dividends, Expenses, Assets (debit); Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, Revenue (credit)—summarizes how T‑accounts behave. Throughout, the core principle “debits = credits” preserves the accounting equation and ensures internal consistency across all financial statements.
