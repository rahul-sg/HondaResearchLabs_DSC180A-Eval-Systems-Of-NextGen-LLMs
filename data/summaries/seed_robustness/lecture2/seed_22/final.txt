This lecture deepens the foundation of financial accounting by linking the conceptual framework introduced in Week 1 to the practical mechanics of double‑entry recording. It begins by emphasizing that economic activity is inherently complex, requiring **judgment and critical thinking** when interpreting and classifying transactions. Accounting is presented as both an analytical and procedural discipline—one that transforms economic events into structured financial information.  

The first part revisits the **four financial statements** as the core reporting tools used to communicate a company’s financial position and performance. The **balance sheet** captures resources and obligations at a specific point in time, while the **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows** describe changes over an accounting period. The lecture stresses their **interdependence**: profit or loss from the income statement alters retained earnings within equity; the statement of cash flows explains cash movements between balance‑sheet dates; and the equity statement summarizes owners’ contributions and distributions. Revenues raise and expenses reduce stockholders’ equity, so net income increases retained earnings, and net loss decreases it.  

Building on these relationships, the lecture expands the **accounting equation**—Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity—by expressing equity as contributed capital and retained earnings that evolve through revenues, expenses, and dividends. This expanded equation provides the logical base for **double‑entry accounting**, where every event affects at least two accounts and **must keep the equation in balance**. Debits increase assets and decrease liabilities or equity; credits do the opposite.  

To illustrate these principles, a sequence of examples applies them numerically: borrowing **$100,000** from a bank, purchasing **$60,000** of equipment with cash, buying **$15,000** of inventory on account, paying **$8,000** of that payable, prepaying **$600** for insurance, and issuing **10,000 shares for $100,000**. Each transaction is shown through T‑accounts in which debits equal credits, reinforcing how balance is maintained. The resulting ending balances (for example, cash and trade payable) become the beginning balances for the next period, linking accounting cycles over time.  

Finally, the lecture summarizes the **recording process**: (1) analyze each transaction, (2) record debits and credits as journal entries, and (3) post them to the ledger to determine account balances. The consistent rule that **Debits = Credits** upholds the accounting equation and ensures the accuracy and integrity of all financial statements.
