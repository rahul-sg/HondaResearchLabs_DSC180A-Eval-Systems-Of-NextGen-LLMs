This lecture extends Week 1’s foundation by explaining how financial statements interconnect and how economic events are captured through double‑entry accounting. It first stresses that accounting translates complex economic activity into structured financial information that requires judgment and critical thinking. The four core financial statements—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—serve as complementary reports of a firm’s financial communication. The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time, while the income statement, equity statement, and cash flow statement summarize results over a fiscal period and explain changes between two balance‑sheet dates. Net income increases stockholders’ equity, and net losses reduce it, since revenues raise and expenses lower retained earnings.  

The lecture then rebuilds the accounting equation in stages to show how equity evolves. Starting with Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity, equity is divided into contributed capital and retained earnings. Retained earnings expand as the beginning balance plus net income for the period minus dividends, which is then detailed further as beginning retained earnings + revenues – expenses – dividends. This expanded form connects operational performance with financial position and underlies all record‑keeping steps.  

Next, double‑entry mechanics are introduced: every economic event has two sides—a give and a take—and must affect at least one account within assets, liabilities, or equity. Each transaction involves at least one debit and one credit, and **no negative numbers are allowed**; instead, debits and credits track increases or decreases. Debits increase assets and decrease liabilities and equity, while credits have the opposite effect. The equality of debits and credits maintains the accounting equation balance.  

Examples demonstrate these rules through transactions such as borrowing from a bank, purchasing equipment and inventory, paying suppliers, prepaying insurance, and issuing stock. Ending balances from T‑accounts form the beginning balances of the next period, reinforcing how the accounting cycle continues. The D‑E‑A‑L‑O‑R aid (Dividends, Expenses, Assets, Liabilities, Owners’ equity, Revenue) helps recall normal debit and credit orientations.  

Finally, the lecture outlines the recording sequence: analyze transactions, prepare journal entries, and post them to T‑accounts (the ledger). These steps ensure that debits equal credits for every transaction, preserving accuracy and the integrity of financial statements.
