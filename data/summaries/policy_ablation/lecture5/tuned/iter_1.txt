The lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity, law, and education in the United States. It began with Sonia Nieto’s concept of “constructing others,” explaining how society classifies people by visible and invisible traits, attaches stereotypes to those differences, and uses them to justify unequal policies and practices. Students were asked to reflect on how labeling by teachers or counselors might have influenced their sense of ability and identity, and the class discussed how silence and fear around conversations about race often reinforce misunderstanding rather than address it. Courage, the slides emphasized, is needed to confront how social attitudes and personal upbringings shape our views on race and cultural difference.  

Scientific and historical evidence reaffirmed that race is not biologically real but a social invention developed through economic, political, and legal struggles. The American Anthropological Association’s *RACE: Are We So Different?* project was introduced as evidence challenging myths of inherent racial difference. Legal structures were highlighted as key to how racial meaning has been produced. Ian Haney López’s framework distinguished between **law as coercion**—legal rules that physically and materially shaped racial boundaries through immigration restrictions, marriage bans, and naturalization tests—and **law as ideology**, the power of law to make racial hierarchies seem natural or inevitable. Court cases involving Armenians, Japanese, Mexicans, and interracial marriage revealed how law defined who counted as “white” and who had access to citizenship rights. James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” and the slide on the “social construction of whiteness” illustrated how whiteness developed through the exclusion of others and the granting of privilege.  

The lecture then turned to language and culture. Nieto defined culture as the ever-changing values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews created and transformed by human groups—learned rather than inherited. Culture is multifaceted and hybrid, shaped by family, peers, class, geography, gender, and media, and cannot be reduced to food or holidays.  

In education, schools themselves reflect cultural systems. Slides on the “culture of schooling” identified guiding assumptions: that ability varies by student, is fixed, and can be accurately measured through hierarchies of skills. These beliefs normalize inequality by legitimating tracking and unequal opportunities. Recognizing how school culture shapes students’ experiences, participation, and futures linked the lecture’s broader theme—how social constructions of race and culture structure everyday life.
