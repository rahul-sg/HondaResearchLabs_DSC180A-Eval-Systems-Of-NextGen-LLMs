The lecture explored how race, law, and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity and schooling in the United States. It began with Sonia Nieto’s discussion of how society “constructs others” by classifying people through visible and invisible traits, attaching stereotypes, and building policies that endanger some groups while benefiting others. Students were asked to consider how labels from teachers or counselors may have influenced their sense of intelligence, motivation, and capability, and whether such labels reflected or distorted their full selves. The slides emphasized that silence and denial around race are common but inadequate responses—courage is needed to reflect honestly on how personal upbringing and social norms shape views on cultural difference.  

Scientific and historical evidence affirmed that race is not biologically determined. Rather, racial categories have shifted through political struggles, economic conditions, and legal definitions, becoming “common sense” through everyday learning. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project by the American Anthropological Association illustrated this with evidence dispelling racial myths. Legal frameworks were presented as central to making race real in material life. Ian Haney López explained **law as coercion**, showing how legal rules defined racial boundaries through immigration and naturalization laws, marital restrictions, and exclusionary policies that determined who qualified as “white.” Court cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans demonstrated how rulings redefined racial eligibility for citizenship and reshaped the meaning of whiteness. **Law as ideology** described how legal authority persuades people that racial hierarchies are natural, appearing race‑neutral even in criminal justice or school discipline. James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” underscored the historical construction of whiteness through coercion and privilege.  

The class then turned to language and culture. Nieto defined culture as the evolving values, traditions, social and political relationships, and worldviews formed and transformed by human groups. Culture is learned, dynamic, and hybrid, influenced by factors such as family, peers, class, geography, gender, religion, language, and media.  

Finally, the lecture examined the “culture of schooling.” U.S. schools operate from guiding assumptions that ability varies among students, is relatively fixed, and can be measured in a hierarchy of skills. School culture—reflected in tracking, resources, routines, and expectations—shapes how students experience learning and perceive their potential.
