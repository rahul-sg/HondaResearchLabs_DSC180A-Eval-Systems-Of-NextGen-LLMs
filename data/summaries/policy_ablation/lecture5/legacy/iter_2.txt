This lecture explored how race and culture are socially constructed systems that shape identity, law, schooling, and everyday life. It opened with reflection on labeling in education—how teachers’ or administrators’ assumptions can influence students’ self‑perception and expectations—and urged the courage to confront how societal views of race and difference inform personal beliefs.  

Drawing on Sonia Nieto, the lecture explained that society categorizes people by visible and invisible traits, attaches fixed meanings to those categories, and sustains policies that privilege some while disadvantaging others. Although widely accepted that race has no biological basis, it operates as “common sense” learned through social and political processes. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project demonstrates how scientific evidence has dispelled myths of biological race.  

Law has been central in constructing racial meaning. Ian Haney Lopez described **law as coercion**—legal decisions that shaped who could immigrate, marry, or gain citizenship, thereby influencing both the appearance and social position of racial groups—and **law as ideology**, which persuades people to view racial hierarchies as natural rather than human‑made. Court cases over naturalization (Armenian eligibility for citizenship, Japanese denial, Mexican reclassification) exposed how “whiteness” was defined and redefined to regulate belonging. Armenians were deemed white partly because they posed no perceived threat, while Japanese applicants were rejected when color‑based logic failed. These shifting legal boundaries linked race to fears about power, morality, and national identity. Interracial marriage bans, struck down only in 1967, revealed how law enforced racial separation. Census classifications that continue to evolve further illustrate how governments formalize changing ideas of race.  

James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” emphasized that whiteness itself was produced historically through exclusion and privilege.  

The lecture’s second half examined culture as described by Nieto: the evolving values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews that people collectively create—not inherited but learned, dynamic, and hybrid. Cultural identities are multiple and shaped by family, peers, class, geography, and media. Schools have distinct cultures signaled by facilities, routines, programs, and disciplinary practices. Many U.S. schools operate on assumptions that ability is fixed and hierarchically ranked—beliefs that reflect broader ideologies about merit and difference rather than objective truth. These institutional cultures powerfully influence how students define intelligence, motivation, and their educational possibilities.
