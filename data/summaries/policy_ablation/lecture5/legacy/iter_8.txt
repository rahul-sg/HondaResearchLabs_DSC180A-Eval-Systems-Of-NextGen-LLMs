The lecture examined how both race and culture are socially constructed through historical, legal, and educational systems rather than biologically determined facts. Drawing on Sonia Nieto, it emphasized that societies classify people by visible and invisible traits, attach stereotypes and fixed meanings to those categories, and enforce them through policies that advantage some groups while disadvantaging others. Students were asked to reflect on how labeling in schools may have shaped their motivation, sense of intelligence, or self‑image, and whether such labels reflected or distorted their identities. The section on **Silence, Fear, and Denial** discussed the common reluctance to talk about race in schools, calling for courage and honest reflection on how personal backgrounds shape perceptions of difference.  

The lecture affirmed that race has no biological basis and becomes “common sense” through social learning and historical struggles that redefine racial categories. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated scientific evidence against racial myths. Legal scholar **Ian Haney Lopez** described **law as coercion**, showing that rules governing immigration, naturalization, and marriage literally defined racial boundaries—determining who could become a citizen, marry across lines, or own property. The **Armenian**, **Japanese**, and **Mexican** cases demonstrated how courts created and shifted racial hierarchies linked to citizenship and belonging. **Law as ideology** explained how legal systems normalize these divisions by presenting them as natural rather than constructed, reinforced by race‑neutral criminal‑justice or school‑discipline practices and media depictions that evoke fear or loss of status. Restrictions on interracial marriage—invalidated nationwide only in 1967—and the changing **U.S. Census** categories exemplified how government institutions codify racial identity. James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” underscored how whiteness itself was produced through coercion and time.  

The second half turned to culture. Nieto defined culture as dynamic and learned—a continually changing set of values, traditions, relationships, and worldviews shaped by shared history, geography, language, class, and religion. Everyone has culture, which is multifaceted and hybrid, influenced by families, peers, socioeconomic background, gender, religion, and media. Schools were analyzed as cultural systems (Slide 10) whose physical conditions, grouping practices such as tracking, program offerings, and extracurricular opportunities all express institutional values. The **culture of schooling** assumes that ability varies, is fixed, and can be accurately measured, creating hierarchies of “smart” and “bad” students. These cultural beliefs shape how students experience education, internalize expectations, and reproduce broader racial and social inequalities.
