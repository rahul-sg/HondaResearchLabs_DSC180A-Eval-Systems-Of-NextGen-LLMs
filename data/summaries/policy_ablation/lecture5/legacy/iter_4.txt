This lecture examined how both race and culture are socially constructed, highlighting their formation through law, policy, and education. It opened with Sonia Nieto’s idea that society classifies people by visible and invisible traits, draws fixed conclusions about behavior or intelligence, and builds systems that benefit some groups while harming others. Students were asked to reflect on labeling by teachers or schools—how such labels might shape one’s self‑perception or sense of ability—and on the importance of courage rather than silence or denial in honestly examining how personal upbringing and social influences affect views on race and difference.  

Scientific, historical, and social evidence confirmed that race has no biological basis but is created and redefined through politics, economics, and everyday “common‑sense” learning. The American Anthropological Association’s *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated how research dispels myths of biological race. Law emerged as a central mechanism in constructing racial categories. Ian Haney Lopez described **law as coercion**, showing that legal rules—immigration, naturalization, and marriage laws—literally shaped populations and the physical appearance of communities. Court cases concerning Armenians, Japanese immigrants, and Mexicans demonstrated how racial status shifted with social and political interests: Armenians were accepted as “white,” Japanese applicants were denied citizenship, and Mexicans were legally distinguished as inferior after U.S. territorial expansion. **Law as ideology** further made race appear natural by persuading people that legal and social arrangements simply reflected reality. Examples included criminal enforcement, school discipline, and media practices that embed racial assumptions without naming race. Interracial marriage bans, repealed only in 1967, and changing U.S. Census categories illustrated how government reinforced and revised racial boundaries. James Baldwin’s observation that whiteness itself was created through “a vast amount of coercion” encapsulated this process.  

The latter portion shifted to culture as defined by Nieto: an evolving set of values, relationships, and worldviews continuously created and transformed by people. Culture is learned, dynamic, and hybrid, influenced by family, peers, language, class, religion, geography, and media. Schools also embody distinct cultures—visible in routines, grouping policies, facilities, and resource distribution—that reflect assumptions about ability and hierarchy. Such institutional norms shape students’ identities, participation, and beliefs about being “smart” or “capable,” connecting large‑scale constructions of race and culture to the lived experience of schooling.
