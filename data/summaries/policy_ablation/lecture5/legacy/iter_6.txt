The lecture examined how both race and culture are socially constructed rather than biologically or naturally fixed, showing how social institutions—especially law and education—create and sustain these categories. It began with Sonia Nieto’s explanation that society classifies people by visible and invisible traits, attaches supposed mental or behavioral characteristics to those labels, and builds systems that privilege some groups while disadvantaging others. Students were encouraged to reflect on whether they had been labeled by educators and how such labeling may have influenced their sense of capability or motivation. The instructor emphasized that silence or denial about racial issues only perpetuates inequity; instead, courage and honest self‑reflection are required to confront how upbringing and social context shape attitudes toward race and difference.  

The lecture reinforced the consensus that race has no biological foundation. Historical shifts in racial definitions reveal how economic and political struggles have repeatedly reshaped categories, making race appear to be “common sense.” The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated how scientific evidence counters racial myths. Ian Haney Lopez’s analysis of **law as coercion** showed how legal rules—governing immigration, naturalization, marriage, and property—have literally shaped physical appearances and citizenship status by defining who could belong. Cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans illustrated how courts legally manufactured racial boundaries and hierarchies. **Law as ideology** describes how these legal systems persuade people that racial divisions are natural, a process visible in criminal justice disparities, school discipline, and media narratives that exploit fears without explicitly naming race. Interracial‑marriage bans, repealed only in 1967, and the evolving racial categories of the U.S. Census further demonstrated how government defines belonging. James Baldwin’s observation that whiteness required generations of coercion summarized this creation of racial identity.  

The second half focused on the social construction of culture. Nieto defined culture as continually changing values, traditions, and worldviews created and transformed through shared history, geography, class, religion, and language. Culture is learned rather than inherited, dynamic and hybrid, and everyone has one. Cultural experience is mediated by multiple factors—family, peers, class, gender, religion, language, community, and media. Schools themselves possess distinct cultures shaped by routines, resources, and hierarchies. The *culture of schooling* rests on assumptions that student ability varies, is relatively fixed and measurable, and that learning follows a strict hierarchy of skills. Such beliefs define who is considered “smart” or “a bad student” and affect students’ participation, motivation, and self‑perception—revealing how institutional culture reproduces social assumptions about merit, race, and difference.
