This lecture examined how racial and cultural identities are socially constructed and how those constructions influence law, education, and personal experience. It began by prompting reflection on labeling in schools—how teachers or administrators’ assumptions can shape students’ self-concept and sense of capability. Students were encouraged to confront how fear or silence around discussions of race perpetuate misunderstanding, whereas courage and self-reflection create space for honest dialogue about difference.  

Drawing on Sonia Nieto, the lecture emphasized that society classifies people by visible and invisible traits and then attaches meanings that advantage some groups while restricting others. Race, while having no biological basis, has become “common sense” through social and political processes. The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrates how scientific evidence has dispelled myths of biological race. Laws have also played a decisive role in forming these categories. Ian Haney López distinguished between **law as coercion**, through which legislation and court decisions have physically and socially shaped racial groups—such as defining who could marry or immigrate—and **law as ideology**, which persuades people to see racial hierarchies as natural rather than constructed. Legal cases around the naturalization of Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans and the bans on interracial marriage exposed how racial meaning has been defined and redefined to regulate citizenship and belonging. James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” underscored how whiteness itself was historically produced through exclusion and privilege. Changing census categories further illustrated evolving official definitions of race and identity.  

The second portion of the lecture turned to culture. Nieto described culture as the shared and continually transforming values, relationships, and worldviews created by human groups—not inherited but learned and hybrid. Cultural identities are multiple and situational, influenced by family, peers, class, geography, and media. Schools have their own cultures shaped by facilities, routines, student grouping, and extracurricular opportunities. Many U.S. schools operate on assumptions that ability is fixed, measurable, and hierarchically ordered—beliefs that can reinforce inequality. These institutional cultures affect how students view intelligence, motivation, and their educational futures. Overall, the lecture connected how race and culture, as socially constructed systems, structure opportunities and shape everyday experiences in education and society.
