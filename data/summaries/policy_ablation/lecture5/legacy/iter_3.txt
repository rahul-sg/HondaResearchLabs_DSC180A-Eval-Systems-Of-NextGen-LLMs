This lecture examined how race and culture are socially constructed, emphasizing both legal‑political and educational dimensions. It began with Nieto’s discussion of how societies classify people by visible and invisible traits, attach generalizations to those traits, and institutionalize policies that advantage some groups while harming others. Students were invited to reflect personally on labeling—how teachers’ or administrators’ assumptions may have shaped their self‑understanding—and to practice courage rather than silence or denial in confronting how upbringing shapes perceptions of race and difference.  

Scientific and historical evidence confirmed that race is not biologically determined but socially created through political struggle, economic change, and everyday learning. Projects like *RACE: Are We So Different?* use research to dispel biological myths and show how racial categories become “common sense.”  

Law provided a key mechanism in producing racial meaning. Ian Haney Lopez described **law as coercion**, illustrating how legal rules governing immigration, naturalization, and marriage defined who was “white” or eligible for citizenship. Cases of Armenians, Japanese immigrants, and Mexicans revealed shifting definitions: Armenians were accepted as white because they were not viewed as a threat, Japanese petitioners were rejected when skin‑color arguments collapsed, and Mexicans were reclassified as distinct and inferior after U.S. territorial expansion. **Law as ideology** further normalized these inventions—convincing people to see racial hierarchy as natural through seemingly neutral policies in schools, courts, and media discourse. Interracial marriage bans, struck down only in 1967, and changing U.S. Census categories illustrated how the state codified racial boundaries. James Baldwin’s remark that “no one was white before coming to America” underscored the historical construction of whiteness through exclusion and privilege.  

In the second half, the focus shifted to culture. Drawing on Nieto, culture was defined as the continually changing values, relationships, and worldviews that people create, share, and transform. It is learned, not inherited, and always hybrid—shaped by family, peers, language, class, religion, geography, and media. Schools themselves have distinct cultures—visible in buildings, routines, grouping practices, and resource distribution—that convey cultural assumptions such as fixed ability and ordered hierarchies of talent. These institutional norms affect students’ identities, experiences, and beliefs about intelligence and potential, showing how systemic ideas about race and culture operate both through law and in everyday schooling.
