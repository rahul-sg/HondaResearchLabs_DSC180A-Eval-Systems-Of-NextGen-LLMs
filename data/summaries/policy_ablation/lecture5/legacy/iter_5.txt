The lecture explored how race and culture are socially constructed rather than biologically fixed, emphasizing their creation through law, policy, and educational practice. It began with Sonia Nieto’s idea that society classifies people by visible and invisible traits, attaches assumptions about behavior or intelligence, and builds systems that advantage some while disadvantaging others. Students were invited to reflect on whether educators had labeled them and how those labels shaped their self‑perception or motivation. Another theme was the need for courage—not silence or denial—to confront how upbringing and social context influence personal views of race and difference.  

Scientific and historical evidence confirmed that race has no biological basis; instead, categories emerge from political, social, and economic forces that make race appear as “common sense.” The American Anthropological Association’s *RACE: Are We So Different?* project exemplified efforts to dispel myths of biological race. Law was highlighted as a key mechanism constructing racial meaning. Ian Haney Lopez described **law as coercion**, showing how immigration, naturalization, and marriage policies determined who could belong and even shaped the physical composition of the population. The lecture noted cases in which Armenians secured “white” status, Japanese immigrants were denied citizenship, and Mexicans were recast as legally distinct and inferior after U.S. expansion. **Law as ideology** operates when legal rules persuade people that racial hierarchies are natural rather than socially produced, seen in criminal justice and school discipline patterns or media messaging that implies racial difference without naming it. Interracial‑marriage bans, repealed only in 1967, and evolving U.S. Census categories illustrated government roles in redefining racial boundaries. James Baldwin’s observation that whiteness was created through generations of coercion captured this process.  

The lecture then examined the social construction of culture. Nieto defined culture as continually changing values, traditions, and worldviews shaped through shared history, geography, language, class, and religion. Culture is learned, dynamic, and hybrid, not inherited or fixed, and everyone participates in creating it. Multiple factors—family, peers, class, gender, religion, language, community, and media—mediate cultural experience. Schools also possess distinct cultures evident in routines, resources, and practices such as tracking. Many U.S. schools share guiding assumptions that ability differs among students, is relatively fixed, and can be precisely measured, reinforcing hierarchies. These institutional beliefs influence students’ identities, participation, and definitions of being “smart” or capable, linking societal constructions of race and culture to everyday schooling.
