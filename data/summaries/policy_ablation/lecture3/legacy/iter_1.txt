This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the *coloniality of power* and its continuing influence on modernity, capitalism, and identity across Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano’s intellectual trajectory—from dependency theory in the 1960s–70s to the decolonial turn of the 1990s—traced how power and knowledge systems created through colonialism still shape global hierarchies. His work forms part of the Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project, which links counter‑histories of Western modernity with critiques of colonial domination and calls for alternative epistemologies centered on non‑Western cultures and worldviews.

At the center of Quijano’s framework is the *coloniality of power*, a historical and structural order born from two interrelated processes: the classification of human differences separating conquerors (“West”) from the conquered (“Rest”), and the creation of new systems of labor control to exploit American resources and peoples. Racial categorizations served as the mental foundation of modernity, legitimizing slavery, encomienda, and mita labor systems and embedding racial hierarchies into global capitalism. These divisions governed access to work, authority, and privilege, maintaining social order long after formal colonialism ended.

The lecture’s second focus, *Eurocentrism*, exposes how Western knowledge systems came to define what counts as universal truth and progress. Quijano argued that Europe’s ascent was not a natural outcome of superiority but a historical construction built on exploitation. Concepts such as “developed” versus “underdeveloped” societies reinforced linear narratives of history that naturalized whiteness and Western dominance. This critique parallels Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest” discourse by showing how representation and power remain intertwined.

In connecting coloniality and Eurocentrism to Latin American national identity, the lecture turned to historian Rebecca Earle’s discussion of *Indianesque nationalism* and later *mestizaje*. Early independence movements romanticized Indigenous heritage as a symbolic past while excluding living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant peoples from the imagined nation. The ideal of racial mixing in *mestizaje* appeared to bridge differences but often reaffirmed whiteness, as illustrated by artworks like Modesto Broccos’s 1895 painting *A Redenção de Cã*, which visually celebrated “whitening” through generations. Such visual politics reveal how colonial hierarchies persist within nationalist myths.

Ultimately, Quijano’s call for *decoloniality*—to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror”—urges critical re‑evaluation of knowledge, culture, and identity. By unsettling Eurocentric standards of progress and embracing heterogeneous histories and perspectives, decolonial thought aims to dismantle the enduring matrix of colonial power.
