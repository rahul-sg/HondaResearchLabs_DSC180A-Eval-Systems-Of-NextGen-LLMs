This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the *coloniality of power* and its lasting influence on modernity, capitalism, and identity in Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano’s intellectual trajectory—from dependency theory in the 1960s–70s, to studies of culture and identity in the 1980s, and to decolonial thought from the 1990s onward—situates him within the Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project. This body of scholarship reconstructs world history from perspectives that challenge Eurocentric narratives of “Western superiority,” centering instead the epistemologies, languages, and cosmologies of non‑Western societies.

At the core of Quijano’s framework lies the *coloniality of power*, a structure born from two intertwined historical processes: the classification of human differences separating conquerors (“West”) from the conquered (“Rest”), and the formation of new systems of labor control to extract resources from the Americas. Racial categories—mental constructs of modernity—legitimized exploitative systems such as slavery, encomienda, and mita. Quijano emphasized that Europeans associated nonpaid or coerced labor with those they deemed “inferior races,” intertwining racialization with the rise of global capitalism. These divisions determined who had access to resources, authority, and privilege, persisting beyond formal colonial rule.

The second analytic, *Eurocentrism*, critiques how Western knowledge came to define universal truth and historical progress. Europe’s dominance, Quijano argued, was not an inevitable result of civilization’s advancement but a historical construction grounded in exploitation and exclusion. Linear developmental theories—“underdeveloped” to “developed,” “Third World” to “First World”—reinforced a dualist worldview that naturalized whiteness and Western power. This mirrors Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest” discourse by showing how representation normalizes hierarchy within global thought.

The lecture also emphasized the *crisis of the European perspective of knowledge* as an opportunity to embrace heterogeneity and discontinuity. Rejecting linear progress, decolonial thought values diverse, historically situated forms of agency and recognizes the complexity of overlapping processes—what Aymara notions of *pachakuti* express as cyclical transformation.

Extending theory to culture, historian Rebecca Earle’s concept of *Indianesque nationalism* and later *mestizaje* illustrate how early national identities romanticized Indigenous pasts while excluding Indigenous and Afro‑descendant peoples in the present. Works like Modesto Broccos’s 1895 painting *A Redenção de Cã* visualized “whitening” as social advancement, revealing how colonial hierarchies persisted under post‑independence ideals.

Ultimately, Quijano’s call for *decoloniality*—to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror”—invites a critical re‑imagining of modernity. By recognizing heterogeneity, re‑centering suppressed worldviews, and dismantling the racial–capitalist matrix of power, this approach envisions plural modernities grounded in justice and epistemic renewal.
