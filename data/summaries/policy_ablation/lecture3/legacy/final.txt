This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the *coloniality of power* within the broader Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project—a collaborative intellectual current including figures such as Walter Mignolo, Arturo Escobar, and Catherine Walsh. Rooted in Latin American critical thought, the MCD framework reinterprets modernity as inseparable from coloniality, positioning Western modernity not as universal progress but as historically co‑constituted through domination, racial hierarchy, and the suppression of non‑Western epistemologies.

Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist, contributes to this framework across several decades: from dependency theory’s focus on political economy in the 1960s–70s, to cultural and identity analysis in the 1980s, and to the epistemological turn toward decolonial thought in the 1990s onward. His core thesis identifies the *coloniality of power* as the enduring matrix linking race and labor after formal colonialism ended. Two historical processes define it: the classification of differences between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and the establishment of new labor structures to control American resources. Race, constructed as a mental category of modernity, legitimized systems such as slavery, the encomienda, and the mita, embedding racial divisions within emerging global capitalism and producing persistent inequalities in access to resources and authority.

The second analytic, *Eurocentrism*, critiques the naturalization of European perspectives as universal truth. Quijano contends that Europe’s global dominance was not the inevitable result of superior “civilization,” but of exploitation justified through dualist histories—“underdeveloped” versus “developed,” “Third World” versus “First World.” These narratives, similar to Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest” discourse, illustrate how knowledge production sustains racialized and geopolitical hierarchies.

Pedagogically, the lecture guided students through portfolio tasks that modeled decolonial interpretation: locating textual passages that illustrate key concepts, annotating their relevance to coloniality, and articulating how Quijano’s ideas expose ongoing legacies of domination and exclusion. This exercise reinforced reading as a practice of discernment and resistance within the MCD approach.

Later sections extended theory to cultural and historical examples. Rebecca Earle’s analysis of *Indianesque nationalism* and *mestizaje* demonstrated how post‑independence nations romanticized Indigenous ancestry while marginalizing living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant populations, visualized in Modesto Broccos’s painting *A Redenção de Cã*. These cases reveal how Eurocentric ideals persisted through national mythmaking.

Ultimately, the lecture closed with Quijano’s call for *decoloniality*—to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror.” By embracing heterogeneity and recovering suppressed worldviews, decolonial thought envisions plural modernities and opens pathways toward epistemic and social renewal.
