This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the *coloniality of power*—a framework explaining how colonial-era patterns of domination continue to organize knowledge, labor, and identity in Latin America. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist, developed this idea after earlier work in dependency theory and regional cultural analysis. His thinking became part of the wider Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project, which interrogates the links among modernity, coloniality, and efforts toward decoloniality.  

Modernity, in this framework, is understood not as a Western gift but as a historical condition co‑constituted with colonial expansion. Coloniality exposes how domination persisted after formal colonialism through Eurocentric categories of knowledge that privileged Western perspectives while marginalizing Indigenous, African, and other epistemologies. Decoloniality, then, does not simply oppose coloniality but seeks to open intellectual and cultural spaces for multiple, non‑Western worlds of knowing and being.

At the core of Quijano’s argument are two intertwined historical processes: the invention of racial distinctions separating *conquerors* (“West”) from the *conquered* (“Rest”) and the formation of new systems of labor control in the Americas. Enslavement, the encomienda, and the mita institutionalized a global model of racialized labor that tied whiteness to power and non‑European peoples to coerced or unpaid work. This fusion of race and economy produced a “systemic racial division of labor,” shaping access to resources, status, and authority even beyond colonial rule. Eurocentrism emerged from and sustained this structure, naturalizing Europe’s dominance and portraying its trajectory as universal progress.

Slides also link Eurocentrism to ideas of nation, development, and democracy after independence. Linear narratives of “progress” and “underdevelopment” reproduced colonial hierarchies inside new nation‑states. Rebecca Earle’s discussion of “Indianesque nationalism” shows how independence‑era leaders romanticized Indigenous ancestry as national symbolism while excluding living Indigenous communities from citizenship. Later *mestizaje* ideologies invoked racial mixing as harmony but continued privileging whiteness, evident across cultural forms from family portrayals to artworks such as *A Redenção de Cam.*  

The lecture situates cultural examples like Alfonso Cuarón’s *Roma* within this matrix, illustrating how racialized and gendered hierarchies persist in everyday life. Quijano’s closing challenge—to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror” that distorts identity—frames decoloniality as an ongoing process of unlearning inherited dualisms, recognizing historical heterogeneity, and envisioning plural modernities beyond the confines of Eurocentric thought.
