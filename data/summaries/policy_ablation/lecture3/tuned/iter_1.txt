This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the *coloniality of power* as an ongoing matrix linking race, labor, and Eurocentrism from the conquest of the Americas to contemporary Latin American realities. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist, evolved from dependency theory and cultural analyses of modernity to develop decolonial theory—a framework exposing how colonial domination continues through hierarchies of knowledge, being, and economy.

Within the modernity/coloniality/decoloniality (MCD) project, the lecture highlights three interrelated spheres. *Modernity* is reconsidered not as Europe’s natural progression but as a historical outcome of conquest and global exploitation. *Coloniality* denotes the persistence of hierarchical ways of thinking that privilege Western epistemologies and silence Indigenous and Afro-descendant worldviews. *Decoloniality* involves efforts to re-center these marginalized perspectives, cultures, and cosmologies.  

The “coloniality of power” emerged through two foundational processes: the construction of differences between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and the invention of new structures of labor control within global capitalism. Racial classification became a mental category of modernity that justified slavery, the encomienda, and the mita, embedding economic dependence and social inequality across the Americas. As shown on slides connecting coloniality and global capitalism, racialized labor produced systemic inequality in access to privilege, wealth, and authority—making whiteness appear as a natural sign of progress.

The lecture also clarifies Quijano’s critique of *Eurocentrism* as a perspective that constructs linear hierarchies—“pre-capital” versus “developed,” “Third World” versus “First World”—which mask the co‑constitution of modernity and coloniality. This “Eurocentric mirror” presents the West as universal measure of civilization. However, the *crisis of the European perspective of knowledge* opens space for more heterogeneous, non-linear understandings of history inspired by notions such as the Aymara *pachakuti* and by situated political agency.

Applications like Cuáron’s *Roma* illustrate how racial, class, and gender inequalities persist through modern cultural narratives. Turning to national identity, Rebecca Earle’s analysis of “Indianesque nationalism” shows post‑independence elites praising Indigenous symbols while excluding living Indigenous peoples, later replaced by *mestizaje* ideals that celebrated whitening under claims of unity. These examples demonstrate Quijano’s call for decoloniality: to unlearn Eurocentric hierarchies of thought and open plural, locally grounded ways of knowing, remembering, and being in the Americas.
