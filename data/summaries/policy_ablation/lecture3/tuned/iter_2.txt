This lecture explores Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the *coloniality of power*—the enduring matrix that links race, labor, and Eurocentrism from the conquest of the Americas to the present. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano developed this framework through successive stages of thought: from dependency theory and analyses of Latin American political economy and culture to decolonial theory focused on the relationship between knowledge and power.  

Within the modernity/coloniality/decoloniality (MCD) project, the lecture distinguishes three interrelated yet separate dimensions. *Modernity* refers to the dominant historical narrative of Western progress, which Quijano and others reinterpret as the contingent outcome of colonial expansion rather than an internal European achievement. *Coloniality* identifies the continuing logic of domination that privileges Western epistemologies, categories, and hierarchies while silencing Indigenous and Afro‑descendant ways of knowing. *Decoloniality* is treated less as a complete alternative ideology than as a critical practice that seeks to unlearn Eurocentric assumptions and recognize the multiplicity of suppressed perspectives.  

The “coloniality of power” took shape through two historical processes: constructing differences between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”) and establishing new labor systems—slavery, encomienda, and mita—that organized production for global markets. Racial classification became a mental category of modernity, naturalizing inequality and embedding a racialized division of labor that tied whiteness to authority and development.  

Quijano links this racial ordering to global capitalism and critiques *Eurocentrism*—a worldview that frames Europe as the solitary agent of modernity. Eurocentric thought produces dualisms like “developed/underdeveloped” and promotes linear, evolutionary histories that obscure how colonial encounters created these distinctions. The lecture highlights a growing “crisis” of this perspective of knowledge, emphasizing heterogeneous, non‑linear understandings of history, exemplified by the Aymara notion of *pachakuti* and by Hall’s analysis of the “West and the Rest.”  

Applied examples extend these ideas. Cuáron’s *Roma* reveals how classed and racialized hierarchies persist within modern cultural representations. Rebecca Earle’s study of “Indianesque nationalism” shows post‑independence elites celebrating Indigenous symbols while marginalizing living Indigenous peoples—a contradiction later masked by *mestizaje* ideologies of racial democracy. Closing with Quijano’s challenge to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror,” the lecture invites critical reflection on how knowledge, identity, and national self‑understandings continue to reproduce colonial patterns.
