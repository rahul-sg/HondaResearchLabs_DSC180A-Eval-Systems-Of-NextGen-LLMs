This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the *coloniality of power*—an enduring system linking race, labor, and Eurocentrism from the conquest of the Americas to the present. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist, developed his thinking from dependency theory and analyses of Latin America’s political economy to later work in decolonial theory on knowledge and power. His approach situates Latin American experience within global capitalism and exposes how colonial hierarchies continue to organize power, culture, and identity.  

Within the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework, the lecture distinguishes three dimensions of critique. *Modernity* revises the idea of European progress by showing that “Western” modernity arose through colonial expansion and exploitation rather than internal superiority. *Coloniality* explains how matrices of domination persist by privileging Eurocentric categories of knowledge and obscuring Indigenous and other non‑Western ways of being. *Decoloniality* seeks to center non‑Western languages, cosmologies, and histories, advancing plural modernities and alternative epistemologies.  

The *coloniality of power* emerged through two formative processes: the creation of racial difference between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”) and the reorganization of labor in the Americas through systems such as slavery, encomienda, and mita. These arrangements tied racial classification to global economic roles, producing a racialized division of labor that became foundational to global capitalism. Quijano highlights how ideas of whiteness and European superiority came to define who holds authority, access, and privilege.  

Slides connect this racial ordering to **Eurocentrism**, a worldview that frames Europe as both source and measure of knowledge, casting non‑Europe as inferior. Eurocentric narratives promote linear binaries—“developed/underdeveloped,” “First/Third World”—that naturalize inequality. Quijano’s critique also recalls dependency theory, identifying how colonial and neocolonial economic structures maintain geopolitical dependence within Latin America. The lecture points to growing recognition of discontinuity and heterogeneity in history—illustrated by the Aymara *pachakuti* concept and Hall’s “West and the Rest”—that challenges a single, continuous European viewpoint.  

Applications further illustrate these logics. Cuáron’s *Roma* reveals how racial and class hierarchies endure in contemporary representation. Rebecca Earle’s study of post‑independence “Indianesque nationalism” shows elites romanticizing Indigenous symbols while excluding Indigenous peoples, later reimagined through *mestizaje* ideologies of racial democracy. The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror,” urging a decolonial rethinking of knowledge, identity, and national belonging.
