This week’s lecture examined Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** and its continuing influence on modernity, capitalism, and national identity in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist—developed key ideas through phases of dependency theory (focused on political economy), cultural and historiographic analysis (modernity, identity, culture), and decolonial theory (knowledge and power). His work contributes to the broader **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project**, alongside scholars such as Arturo Escobar, Walter Mignolo, and Catherine Walsh, who collectively question Europe’s claim to universal modernity and assert the importance of non‑Western epistemologies.

The **coloniality of power**, Quijano argues, originated through two interconnected historical processes: the creation of racial hierarchies that divided conquerors (“the West”) from the conquered (“the Rest”), and the establishment of new structures for controlling labor and resources in the Americas. This reorganization of global production relied on coerced systems such as slavery, the **encomienda**, and the **mita**, embedding racialized labor into emerging capitalism. The result was a lasting global order where access to privilege, space, and authority remained tied to race.

Building on this, the lecture turned to **Eurocentrism**—the naturalized worldview that locates Europe as the center of history and knowledge. Slides emphasized that Eurocentrism rests on **linear and dualistic models of progress** (“pre‑capital” → “industrial,” “Third World” → “First World”), which portray Western development as the universal path and render other histories “backward.” Quijano exposes how such evolutionist thinking sustains global inequality while erasing the colonial origins of modern capitalism.

Later sections linked these ideas to Latin American **nation‑building** and identity. Drawing on Rebecca Earle’s study of *“Indianesque nationalism,”* the lecture showed how independence movements romanticized Indigenous imagery to distinguish new nations from Spain, even as they marginalized living Indigenous peoples. The ideology of **mestizaje**, presented as harmonious “racial mixing,” further reinforced Eurocentric standards of beauty and belonging while excluding Afro‑descendant and Indigenous groups. Quijano’s framework helps explain these continuities as expressions of coloniality within post‑colonial forms.

Finally, the lecture introduced Quijano’s and others’ call for **decoloniality**, aligning with the MCD project’s move beyond linear temporality toward **heterogeneity and discontinuity** in historical experience—echoing Aymara notions of *pachakuti* as cyclical world renewal. Decolonial thinking thus seeks to free knowledge from the “Eurocentric mirror,” recover plural modernities, and recognize multiple, situated ways of being and knowing beyond inherited colonial frameworks.
