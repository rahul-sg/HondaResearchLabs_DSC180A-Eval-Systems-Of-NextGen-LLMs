This week’s lecture expanded on Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power**, a framework for analyzing how colonial structures continue to shape Latin American societies and global hierarchies. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist whose work spans dependency theory, cultural analysis, and decolonial thought—forms part of a broader group of scholars (including Arturo Escobar, Walter Mignolo, and Catherine Walsh) often associated with the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** perspective. His scholarship situates colonialism not as an isolated past event but as an enduring set of epistemic and material relationships linking knowledge, race, labor, and power.  

According to Quijano, the **coloniality of power** emerged through two foundational processes: (1) the creation of racial difference separating conquerors (the “West”) from the conquered (the “Rest”), and (2) new systems for organizing labor and resources in the Americas. Colonial authorities established racialized hierarchies within developing capitalist economies—through slavery, the **encomienda**, and the **mita**—that structured who held access to land, wealth, and authority. These racialized divisions became the basis for a global order that persists under capitalism and modern national systems.  

The lecture then linked these frameworks to **Eurocentrism**, described as a Western standpoint that presents its own historical experience as universal. Eurocentric narratives of “progress” rely on dualisms such as “pre‑capital/industrial” and “Third World/First World,” which conceal the colonial origins of global capitalism and normalize European dominance. By coupling racial hierarchies with capitalist development, Eurocentrism defined whose perspectives counted as knowledge and whose labor sustained the modern world.  

Applications illustrated how these logics continue in cultural and national imaginaries. The film *Roma* was discussed as an example of how domestic labor and racial hierarchy in contemporary Mexico reflect colonial divisions. Drawing on historian Rebecca Earle’s research, slides on **“Indianesque nationalism”** showed that after independence, Latin American elites celebrated Indigenous symbols to mark separation from Spain while excluding living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant peoples. The ideology of **mestizaje**, though portrayed as unity, effectively reaffirmed Eurocentric norms and whitened national identity.  

Later slides emphasized **decoloniality** as a call to break from the Eurocentric “mirror” that distorts collective self‑understanding. Questioning linear, homogenizing histories opens space for recognizing heterogeneous and historically specific processes of change. In this sense, decolonial thinking re‑centers marginalized epistemologies and reimagines modernity through plural, locally grounded worldviews.
