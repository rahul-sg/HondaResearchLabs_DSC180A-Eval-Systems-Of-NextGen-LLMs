The Week 1 lecture in *Principles of Accounting* introduces the foundation and purpose of financial accounting and its role in supporting business decision‑making. Students are expected to learn how financial statements are created, interpreted, and used to make informed judgments in any organizational context. Accounting is described as the **language of business**, telling a company’s financial story—its successes, risks, and opportunities—through organized financial information.  

Accounting serves two main functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys results to external decision makers through financial statements. Financial information is used by diverse groups: **investors**, **creditors**, and **suppliers** assess performance and creditworthiness; **management** and **boards of directors** evaluate strategy and operations; and **employees**, **customers**, regulators, analysts, and others use the information to gauge financial stability and reliability.  

Financial accounting captures three categories of **business activity**: *financing* (transactions with investors and creditors, such as issuing stock or obtaining loans), *investing* (acquiring productive resources like equipment or land), and *operating* (day‑to‑day production and sales). The resulting information is reported in four key **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows.  

The **financial reporting process** follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** so reports are comparable and consistent. The **SEC** oversees public reporting and authorizes the independent **FASB** to set standards, while the **IASB** issues international standards (IFRS). Public companies release audited annual reports (10‑K), unaudited quarterly reports (10‑Q), and current reports (8‑K) for major events. Independent **auditors**, required for public firms, test compliance with GAAP and add credibility to financial statements. They issue one of four opinions: **unqualified** (clean), **unqualified with explanatory emphasis**, **qualified**, or **adverse** (rare).  

The lecture emphasizes the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific point in time, built around the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Each transaction has a dual effect that preserves this equality. The *Klein, Inc.* example illustrates balance sheet mechanics: issuing $1 million in common stock and borrowing $500 thousand created $1.5 million in assets; subsequent purchases of equipment, prepaid rent, and supplies on account demonstrated how financing, investing, and operating activities interact while maintaining balance in total resources.
