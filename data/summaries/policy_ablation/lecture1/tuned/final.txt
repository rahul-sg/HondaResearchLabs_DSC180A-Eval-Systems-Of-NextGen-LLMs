The opening week of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the **financial accounting framework** and establishes how the course builds conceptual understanding over time—from the basic framework and double-entry mechanics to the full accounting cycle and specific asset and equity topics. The week’s lecture positions accounting as the **language of business**, explaining how it conveys a company’s financial story—its successes, risks, and opportunities—through systematically recorded and communicated information.

Accounting serves two central functions. **Recordkeeping** involves identifying, measuring, and recording a company’s economic events in value terms. **Communication** translates that information into reports used by decision makers outside the company. These external users include investors, creditors, suppliers, management, boards of directors, employees, customers, and others such as analysts or regulators—each relying on accounting data to assess performance, evaluate creditworthiness, or plan future actions.

Financial accounting captures and summarizes three categories of business activity: **financing** (transactions with owners and creditors such as issuing stock or borrowing), **investing** (acquiring or disposing of resources like buildings or equipment), and **operating** (day-to-day transactions related to producing and selling products or services). These activities produce the four primary **financial statements**—the **balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity,** and **statement of cash flows**—which together communicate a company’s financial position and results over time.

Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, the rules governing what, when, and how transactions are presented. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** holds ultimate authority for setting standards for publicly traded firms but **delegates** the standard‑setting process to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**. The **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues comparable international guidelines known as **IFRS**. Independent **auditors** evaluate whether a company’s financial statements comply with GAAP and are free from material misstatements, issuing unqualified, qualified, or adverse opinions as appropriate.

The lecture concludes with the **balance sheet**, which reports financial position at a specific date following the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets represent resources with future economic benefits, liabilities are obligations to creditors, and equity is owners’ residual claim. Through the *Klein, Inc.* example—showing stock issuance, bank borrowing, equipment purchases, prepaid rent, and credit purchases—students see that every transaction has a **dual effect**, preserving balance within the equation and illustrating how accounting reflects real business activity.
