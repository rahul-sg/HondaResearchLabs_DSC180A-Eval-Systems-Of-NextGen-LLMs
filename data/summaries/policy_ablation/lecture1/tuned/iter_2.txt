The Week 1 lecture in *Principles of Accounting* introduces the foundation and purpose of financial accounting, explaining how it supports informed business decisions. Accounting is described as the **language of business**, communicating a company’s financial story—its successes, challenges, and opportunities—through systematically recorded information. It performs two central functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to external decision makers through financial statements.

Financial information is used by diverse groups. **Investors** and **creditors** evaluate performance and the ability to repay debt; **suppliers**, **management**, and **boards of directors** assess operations, policies, and strategy; while **employees**, **customers**, regulators, analysts, and other stakeholders assess financial stability and reliability.  

Financial accounting captures three types of **business activities**: *financing activities* (transactions with investors and creditors, such as issuing stock or borrowing funds), *investing activities* (acquiring or disposing of productive resources like equipment or buildings), and *operating activities* (the daily production, promotion, and sale of goods or services). Information from these transactions is communicated through four main **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows.

The **financial reporting process** follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** to ensure comparable and reliable reporting. The **SEC** requires public companies to prepare periodic statements and delegates standard‑setting to the **FASB**, while international standards are governed by the **IASB** under IFRS. Public firms issue annual audited reports (10‑K), quarterly unaudited reports (10‑Q), and current reports (8‑K) for major events. Independent **auditors**, required for public companies, strengthen confidence in financial statements by verifying GAAP compliance and issuing professional opinions.

The lecture focuses on understanding the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific point in time. It demonstrates the **accounting equation**—**Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**—showing that every transaction has a dual effect yet keeps the totals balanced. Through the *Klein, Inc.* example, monetary transactions illustrate this balance: issuing $1 million of common stock (equity) and obtaining a $500 thousand bank loan (liability) created $1.5 million in total assets. Subsequent transactions—purchasing $240 thousand in equipment, prepaying $180 thousand rent, and buying $14 thousand of supplies on account—showed how financing, investing, and operating decisions interrelate while maintaining equality within the balance sheet.
