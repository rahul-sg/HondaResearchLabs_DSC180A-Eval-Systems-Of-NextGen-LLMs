The Week 1 lecture in *Principles of Accounting* introduces the foundation and purpose of financial accounting and explains how it supports business decision‑making. Accounting is presented as the **language of business**, a system that tells a company’s financial story—its successes, failures, risks, and opportunities—through systematically recorded data. It serves two key functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to decision makers through financial statements.

Users of accounting information include investors evaluating management performance, creditors assessing a borrower’s ability to repay, suppliers determining credit and supply‑chain commitments, and management and boards monitoring company operations and strategy. Other users—employees, customers, analysts, and regulators—also rely on financial reports to judge financial health, reliability, and compliance.

Financial accounting captures and measures three categories of **business activities**. *Financing activities* involve transactions with investors and creditors, such as stock issuance or borrowing. *Investing activities* relate to obtaining or disposing of assets needed to operate, such as buildings or equipment. *Operating activities* encompass day‑to‑day production, promotion, and sales. Together, these activities generate information summarized in four main **financial statements**: the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows.

The lecture outlines the **financial reporting process** governed by Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP). The SEC oversees standards for public companies and delegates standard‑setting to the independent Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB); internationally, similar standards are maintained by the IASB under IFRS. Public companies file periodic reports—10‑K (annual, audited), 10‑Q (quarterly, unaudited), and 8‑K (current reports on major events). **Independent auditors**, required for public firms, assess compliance with GAAP and issue opinions that enhance the credibility of financial statements.

The session emphasizes the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific date. It must satisfy the accounting equation **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**, illustrating that each transaction affects at least two accounts but keeps totals in balance. Assets represent resources with future benefits; liabilities reflect amounts owed; and stockholders’ equity shows owners’ residual claims. Step‑by‑step examples—issuing stock, borrowing from a bank, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and buying supplies on account—demonstrate how real transactions maintain this balance and convey the firm’s overall financial position.
