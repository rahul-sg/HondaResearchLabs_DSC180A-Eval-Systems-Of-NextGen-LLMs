The first week of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the foundations of financial accounting—the process of recording and communicating a company’s economic activities to support informed decision‑making. Accounting is described as the “language of business,” telling a firm’s financial story of performance, opportunities, and risks. It fulfills two core functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records transactions in value terms, and **communication**, which conveys the results to external users through financial statements.  

Financial information serves a wide range of users. **Investors**, **creditors**, and **suppliers** evaluate profitability, creditworthiness, and reliability. **Management** and **boards of directors** use accounting data to monitor operations, design strategy, and assess performance. **Employees** and **customers** consider the company’s stability when making job or purchasing decisions, while **analysts**, **regulators**, and **competitors** rely on transparent reporting for oversight and comparison.  

Financial accounting captures three types of business activity: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders), **investing** (acquiring resources such as equipment, land, or inventory), and **operating** (daily production, marketing, and sales). These transactions generate information compiled into four key **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows.  

Reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under authority of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**, which requires public companies to issue periodic statements. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS**. Independent **auditors**, required by the SEC for publicly traded firms, enhance reliability by evaluating compliance with GAAP and issuing one of four opinions: unqualified, unqualified with explanatory note, qualified, or adverse.  

Public companies routinely communicate results through three main filings: the **10‑K** (annual, audited), **10‑Q** (quarterly, unaudited), and **8‑K** (current events within four business days).  

The lecture concludes with the **balance sheet**, presenting a company’s financial position at a specific date through the equation **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets are resources providing future benefit, liabilities are obligations to creditors, and equity represents owners’ residual claims. Each transaction affects at least two accounts to maintain balance, demonstrated through examples of issuing stock, borrowing, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and buying supplies on credit—showing how financing, investing, and operating activities interconnect to portray an organization’s financial standing.
