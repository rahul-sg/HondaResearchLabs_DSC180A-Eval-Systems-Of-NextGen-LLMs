The first week of *Principles of Accounting* introduces students to the foundation of financial accounting and its role in understanding business performance. Accounting is presented as the **language of business**, conveying a company’s financial story—its successes, failures, opportunities, challenges, risks, and uncertainties. The discipline serves two essential functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events, and **communication**, which conveys this information to decision makers through financial statements.

Users of financial information include **investors**, who assess management performance and financial condition; **creditors**, who evaluate repayment ability; **suppliers**, who determine credit terms and partnership reliability; **management** and **boards of directors**, who monitor operations and strategy; as well as **employees**, **customers**, **regulators**, and other groups relying on credible financial data.  

Financial accounting measures three categories of business activity: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of resources needed to operate), and **operating** (transactions tied to producing and selling goods or services). These are reported through four primary **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows.

Financial reporting is governed by **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, ensuring consistency and comparability. In the U.S., the **SEC** requires public firms to prepare financial statements and delegates standard‑setting to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**; internationally, standards are guided by the **IASB** under **IFRS**. Public companies’ statements are examined by **independent auditors**, who assess compliance with GAAP and the absence of material misstatements, issuing one of four possible opinions that enhance report credibility.

The lecture centers on the **balance sheet**, which presents a company’s financial position at a specific date and must satisfy the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets are resources with future economic benefits, liabilities are obligations to creditors, and equity represents owners’ residual claims after debts are settled. Through the *Klein, Inc.* example, students see how issuing stock, borrowing funds, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and acquiring supplies each alter accounts yet keep the equation balanced. This illustrates how transactions reveal the sources and uses of company resources and how the balance sheet functions as a snapshot of overall financial position—the foundation for interpreting business activity and decision‑making.
