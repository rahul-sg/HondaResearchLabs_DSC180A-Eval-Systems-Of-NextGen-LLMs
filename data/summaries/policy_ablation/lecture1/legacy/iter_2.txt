The first week of *Principles of Accounting* introduces financial accounting as the language of business—how organizations record and communicate their financial stories to support decision‑making. Accounting serves two essential functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which provides information to external users through financial statements.

Accounting information aids many stakeholders. **Investors** and **creditors** assess performance and repayment ability, while **suppliers**, **management**, **boards of directors**, **employees**, and **customers** use this information to evaluate relationships, competitiveness, and company stability. Other users include analysts, regulators, and competitors who rely on accurate reporting for transparency and strategy.

Financial accounting captures three categories of business activity. **Financing activities** involve transactions with investors and lenders, such as issuing stock or borrowing funds. **Investing activities** include acquiring or disposing of long‑term resources—equipment, land, or inventory—that support operations. **Operating activities** encompass daily functions like producing, marketing, and selling goods and services. Together, these actions generate the data compiled in financial reports.

Information is conveyed through four key **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. These follow **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, issued by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under oversight of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**, with international standards developed by the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** under **IFRS**. Independent **auditors** examine companies’ compliance with GAAP and issue one of four opinions—unqualified, unqualified with explanatory note, qualified, or adverse—to enhance statement credibility.

Publicly traded firms communicate results periodically through standardized reports: the **10‑K** (annual, audited), **10‑Q** (quarterly, unaudited), and **8‑K** (filed for significant current events). These ensure timely disclosure to investors and regulators.

The lecture then focuses on the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific date using the equation **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets represent resources providing future benefits; liabilities are obligations to creditors; equity is owners’ residual claim. Every transaction affects at least two accounts to keep this equation balanced—illustrated through examples of issuing stock, borrowing, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and buying supplies on account. These examples demonstrate how financing, investing, and operating activities interact to maintain equilibrium and accurately portray a firm’s financial position.
