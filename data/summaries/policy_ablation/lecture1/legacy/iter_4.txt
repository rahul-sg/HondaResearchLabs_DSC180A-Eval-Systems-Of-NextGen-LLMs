The Week 1 lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the foundation of financial accounting—the system used to record, measure, and communicate a company’s economic activities to support business and investment decisions. Accounting is presented as the “language of business,” telling an organization’s financial story of operations, success, and risk. It serves two essential functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies and measures transactions in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to external parties through formal financial statements.  

Financial information assists many decision makers. **Investors**, **creditors**, and **suppliers** use it to judge profitability, repayment ability, and reliability. **Management** and **boards of directors** monitor corporate performance and strategy. **Employees** and **customers** review it to assess financial stability, while **analysts**, **regulators**, and other observers depend on standardized reporting for evaluation and oversight. The lecture emphasizes that this course focuses on **financial accounting**—external reporting of a company’s results—rather than **managerial accounting** (internal budgeting and cost decisions) or **financial statement analysis** (valuation applications).  

Financial accounting tracks three types of activity: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders), **investing** (acquiring productive resources such as equipment, land, or inventory), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production and sales). These activities generate data summarized in four interrelated **financial statements**—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows.  

Reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under the authority of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**, which mandates periodic filings for public companies. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues **IFRS**. **Auditors**, independent of management, evaluate whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements. They provide one of four opinions: unqualified, unqualified with explanatory paragraph, qualified, or adverse, adding credibility to reports. Public disclosures include annual audited **10‑K**, quarterly unaudited **10‑Q**, and event‑based **8‑K** filings.  

The lecture concludes with the **balance sheet**, which presents a company’s financial position at a specific date using the equation **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Assets are resources that yield future benefit; liabilities are obligations to creditors; and equity represents owners’ residual claims. Each transaction affects at least two accounts to maintain balance—illustrated through examples of issuing stock, borrowing funds, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and buying supplies on credit—demonstrating how financing, investing, and operating activities collectively depict a firm’s financial position.
