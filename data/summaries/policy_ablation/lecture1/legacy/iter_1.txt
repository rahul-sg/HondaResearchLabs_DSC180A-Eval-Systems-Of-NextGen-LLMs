The first week of *Principles of Accounting* introduces students to the purpose and foundation of financial accounting as the language of business. Accounting tells a company’s financial story—its successes, failures, and risks—through information that aids decision‑making. It serves two main functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms; and **communication**, which provides information to external parties through financial statements.

Users of accounting information include investors evaluating performance, creditors assessing repayment capacity, suppliers determining sales terms, managers and boards overseeing operations, and others such as employees, customers, financial analysts, regulators, and competitors. Understanding financial statements allows these users to make informed decisions about a company’s condition and strategy.

Financial accounting measures three categories of business activity. **Financing activities** involve transactions with investors and creditors, such as obtaining loans or issuing stock. **Investing activities** relate to acquiring and disposing of resources—buildings, equipment, land, or inventory—used to produce and sell products and services. **Operating activities** concern daily business functions such as production, promotion, and sales. Together, these transactions form the data that accountants measure and report.

Information is communicated through four primary financial statements: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**. Financial reporting follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** developed by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under oversight of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**; internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** establishes **IFRS** standards. Independent **auditors** evaluate whether company financial statements comply with GAAP and are free from material misstatement, issuing one of four opinions—unqualified, unqualified with explanatory note, qualified, or adverse—to enhance their credibility.

The lecture focuses on the **balance sheet**, which reports a company’s financial position at a specific date. It shows the relationship **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**, and every transaction affects at least two accounts to keep this equation balanced. Examples—including issuing stock, borrowing from a bank, purchasing equipment, prepaying rent, and acquiring supplies on account—demonstrate how resources, obligations, and ownership claims change but remain in equilibrium. Assets represent resources providing future economic benefit, liabilities reflect debts or obligations to creditors, and stockholders’ equity shows the residual ownership claim. This foundational framework underlies all further accounting topics by explaining how business transactions shape financial position and communicate economic reality.
