This lecture deepens the foundation of financial accounting by connecting the four financial statements to the accounting equation and demonstrating how double‑entry mechanics ensure balance across transactions and reporting periods. It emphasizes that translating complex economic activity into financial information requires careful judgment and consistent application of accounting principles.  

The session begins with a refresher on the **financial accounting framework**, focusing on how the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows** work together to communicate performance and position. The balance sheet reflects assets, liabilities, and equity at specific points in time (the beginning and end of the fiscal year), while the other statements describe changes over that period. Slides highlight how net income increases stockholders’ equity and losses decrease it, because **revenues raise and expenses reduce retained earnings**. Collectively, these statements explain the changes between two balance sheet dates, forming a fully linked reporting system.  

Next, the **accounting equation** is expanded from **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity** to show the internal composition of equity: **Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings**. Retained earnings evolve through **beginning balance + net income – dividends**, and net income itself derives from **revenues – expenses**. The slides then rearrange this form to present revenues, expenses, and dividends explicitly—clarifying how operations feed into equity and preserve the fundamental equation throughout each reporting period.  

The lecture then introduces **double‑entry accounting**, where every economic event has two reciprocal sides that must balance: one debit and one credit. Debits increase assets (and decrease liabilities and equity), while credits have the opposite effect. All transactions must include at least one debit and one credit, and **no negative numbers** are recorded—maintaining equality in the accounting equation. The **“DEALOR” mnemonic** (Dividends, Expenses, Assets = Debits; Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, Revenues = Credits) and the **super T‑account** summarize these relationships.  

Worked examples apply these rules: borrowing **$100,000** increases both cash and notes payable; purchasing **$60,000** of equipment decreases cash but raises assets; buying **$15,000** of inventory on account increases assets and payables; paying **$8,000** reduces each; **$600** for insurance creates prepaid insurance; issuing **10,000 shares for $100,000** increases both cash and common stock. Ending balances are computed in T‑accounts, shown on the balance sheet, and then carried forward—**each ending balance becomes the beginning balance of the next period**.  

The lecture concludes with the **accounting process cycle**: **analyze transactions → record debits and credits (journal entries) → post to T‑accounts (ledger)**, reinforcing that balanced recording underpins the accuracy and continuity of all financial statements.
