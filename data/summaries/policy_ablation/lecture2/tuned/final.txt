This lecture deepens understanding of the accounting framework by connecting financial statements to the double-entry recording system. It begins by emphasizing that economic activity is complex and accounting requires judgment to translate that activity into meaningful financial information. The four fundamental financial statements—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—serve as the core communication tools of financial accounting. The balance sheet shows a company’s financial position at a specific point in time (the beginning and end of the year), while the other statements describe activity over a fiscal period. The income statement reports revenues and expenses to determine net income, which increases stockholders’ equity, and net loss, which decreases it. The statement of stockholders’ equity explains changes in equity during the period, and the cash flow statement details cash movements between two balance sheet dates.

The lecture revisits the accounting equation—Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity—and expands it to show equity as contributed capital plus retained earnings. Retained earnings evolve according to: beginning retained earnings + net income (revenues – expenses) – dividends. This expanded form highlights how income-producing activities and dividend distributions alter the equity portion of the equation.

Building on this foundation, the lecture introduces the double-entry system: every economic event has two sides—a give and a take—and must be recorded in at least two accounts so that debits equal credits. Debits increase assets and decrease liabilities and equity; credits have the opposite effects. The “D E A L O R” memory aid helps classify accounts that normally carry debit (Dividends, Expenses, Assets) or credit (Liabilities, Owners’ equity, Revenue) balances. Several transaction examples illustrate this symmetry—borrowing from a bank, purchasing equipment or inventory, repaying suppliers, prepaying insurance, and issuing shares—showing how journal entries maintain balance and feed into T‑accounts. Ending balances from T‑accounts become beginning balances in the next period, ensuring continuity across reporting cycles.

Finally, students are reminded that transactions move through a disciplined three‑step process: **analyze** the event, **record** debits and credits as a journal entry (listing debits first and indenting credits), and **post** those entries to the ledger. These procedures uphold the central rule—**Debits = Credits**—which guarantees that the accounting equation remains balanced and financial statements remain accurate.
