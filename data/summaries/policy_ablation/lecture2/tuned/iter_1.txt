This lecture continues the accounting foundations by linking financial statements and introducing the mechanics of double-entry recordkeeping. It opens with the idea that economic activity is inherently complex, so accounting demands judgment and critical thinking when representing these events systematically.  

The first section revisits the financial accounting framework and focuses on how the four key financial statements—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—communicate financial information. The balance sheet reports a company’s financial position at a specific point in time, while the income statement, equity statement, and cash flow statement summarize performance and resource flows over a fiscal period. These statements are interconnected: revenues and expenses affect net income, which increases or decreases stockholders’ equity through retained earnings. Thus, financial statements together describe how activities across a period explain changes between two balance sheet dates.  

Building on this linkage, the lecture expands the accounting equation—Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity—showing that equity consists of contributed capital and retained earnings. Retained earnings evolve through net income (revenues minus expenses) and dividends. This expanded view clarifies how transactions across periods maintain balance and explain changes in equity over time.  

The lecture’s core topic, the double-entry system, ensures that every transaction has dual effects recorded as equal debits and credits, keeping Assets = Liabilities + Equity in equilibrium. Debits increase assets and decrease liabilities or equity; credits do the reverse. The D‑E‑A‑L‑O‑R framework (Dividends, Expenses, Assets, Liabilities, Owners’ equity, Revenue) helps determine normal debit and credit balances. Step-by-step examples—borrowing funds, buying equipment or inventory, paying suppliers, prepaying insurance, and issuing stock—illustrate how transactions are recorded in T‑accounts and journal entries.  

Once all transactions are posted, ending balances in each T‑account become beginning balances for the next accounting period. This continuation ensures consistency across reporting cycles. The lecture concludes by summarizing the recording process: analyze each transaction, journalize debits and credits, and post entries to the ledger. Upholding the rule that debits always equal credits safeguards the balance sheet equation and the reliability of the financial reporting system.
