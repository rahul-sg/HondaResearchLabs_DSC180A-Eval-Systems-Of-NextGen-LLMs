This lecture builds on the foundations of accounting by showing how the financial statements connect through the accounting equation and how transactions are recorded using double‑entry mechanics to maintain balance. It emphasizes that economic activity is complex, so accounting requires analytical judgment to capture and communicate it faithfully.  

The session first revisits the four financial statements—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—as tools for communicating a company’s financial position and performance. The balance sheet reflects resources (assets), obligations (liabilities), and equity at a specific point in time, while the income, equity, and cash‑flow statements cover activity during a fiscal period. These statements are interlinked: revenues and expenses determine net income, which raises or lowers retained earnings; that change appears on the statement of stockholders’ equity and connects the beginning and ending balance sheets. This structure demonstrates that financial statements do not exist in isolation but describe how transactions over time alter the firm’s financial position.  

The lecture then expands the accounting equation from **Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity** to **Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings**. Retained earnings evolve as **Beginning Retained Earnings + Revenues – Expenses – Dividends**, clarifying that revenues increase equity and expenses or dividends decrease it. This expansion links operational results to owners’ claims and ensures that the equation stays in equilibrium.  

Next, the double‑entry framework is presented: every economic event has two sides—a give and a take—so each transaction affects at least two accounts. Debits (left) and credits (right) are used to record increases and decreases, ensuring **Debits = Credits**. Assets normally increase with debits, while liabilities and equity increase with credits. Examples illustrate each step:  
- Borrowing $100,000 from a bank raises assets (cash) and liabilities (notes payable).  
- Buying equipment for $60,000 cash exchanges one asset for another.  
- Purchasing $15,000 inventory on account increases assets and liabilities.  
- Paying $8,000 to a supplier reduces both cash and the payable.  
- Paying $600 for insurance substitutes cash for a prepaid asset.  
- Issuing $100,000 of stock increases both cash and contributed capital.  

T‑accounts show how entries accumulate: **Beginning Balance + increases – decreases = Ending Balance (EB)**. These ending balances form the balance sheet totals and become beginning balances for the next period, demonstrating continuity across accounting cycles.  

Finally, transactions are processed in three steps: (1) analyze the event, (2) record the journal entry with debit first and indented credit second, and (3) post entries to the ledger to determine updated balances. Maintaining **Debits = Credits** keeps the accounting equation **A = L + SE** in balance and underlies reliable financial reporting.
