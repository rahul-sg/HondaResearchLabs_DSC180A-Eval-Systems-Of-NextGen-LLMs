This lecture deepens understanding of how financial statements connect through the accounting equation and how transactions are recorded under the double‑entry system to preserve that balance. It stresses that economic activity is complex and recording it accurately requires critical judgment.  

The session begins by revisiting the four financial statements—balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—as the means through which firms communicate financial position and performance. The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities, and equity at a point in time, while the other statements cover activity during a fiscal period. They are interdependent: revenues and expenses determine net income, which adjusts retained earnings on the statement of stockholders’ equity and links the beginning and ending balance sheets. This linkage demonstrates that statements depict how transactions over time reshape a company’s financial position.  

The accounting equation, **Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity**, is then expanded to show equity as **Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings**, and retained earnings as **Beginning Retained Earnings + Revenues – Expenses – Dividends**. This expansion clarifies that revenues increase equity, while expenses and dividends reduce it.  

Next, the double‑entry framework illustrates that every economic event has two sides—a give and a take—so each transaction affects at least two accounts. Recording follows debit (left) and credit (right) conventions: assets increase with debits, liabilities and equity with credits. Key rules emphasize that no negative numbers are used, every entry includes both a debit and a credit, and the total debits must always equal total credits to maintain **A = L + SE**. The mnemonic **DEALOR** helps recall which accounts carry debit or credit balances (Dividends, Expenses, Assets = Debit; Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, Revenue = Credit). Simple examples demonstrate these mechanics: obtaining a bank loan raises both cash and notes payable; acquiring equipment exchanges one asset for another; inventory purchases on credit increase assets and liabilities; payments to suppliers and for insurance decrease cash; and issuing stock boosts both cash and contributed capital.  

T‑accounts and journal entries summarize transactions and track how balances evolve: **Beginning Balance + increases – decreases = Ending Balance**. Ending balances populate the balance sheet and become next period’s beginning balances, forming the starting point for the accounting cycle. Overall, the lecture shows that maintaining **Debits = Credits** anchors accurate financial reporting and preserves equilibrium in the accounting equation.
