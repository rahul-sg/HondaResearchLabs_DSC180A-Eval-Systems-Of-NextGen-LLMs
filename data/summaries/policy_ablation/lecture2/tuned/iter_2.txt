This lecture continues the foundations of accounting by connecting financial statements to the double‑entry system that ensures all transactions maintain balance in the accounting equation. It begins by emphasizing that economic activity is complex and must be represented with judgment and critical reasoning.  

The first section reviews how the four main financial statements—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—communicate a company’s financial condition and performance. The balance sheet captures financial position at specific points in time, while the other three summarize activity across a fiscal year. These statements are interlinked: revenues and expenses determine net income, which flows into retained earnings and changes stockholders’ equity. This linkage explains how the flow of resources and results over a period connects two balance sheet dates.  

Next, the lecture extends the accounting equation from **Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity** to show that equity comprises contributed capital and retained earnings. Retained earnings increase with net income and decrease with dividends; expressed in full, **Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + (Beginning Retained Earnings + Revenues – Expenses – Dividends)**. This expansion clarifies how revenues raise and expenses lower equity, reinforcing that every element must stay in balance throughout the period.  

The core segment introduces double‑entry recordkeeping. Every economic event has two sides—a give and a take—so each is recorded with equal debits and credits. Debits increase assets and decrease liabilities and equity; credits have the opposite effect. The **D‑E‑A‑L‑O‑R** guide (Dividends, Expenses, Assets = normally debit; Liabilities, Owner’s equity, Revenues = normally credit) supports this logic. Step‑by‑step examples, such as borrowing cash, purchasing equipment, buying inventory on account, paying suppliers, purchasing insurance, and issuing stock, illustrate how transactions are posted in T‑accounts so that debits always equal credits.  

Detailed slides demonstrate that ending balances (EB) in T‑accounts follow the relation **Beginning Balance + increases – decreases = Ending Balance**. These ending amounts appear on the balance sheet and become the next period’s beginning balances, showing the continuity of accounting periods.  

The lecture concludes with the systematic recording process: (1) analyze each transaction, (2) record the journal entry—placing the debit first and indenting the credit line—and (3) post those entries to the ledger to determine account balances. Adhering to **Debits = Credits** preserves the equilibrium **A = L + SE** and underpins the reliability of financial reporting.
