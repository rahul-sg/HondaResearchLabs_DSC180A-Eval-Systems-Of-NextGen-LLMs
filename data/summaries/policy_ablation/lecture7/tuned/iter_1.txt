This lecture focused on within-subjects designs as a methodological alternative to between-groups designs. A between-groups design compares different groups, with each participant contributing data for only one condition—an approach that introduces variability due to individual differences. A within-subjects design, in contrast, tests each participant in all conditions and compares each person to themselves. This comparison reduces participant-related noise, requires fewer participants to achieve equivalent statistical power, and yields more sensitive tests of experimental effects.

Several specific forms of within-subjects designs were introduced. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more experimental conditions within the same session, such as testing perceptual accuracy at different levels of stimulus difficulty. A **longitudinal design** observes the same individuals over extended time intervals, offering rich developmental insights but requiring lengthy data collection periods. All of these designs benefit from reduced nuisance variability and increased efficiency.

However, within-subjects designs also introduce potential complications. Because participants experience multiple conditions, they may become aware of the manipulation (demand characteristics) or exhibit **carryover effects**, where earlier experiences influence later responses. Carryover effects can take several forms: fatigue (poorer performance from tiredness), practice (improvement through learning), history (external events coinciding with the study), maturation (changes due to time or biological development), and testing effects (familiarity with a repeated test). Order effects—when the sequence of conditions alters performance—can further confound outcomes.

To manage these influences, researchers often employ **counterbalancing**, varying the order of conditions across participants. **Complete counterbalancing** techniques include the ABBA design for two conditions and **block randomization** when there are three or more conditions. When it is impractical for every participant to experience all possible orders, **incomplete counterbalancing** offers feasible alternatives like random order with rotation or **Latin-square designs**, ensuring each condition appears in every position and follows the others equally often.

Slides also illustrated real-world applications, such as the study comparing **spaced versus regular text** for children with dyslexia—an example highlighting how within-subjects and counterbalancing logic guide experimental control. In cognitive science tasks with numerous short trials, researchers sometimes intermix conditions randomly to neutralize order effects. Yet, counterbalancing has clear limits: some manipulations cannot be reversed, such as drug administration, surgical procedures, or once-only experiences. Overall, within-subjects designs are efficient and powerful but demand thoughtful handling of order, carryover, and uncontrollable effects to maintain internal validity.
