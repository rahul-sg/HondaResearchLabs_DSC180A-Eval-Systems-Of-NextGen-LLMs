This lecture examined **within-subjects designs**, which differ from **between-groups designs** in how they handle individual differences. In between-groups studies, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so variation among participants adds noise and requires larger samples to detect effects. In a within-subjects design, each participant experiences all conditions and is compared to themselves across those conditions. This comparison reduces nuisance variability and yields a more powerful test—20 participants can provide 20 data points per condition, rather than needing 40 or more.

Several common within-subjects formats were covered. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** involves two or more conditions within a session, such as several stimulus difficulty levels. A **longitudinal design** measures the same individuals over time, providing rich data but often proceeding slowly.

The lecture emphasized that while within-subjects designs are efficient, they bring specific challenges. When participants experience every condition, they may guess the study’s purpose (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects**—performance in one condition influencing the next through **fatigue** or **practice**. Additional time-related threats include **history effects** (outside events such as exams or holidays altering performance), **maturation effects** (changes from aging or development rather than the manipulation), and **testing effects** (improvements or strategy shifts from repeated testing). Together these produce **order effects**, where condition sequence itself distorts results.

To reduce such problems, researchers use **counterbalancing**. In **complete within-subjects designs**, every participant experiences all possible condition orders—for example, the **ABBA design** (controlling for linear practice effects) or **block randomization** when more than two conditions are present. **Incomplete within-subjects designs** expose each participant to all conditions but not every possible order. Methods include **random order with rotation** and the **Latin-square design**, which ensure each condition appears equally often in each position and after every other condition while minimizing participant burden.

In cognitive science tasks with many brief trials, conditions may instead be **intermixed randomly**, which effectively cancels order effects without formal counterbalancing. However, counterbalancing cannot address irreversible manipulations—you cannot un-take a drug, undo a surgery, or cause someone to “unlearn” a technique.  

Overall, within-subjects designs provide powerful, efficient comparisons but require careful control of order, time, and carryover effects.
