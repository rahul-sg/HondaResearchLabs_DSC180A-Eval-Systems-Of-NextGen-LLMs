Within-subjects designs offer a powerful alternative to between-groups designs by allowing each participant to serve as their own comparison. In a between-groups design, participants contribute data to only one condition, so individual differences—such as ability or motivation—introduce noise that can obscure effects. Within-subjects designs reduce this nuisance variability because every participant experiences all conditions, and analyses focus on differences within each person. This approach increases statistical power and efficiency, enabling researchers to detect effects with fewer participants.

Several major forms of within-subjects designs were covered. **Pretest–posttest designs** assess change across an intervention, **repeated‑measures designs** expose participants to multiple conditions within one session, and **longitudinal designs** assess repeated measurements over extended periods. These approaches are especially useful when changes occur within individuals over time, but longitudinal work can be slow and resource‑intensive.

The benefits of within-subjects designs—fewer participants and lower variability—come with important challenges. Because participants experience all conditions, they may guess an experiment’s purpose (**demand characteristics**) or exhibit **carryover effects**, where one condition influences the next. Carryover effects can reflect **fatigue** (decline in later tasks), **practice** (improvement from repetition), or external and temporal confounds such as **history**, **maturation**, and **testing** effects. For example, events between sessions or natural development might mimic an experimental effect, while taking the same test twice can alter performance through familiarity.

**Order effects** arise when the sequence of conditions itself affects outcomes. Researchers manage these by **counterbalancing**—systematically varying the order of conditions across participants. In **complete within‑subjects designs**, every participant experiences all possible orders, such as the **ABBA design** for two conditions or **block randomization** when there are three or more. However, the number of possible sequences grows factorially (e.g., 3! = 6 orders, 4! = 24, 5! = 120), making complete counterbalancing impractical for many conditions.

When full counterbalancing is infeasible, **incomplete within‑subjects designs** offer efficient alternatives. Methods like **random order with rotation** assign each participant a unique starting point so that all conditions appear equally often in each position. **Latin‑square designs** further ensure that each condition occupies every ordinal position and follows each other condition equally often, balancing order effects while limiting the number of participants required.

In cognitive science research, tasks with many short trials may instead **intermix** conditions randomly, which tends to cancel out order effects. Yet counterbalancing remains preferable when tasks are long or complex. Some manipulations—like taking a drug, undergoing surgery, or learning a specific skill—cannot be undone, setting practical limits on counterbalancing. Altogether, within-subjects designs provide an efficient, statistically sensitive framework for comparing conditions, provided researchers actively control for carryover and ordering influences.
