This lecture examined **within-subjects designs** as a contrast to **between-groups designs**. In a between-groups design, each participant appears in only one condition, so individual differences add noise that can obscure effects—requiring many participants to achieve stable results. In a within-subjects design, every participant contributes data to every condition and is compared to themselves. This reduces participant-related variability and thus increases statistical power; for example, to get 20 data points per condition, only 20 participants are needed instead of 40.  

Three major forms were highlighted. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more conditions within one session (e.g., different dot-motion coherence levels). A **longitudinal design** measures the same people over multiple time points—highly informative but often time-consuming.  

Although efficient and powerful, within-subjects studies introduce potential **confounds**. When participants receive all conditions, **demand characteristics** may occur if they infer the experiment’s purpose. **Carryover effects** appear when one condition influences the next, producing **fatigue** (performance worsens), **practice** (performance improves), or other sequencing effects. Additional time-based threats include the **history effect**—outside events interfering with performance (e.g., midterms, major campus or world events); the **maturation effect**—changes due to natural development rather than the manipulation (e.g., vocabulary growth in children or hearing decline with age); and the **testing effect**—getting the same test twice can change performance through familiarity or expectation. Collectively these factors produce **order effects**, so the order and timing of conditions must be managed carefully.  

The lecture then introduced **counterbalancing** as a key remedy. In **complete within-subjects designs**, each participant experiences all possible condition orders—for example, an **ABBA design** with two conditions or **block randomization** for three or more (where three conditions yield six possible orders, 3! = ABC, ACB, BAC, BCA, CAB, CBA). In **incomplete within-subjects designs**, participants receive every condition but not all orders, such as **random order with rotation** or a **Latin-square design**, which ensures each condition appears equally often in each position. In cognitive science tasks with many short trials, experimenters may instead **randomly intermix** trials to distribute order effects naturally. However, some manipulations (e.g., drug administration, surgery, or teaching a memory technique) cannot be undone, limiting counterbalancing options.  

Overall, within-subjects designs provide strong efficiency and power but require thoughtful control of order, carryover, and time-related effects to ensure valid conclusions.
