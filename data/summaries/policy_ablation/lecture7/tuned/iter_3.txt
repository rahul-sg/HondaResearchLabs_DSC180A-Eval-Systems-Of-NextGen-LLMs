This lecture explored **within-subjects designs** as an alternative to between-groups approaches. In a between-groups design, each person provides data for only one condition, and differences across participants introduce noise that can obscure true effects. In contrast, a within-subjects design has each participant complete all conditions and be compared to themselves, reducing participant-related variability. This efficiency means researchers need fewer participants to achieve the same data points per condition and can conduct more powerful statistical tests.

Several specific forms of within-subjects designs were discussed. A **pretest–posttest design** measures each participant before and after an intervention to assess change. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more within-session conditions, such as testing at multiple stimulus levels. A **longitudinal design** measures the same individuals over a long time period, providing valuable information about development or change but requiring substantial time and commitment.

While within-subjects designs offer efficiency and control, they also create potential challenges. When participants experience all conditions, **demand characteristics** may arise if they infer the study’s purpose, and **carryover effects** can occur when experience in one condition influences the next. Examples include **fatigue** (declining performance), **practice** (improvement through repetition), and time-based **history**, **maturation**, or **testing effects**, where external events, natural development, or prior testing alter results. **Order effects**—changes due solely to condition sequence—represent a key concern.

Researchers manage order effects using **counterbalancing**, systematically varying the sequence of conditions. In **complete counterbalancing**, each participant experiences all possible orders, such as an **ABBA design** for two conditions or **block randomization** for three or more. When full counterbalancing is infeasible, **incomplete designs** like **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square designs** distribute conditions evenly across positions and combinations.

In cognitive science tasks with many short trials, researchers may **intermix** conditions randomly. Intermixing can largely cancel out order effects but is most suitable when trials are numerous and brief; counterbalancing is preferred when intermixing would confuse participants or when few or lengthy items are used. Counterbalancing has practical limits—certain manipulations cannot be undone, such as administering a drug, teaching a memory technique, or exposing participants to a website once. Overall, within-subjects designs provide strong control over participant variability but require careful management of sequencing and time-related confounds.
