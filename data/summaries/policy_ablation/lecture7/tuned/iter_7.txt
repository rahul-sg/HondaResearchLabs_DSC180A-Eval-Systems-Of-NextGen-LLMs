This lecture focused on **within-subjects designs**, contrasting them with **between-groups designs**. In between-groups studies, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so individual differences add variability (“noise”) that can obscure effects and require larger samples. In a within-subjects design, each participant experiences all conditions and is compared to themselves, which removes much of this participant-related noise and provides a more powerful test. For example, collecting 20 data points per condition requires only 20 participants instead of 40.  

Several forms of within-subjects design were described. A **pretest–posttest design** involves measuring participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more conditions within a session (e.g., several levels of stimulus difficulty). A **longitudinal design** tracks the same individuals across time points—especially informative but potentially slow to complete.  

While efficient, within-subjects designs can introduce **confounds**. When participants experience all conditions, **demand characteristics** may develop if they infer the study’s purpose. **Carryover effects** occur if performance in one condition influences the next, creating **fatigue** (performance decline) or **practice** (performance improvement). The lecture’s “Condition A…Condition B…” example illustrated how running conditions in a fixed order can confound results with time or sequence. Additional temporal threats include the **history effect** (external events such as exams or world news altering performance), **maturation effect** (changes due to natural growth or aging), and **testing effect** (familiarity or strategy changes from repeated testing). Together these produce **order effects**, meaning that condition order itself can bias results.  

To control order effects, the lecture introduced **counterbalancing**. In **complete within-subjects designs**, each participant experiences all possible condition orders—for instance, an **ABBA design** with two conditions or **block randomization** for three or more. In **incomplete within-subjects designs**, all participants experience every condition but not every order. Methods such as **random order with rotation** or a **Latin-square design** distribute each condition evenly across positions while reducing participant burden compared with complete designs.  

In cognitive science experiments with many brief trials, researchers may instead **intermix** conditions randomly to cancel order effects naturally. However, counterbalancing has limits for manipulations that cannot be reversed (e.g., administering a drug or teaching a memory technique).  

Overall, within-subjects designs provide efficient, powerful comparisons while requiring careful management of order, carryover, and time-related effects.
