This lecture introduced **within-subjects designs** as an alternative to **between-groups designs**. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so individual differences between groups can add noise and make effects harder to detect. Within-subjects designs instead have each participant experience all conditions, allowing each person to be compared to themselves. By reducing participant-related variability, these designs yield cleaner data and therefore more powerful tests of experimental effects—without needing as many participants to obtain the same number of data points per condition.

The lecture outlined several common forms. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more related conditions measured within a session. A **longitudinal design** follows the same individuals over extended periods to observe change over time. Each approach involves comparing participants to themselves, but their timelines, durations, and research goals differ.

Despite their advantages, within-subjects designs bring challenges. Because all participants encounter all conditions, **demand characteristics** can occur if individuals guess the study’s purpose. **Carryover effects** may emerge when one condition influences another through fatigue, practice, or lingering exposure. Additional **history**, **maturation**, and **testing effects** occur when outside events, natural development, or familiarity with a test alter results. Such **order effects**, where performance changes simply because of sequence, must be carefully managed. For instance, in a study comparing 4- and 7-year-olds’ comprehension of accents, the sequence of speech types could affect outcomes if not controlled.

To address order effects, researchers use **counterbalancing**. In **complete within-subjects designs**, every participant experiences all possible condition orders—such as an **ABBA design** (for two conditions) or **block randomization** (for three or more). When full counterbalancing is impractical, **incomplete designs** like **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square designs** distribute condition sequences evenly across participants so each condition appears equally often in each position.

In cognitive science studies with many short trials, conditions may instead be **randomly intermixed**, which tends to cancel order effects. Intermixing is best when tasks are brief and numerous, while counterbalancing suits longer or potentially confusing tasks. Finally, some manipulations—like taking a drug, undergoing surgery, or learning a technique—cannot be reversed, limiting counterbalancing. Overall, within-subjects designs efficiently reduce variability and increase sensitivity, but they require mindful control of order and time-related confounds.
