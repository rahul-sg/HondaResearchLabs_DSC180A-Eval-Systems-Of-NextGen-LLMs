This lecture focused on **within-subjects designs** as a counterpart to **between-groups designs**. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so differences among participants add noise that can obscure effects. A within-subjects design instead measures each participant in every condition, comparing each person to themselves. This approach removes some participant-related variability, often allowing researchers to detect effects more efficiently and with fewer participants—though its benefits depend on effective control of new sources of variability introduced by repeating conditions.

Several key forms were highlighted. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more related conditions within the same session. A **longitudinal design** observes the same individuals over multiple time points. All share the core logic of comparing individuals to themselves but differ in time scale and purpose.

Within-subjects designs introduce potential **confounds** that require attention. When participants encounter all conditions, **demand characteristics** may arise if they infer the study’s purpose. **Carryover effects** occur when experience in one condition influences another—through **fatigue**, **practice**, or lingering exposure. Additional time-related influences include the **history effect** (outside events coincide with measurement), **maturation effect** (natural change over time), and **testing effect** (performance altered by prior testing). These sources of bias can create **order effects**, where results differ simply because of the sequence or timing of conditions. For example, the lecture’s child speech–accent study invited students to consider whether it was a within- or between-subjects design and whether participants experienced all orders—a reminder that order structure is central to design choice.

**Counterbalancing** offers solutions. In **complete within-subjects** designs, each participant experiences all possible condition orders—using methods such as an **ABBA design** for two conditions or **block randomization** for three or more. **Incomplete within-subjects** approaches, like **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square designs**, distribute condition sequences so each appears equally often across participants. In cognitive science tasks with many brief trials, researchers may instead **randomly intermix** conditions to cancel out order effects, though counterbalancing is preferred when tasks are few or potentially confusing. Some manipulations—such as taking a drug or learning a skill—cannot be undone, limiting counterbalancing. Overall, within-subjects designs increase efficiency and power, but careful control of order and time-related effects is essential to maintain validity.
