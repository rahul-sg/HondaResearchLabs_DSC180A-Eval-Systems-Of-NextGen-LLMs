This lecture examined **within-subjects designs** as an alternative to between-groups approaches. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so differences among participants add variability—or noise—that can obscure true effects. In a within-subjects design, each participant completes all conditions and is compared to themselves, helping remove participant-related variability. This efficiency means fewer participants are needed to achieve the same number of data points per condition and reduces nuisance variability.

The lecture introduced several specific forms of within-subjects designs. In a **pretest–posttest design**, each participant is measured twice—before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more conditions tested within the same session, such as different levels of a stimulus attribute. A **longitudinal design** measures the same individuals across multiple time points, yielding valuable information about change over time but requiring extensive data collection. Each of these designs illustrates how comparing people to themselves simplifies interpretation by minimizing noise.

However, within-subjects designs present potential complications. When participants experience all conditions, they may discern the study’s purpose (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects**, where exposure to one condition influences subsequent performance. Examples include fatigue (worse performance from tiredness), practice (improvement through learning), and confounding changes over time such as **history**, **maturation**, or **testing effects**. **Order effects**—when results vary depending on the sequence of conditions—pose additional challenges.

To control for order effects, researchers use **counterbalancing**, systematically varying condition sequences. **Complete counterbalancing** methods include ABBA designs for two conditions, which help control gradual practice effects, and **block randomization** for three or more conditions, where all possible orderings are presented. When full counterbalancing is impractical, **incomplete counterbalancing** provides alternatives: **random order with rotation** distributes each condition across positions, and **Latin-square designs** ensure each condition appears equally often in every position and follows every other condition in turn.

In cognitive science experiments with many short trials, an additional approach is to **intermix** conditions randomly. Intermixing essentially cancels out order effects and is often used unless it would confuse participants or when items are lengthy or limited. Still, counterbalancing has limits—some manipulations (like taking a drug or learning a skill) cannot be reversed. Overall, within-subjects designs offer efficiency and powerful control over participant variability but require careful management of order- and time-related confounds.
