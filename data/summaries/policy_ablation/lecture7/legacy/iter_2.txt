This lecture introduces within-subjects designs as a way to reduce nuisance variability compared to between-groups designs. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, and differences among participants create noise that can obscure real effects. In a within-subjects design, each participant completes all experimental conditions, allowing direct comparison of their own performance across those conditions. This approach removes much of the variability due to individual differences, increases statistical power, and requires fewer participants overall.

Several specific types illustrate how within-subjects designs are implemented. A pretest–posttest design includes two conditions (pretest and posttest), with an intervention in between. Repeated-measures designs involve two or more conditions—such as different levels of task difficulty—to examine how the same individuals respond to multiple manipulations. Longitudinal designs measure participants at multiple time points to capture developmental or long-term change, though they require extensive data collection. Overall benefits include efficiency and more powerful detection of effects.

However, within-subjects designs face potential drawbacks because participants experience all conditions. Demand characteristics may lead them to guess the purpose and adjust their behavior. Carryover effects can occur when one condition influences the next: fatigue can lower performance, while practice can enhance it. Additional confounds include the *history effect* (external events influencing results), *maturation effect* (natural changes over time rather than the manipulation), and *testing effect* (participants performing differently on a repeated test due to familiarity or expectations).

To handle order effects that arise from condition sequencing, researchers use counterbalancing methods. Complete counterbalancing includes all possible orders—for example, an ABBA design for two conditions can offset slow, linear practice effects but not immediate ones. Block randomization extends this to three or more conditions by presenting every possible order in randomized blocks. When full counterbalancing is impractical, incomplete designs like random rotation or Latin-square arrangements ensure balanced exposure: each condition appears equally often in every position and after every other condition. These setups require participant counts to be multiples of the number of conditions. In cognitive science, order effects can also be minimized by randomly intermixing many brief trials, provided doing so is not confusing. Finally, counterbalancing has limits—irreversible experiences such as taking a drug or learning a memory technique cannot be undone.
