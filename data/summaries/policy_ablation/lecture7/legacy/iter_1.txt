This lecture introduces within-subjects designs as an alternative to between-groups designs for reducing nuisance variability in experiments. In between-groups designs, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so individual differences add noise that can obscure true effects. In within-subjects designs, each participant experiences all conditions, allowing direct comparison of their own performance across conditions. This approach eliminates much of the variability linked to participant differences, increases statistical power, and reduces the total number of participants required.

Several common variants illustrate how within-subjects designs can be structured. A pretest–posttest design compares participants’ scores before and after an intervention. A repeated-measures design includes two or more conditions—such as varying levels of task difficulty—within a session. A longitudinal design collects data at multiple time points, providing rich insights into change over time but often requiring lengthy data collection. Benefits include efficiency and stronger detection of effects due to reduced noise.

Potential drawbacks arise from participants completing all conditions. Demand characteristics may cause participants to guess the study’s purpose and alter their responses. Carryover effects occur when earlier conditions influence later performance: fatigue can lower performance, practice can improve it, and external or temporal factors such as history, maturation, or testing effects can confound results. For example, improvements might actually reflect natural development or familiarity with a repeated test rather than an experimental manipulation.

The lecture also highlighted applied examples of these issues, such as assessing whether children understand speech better in their own accent or whether extra letter spacing improves reading in dyslexia—cases that require choosing whether all participants experience all conditions (within-subjects) and how to manage order presentation.

Order effects pose a key methodological challenge. One strategy is counterbalancing—systematically varying or randomizing the order of conditions to distribute sequence influences equally. Complete within-subjects counterbalancing can use an ABBA design, which offsets linear or slow practice effects but cannot control rapid ones, or block randomization, which cycles through all possible condition orders. When all orders are impractical, incomplete methods such as random rotation or Latin-square designs ensure each condition appears equally often in each position and after every other condition. In cognitive science, researchers sometimes intermix conditions randomly across many trials to cancel order effects unless tasks are long or confusing. Counterbalancing, however, has limits—it cannot reverse irreversible conditions like drugs, surgeries, or unique experiences.
