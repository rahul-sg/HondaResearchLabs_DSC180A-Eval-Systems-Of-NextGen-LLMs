This lecture explored **within-subjects designs**, contrasting them with **between-groups designs**. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so individual differences across participants add nuisance variability that can obscure real effects. A within-subjects design instead tests each participant in all conditions and compares each person to themselves across those conditions. This approach helps remove participant-related noise, makes statistical tests more powerful, and requires far fewer participants for the same number of data points per condition.  

The lecture outlined several forms of within-subjects designs. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants both before and after an intervention. **Repeated-measures designs** include two or more conditions—for instance, varying levels of a stimulus such as motion coherence. **Longitudinal designs** measure participants across extended time spans to examine change over time. Each type offers efficiency and reduced variability relative to between-groups approaches.  

While effective, within-subjects designs can face specific challenges. **Demand characteristics** may occur when participants experience all conditions and infer the purpose of the study. **Carryover effects** arise when exposure to one condition (A) alters performance in the next (B)—for example through **fatigue** or **practice**. Broader time-linked threats include **history effects** (external events like exams or world events), **maturation effects** (changes due to aging or time rather than the intervention), and **testing effects** (where retaking the same test influences later performance).  

**Order effects** occur when the sequence of conditions affects results. To manage this, researchers use **counterbalancing**, varying order across participants. In **complete within-subjects designs**, every possible condition order is presented. The **ABBA design** (for two conditions) balances practice effects when learning changes gradually, while **block randomization** (for three or more conditions) arranges all condition orders randomly within repeated blocks. When full counterbalancing is impractical, **incomplete designs** are used. In the **random order with rotation** method, each participant experiences all conditions but only one rotated order; the number of participants must be a multiple of the number of conditions (e.g., 5, 10, or 15 for five conditions). The **Latin-square design** ensures each condition appears equally often in every position and follows every other condition equally often.  

In fields like cognitive science, where tasks involve many noisy trials, researchers may instead **intermix** easy and hard trials to cancel order effects, reserving counterbalancing when conditions are few or long. However, some manipulations—such as taking a drug or undergoing training—cannot be undone, limiting the use of counterbalancing.
