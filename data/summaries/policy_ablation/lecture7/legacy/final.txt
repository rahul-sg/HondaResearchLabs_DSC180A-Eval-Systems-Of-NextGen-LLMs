This lecture introduced **within-subjects designs**, contrasting them with **between-groups designs**. In a between-groups design, each participant is tested in only one condition, so individual differences add nuisance variability (“noise”) that can obscure real effects. In a within-subjects design, each participant experiences all conditions and is compared to themselves across those conditions. This approach removes much of the participant-related noise, yields a more powerful test, and requires far fewer participants for the same number of data points per condition.

Several forms of within-subjects designs were discussed. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** tests several conditions—such as different stimulus levels—within one session. A **longitudinal design** measures participants over longer time spans to track change. These designs share the advantages of efficiency and reduced variability. The dyslexia **letter-spacing study** illustrated how a within-subjects approach lets each child’s reading performance under spaced and regular text be directly compared. Similarly, the **accent comprehension example** with 4‑ and 7‑year‑olds demonstrated testing multiple conditions within the same participants.

The lecture emphasized that within-subjects designs are often “just good experimental design,” but they introduce potential challenges. **Demand characteristics** can arise when participants become aware of the manipulation. **Carryover effects** may occur if one condition influences performance in another—through **fatigue** or **practice**. Broader time-related threats include **history effects** (external events like exams or major news), **maturation effects** (changes due to age or time rather than the intervention), and **testing effects** (improvements from repeated exposure to test materials).

**Order effects** occur when condition sequence itself affects results. These are commonly addressed through **counterbalancing**—varying the order of conditions across participants. In **complete within-subjects designs**, every possible order is used. The **ABBA design** (for two conditions) controls for slow practice effects, while **block randomization** (for three or more conditions) randomizes sequences of all conditions once each. When full counterbalancing is impractical, **incomplete designs** like **random order with rotation** or the **Latin-square design** ensure that each condition appears equally often in each position and follows every other condition equally often. In cognitive science, researchers sometimes intermix trials to cancel out order effects, reserving counterbalancing for tasks where mixing would confuse participants or when each trial is long. Some manipulations—like taking a drug or learning a skill—cannot be undone, setting clear **limits to counterbalancing**.
