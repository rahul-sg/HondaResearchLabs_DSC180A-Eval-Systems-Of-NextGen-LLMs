This lecture traced how natural selection and genetic inheritance together account for evolutionary change, progressing from Darwin’s foundational ideas to the molecular mechanisms that sustain variation. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, supported by three postulates: organisms compete for existence, they vary in traits tied to survival and reproduction, and these variations are heritable. Darwin recognized the importance of variation but could not fully explain its source or persistence. Later genetic discoveries provided these missing mechanisms.  

Artificial selection in domesticated species, such as dogs and pigeons, demonstrated that humans can produce striking changes within a few generations, illustrating the power of inheritance and selection. Evolutionary change in nature can also proceed rapidly or gradually; for instance, minnows evolved placenta‑like structures quickly, whereas the eye evolved through approximately 1,800 small steps over about one million years, showing how incremental modification can yield complex traits.  

Gregor Mendel’s pea‑plant experiments (1856–1863) showed that traits are inherited through discrete “particles,” later identified as genes, which maintain variation across generations. Around the same time, Walther Flemming discovered chromatin and mitosis, and Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri connected chromosomes to heredity in the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Chromosomes—bundles of DNA occurring in homologous pairs—carry genetic material transmitted through cell division.  

Mitosis produces identical diploid somatic cells, while meiosis produces haploid gametes, halving chromosome number so that fertilization restores the full set. Variation arises through mutation and recombination during meiosis. Mendel distinguished genotype (genetic makeup) from phenotype (observable traits) and demonstrated dominant versus recessive inheritance using crosses predicted by Punnett squares. Linkage and crossing over explain why genes close together on a chromosome may not assort independently. The recombination rate—the frequency with which a single chromosomal crossing occurs between two genes during meiosis—quantifies this process, with crossing‑over generating new gene combinations and added variation.  

At the molecular level, DNA is a double helix with a sugar‑phosphate backbone and paired bases (A‑T, C‑G). Genes are functional DNA sequences, while mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), inherited only from mothers and lacking recombination, is shorter and highly conserved. Comparing DNA sequences enables estimation of evolutionary timing through molecular clocks based on neutral sites. mtDNA also identifies “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all humans, who lived roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
