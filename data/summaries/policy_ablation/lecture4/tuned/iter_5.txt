This lecture connected Darwin’s theory of natural selection with the genetic and molecular mechanisms that make evolution possible. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Darwin proposed that individuals struggle to survive, vary in traits related to survival and reproduction, and pass on these traits to offspring. Although he recognized variation as essential, he could not explain how it was inherited or maintained. Artificial selection in domesticated species, such as dogs and pigeons, demonstrated that strong selection could produce major changes in few generations, mirroring natural selection. Evolutionary rates vary, from rapid adaptations like placenta-like structures in minnows to the gradual evolution of complex organs such as the eye through many tiny, incremental steps.

The genetic foundation of inheritance clarified how variation persists. Gregor Mendel’s pea plant experiments showed that traits are transmitted as discrete “particles” from each parent, preserving variation rather than blending it. The early 1900s rediscovery of his work led to studies on chromosomes. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and later, Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri demonstrated that heredity resides in chromosomes—the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans have 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 homologous pairs, one of which determines sex.

Understanding cell division was essential to understanding inheritance. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells for growth, while meiosis generates haploid gametes, maintaining consistent chromosome numbers across generations. Variation arises through mutation and recombination during meiosis. Mendel’s principles distinguished genotype (genetic code) from phenotype (physical expression), with dominant alleles masking recessive ones. Linkage and recombination studies further refined inheritance models: genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but those located close together may be inherited as linked traits. Crossing-over during meiosis can break such linkages, producing new gene combinations. The recombination rate measures how often a crossing-over event occurs between two genes during meiosis.

Molecular discoveries later pinpointed DNA as the hereditary material. Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images enabled Watson and Crick to describe the double‑helix structure in 1953, establishing how structure explains replication, protein coding, and how DNA comparisons reveal evolutionary timelines. DNA has a sugar‑phosphate backbone with base pairs (A‑T, C‑G). Mitochondrial DNA, maternally inherited without recombination, has been used to identify “Mitochondrial Eve”—the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of all humans about 100–200 thousand years ago. Comparisons of neutral DNA sites serve as a molecular clock for estimating species divergence.
