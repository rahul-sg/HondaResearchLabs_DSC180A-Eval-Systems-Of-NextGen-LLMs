This lecture examined how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change, moving from Darwin’s observations to the molecular foundations of heredity. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, supported by three postulates: organisms struggle for existence, vary in traits linked to survival and reproduction, and pass these variations to offspring. While Darwin identified variation as the basis of natural selection, he could not explain how it was generated or maintained. Later discoveries in genetics supplied these mechanisms. Artificial selection in domesticated species, such as dogs and pigeons, showed how targeted breeding could produce significant changes within just a few generations, contrasting with the gradual rates of natural evolution seen in examples like minnows developing placenta-like structures or the eye evolving through numerous small steps over about a million years.  

Gregor Mendel’s pea plant experiments between 1856–1863 revealed that inheritance operates through discrete particles—genes—transmitted from each parent and preserving variation. Early twentieth-century work connected these factors to cell structures: Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, while Sutton and Boveri formulated the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, recognizing chromosomes as the physical carriers of Mendel’s factors.  

Understanding cell division clarified how hereditary material is transmitted. Mitosis produces identical diploid somatic cells, whereas meiosis forms haploid gametes, halving chromosome number and introducing variation through mutation and recombination. Mendel distinguished genotype (genetic makeup) from phenotype (observable traits), with dominant alleles masking recessive ones; crosses among heterozygotes and analyses with Punnett squares demonstrated predictable inheritance patterns. Linkage and crossing over during meiosis further explain why some genes are inherited together, and recombination rate measures the frequency of such exchanges.  

The molecular basis of inheritance was uncovered when Watson and Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images, described DNA’s double‑helix structure: a sugar‑phosphate backbone with complementary base pairs (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional DNA sequences, and a small portion exists as mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), inherited maternally without recombination and more conserved than nuclear DNA. Comparing DNA across species enables reconstruction of evolutionary history through the molecular clock, which estimates divergence timing by analyzing neutral sites—segments not affected by natural selection. mtDNA evidence also identifies “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of modern humans, who lived roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
