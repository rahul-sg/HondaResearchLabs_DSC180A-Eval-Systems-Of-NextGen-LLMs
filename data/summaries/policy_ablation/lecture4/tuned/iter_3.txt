This lecture explained how natural selection and genetic inheritance together drive evolutionary change, tracing ideas from Darwin’s observations to the molecular foundations of heredity. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Darwin identified three postulates: organisms struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and these variations are heritable. His major challenge was explaining how variation arises and persists—questions later solved through genetics.  

Artificial selection in domesticated species such as dogs and pigeons illustrated how directed breeding can rapidly produce significant differences, paralleling natural selection but on a human timescale. Evolutionary rates in nature vary: minnows evolved placenta‑like structures quickly, while the vertebrate eye evolved through about 1,800 small steps over roughly one million years, showing how gradual modification can yield complex traits.  

Gregor Mendel’s experiments with pea plants (1856–1863) revealed that inheritance operates through discrete particles—later called genes—that maintain variation rather than blending. His findings established the laws of segregation and independent assortment, forming the foundation of modern genetics. Around the turn of the 20th century, Walther Flemming described chromatin and mitosis, while Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri independently demonstrated that chromosomes carry hereditary material, leading to the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Chromosomes are bundles of DNA occurring in homologous pairs; humans have 23 pairs, including one pair of sex chromosomes.  

Cell division occurs through mitosis, which produces identical diploid somatic cells, and meiosis, which produces haploid gametes. Meiosis introduces variation through mutation and recombination. Mendel distinguished genotype (the genetic makeup) from phenotype (the expressed traits) and showed how dominant and recessive alleles determine offspring outcomes, as illustrated by Punnett squares. Genes located close together may exhibit linkage, but crossing‑over during meiosis can reshuffle alleles; the recombination rate measures how often such exchanges occur, generating new combinations and genetic diversity.  

At the molecular level, DNA is a double helix with a sugar‑phosphate backbone and base pairs (A‑T, C‑G). Genes are functional DNA segments, while mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)—shorter and more conserved than nuclear DNA—is inherited only from mothers without recombination. DNA comparisons allow estimation of evolutionary timing through a molecular clock and have identified “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all humans, living about 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
