This lecture linked the concept of natural selection with the genetic mechanisms that make evolution possible. Evolution was defined as changes in allele frequencies within populations over time. Darwin’s postulates proposed that individuals compete for existence, vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and transmit this variation to offspring. Although Darwin recognized the importance of variation, he could not explain how it persisted or how new forms arose. Artificial selection in plants and animals, such as dogs and pigeons, demonstrated that strong selection could cause major changes within just a few generations, illustrating how natural selection operates in nature. Rates of evolutionary change can differ markedly: minnows have evolved placenta-like structures rapidly, while complex organs like the eye form through many small, roughly 1% changes across about one million years.

Later discoveries in genetics clarified the basis of inheritance. Gregor Mendel, working with pea plants, showed that traits are inherited as discrete “particles” from each parent, maintaining variation instead of blending it. The rediscovery of his work in the 1900s led to study of cell structures. Walther Flemming described chromatin and mitosis, while Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri linked hereditary material to chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 homologous pairs, including one pair of sex chromosomes), though chromosome number varies widely across species.

Mitosis creates identical diploid cells for growth, whereas meiosis produces haploid gametes, restoring the chromosome number at fertilization. Mutation and recombination during meiosis introduce new variation. Mendel’s distinction between genotype (genetic makeup) and phenotype (visible expression) explained individual differences, and dominant versus recessive alleles determined which traits appear. Linkage studies showed that nearby genes can be inherited together, but crossing-over allows new allele combinations; the recombination rate expresses how frequently this occurs.

Molecular research identified DNA as the molecule of heredity. Watson and Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, proposed the double‑helix model in 1953, showing that understanding structure helps explain replication and protein coding. DNA consists of a sugar–phosphate backbone and paired bases: A with T, C with G. Mitochondrial DNA, inherited maternally without recombination, was used to infer a “Mitochondrial Eve”—the most recent common matrilineal ancestor, estimated 100–200 thousand years ago in Africa. Comparisons of neutral DNA sites serve as a molecular clock, helping estimate divergence times among species.
