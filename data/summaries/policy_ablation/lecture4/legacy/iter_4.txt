This lecture traced how natural selection, inheritance, and DNA together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies over time within a population, driven by three postulates: organisms face a struggle for existence, individuals differ in survival‑ and reproduction‑related traits, and these differences are heritable. Darwin recognized that variation was essential but could not explain how it was produced or maintained. Artificial selection in dogs, pigeons, and crops illustrated that targeted breeding can yield dramatic changes within a few generations, confirming the extent of heritable variation. Comparative examples showed that evolutionary rates vary dramatically—from the rapid evolution of a placenta‑like structure in minnows to the gradual, one‑percent steps that formed the vertebrate eye over roughly a million years.

Mendel’s pea‑plant experiments (1856–1863) demonstrated particulate inheritance: traits are governed by genes that occur in alternate forms called alleles, transmitted from each parent without blending, thereby maintaining variation. The rediscovery of Mendel’s work in the early 1900s led to chromosomal studies. Walther Flemming discovered chromatin and mitosis, while Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri established the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, linking genes to specific chromosomes. Humans have 46 chromosomes arranged in homologous pairs—22 somatic pairs and one sex‑determining pair (XX or XY). Understanding cell division clarified that mitosis preserves chromosome number, whereas meiosis halves it, ensuring stability across generations. Slides 14, 16, and 28 together showed that variation arises through independent assortment, recombination (crossing over), and mutation.

Mendel’s definitions of genotype and phenotype, and his observation that dominant alleles mask recessive ones, enabled prediction of offspring outcomes, as depicted in heterozygote crosses and Punnett squares. Later work on linkage and recombination refined these patterns: genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but when located close together, they may be inherited jointly unless crossing over occurs during meiosis. The recombination rate measures how often such exchanges create new allele combinations, thereby increasing variation.

Watson and Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, described DNA’s double‑helix structure in 1953. DNA has a sugar‑phosphate backbone and complementary base pairing (A‑T, C‑G). Genes are functional DNA sequences, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)—a small, maternally inherited genome lacking recombination—provides a tool for tracing ancestry. mtDNA evidence identifies a most recent common matrilineal ancestor (“Mitochondrial Eve,” about 100–200 thousand years ago in East Africa), who was far older than the most recent common ancestor of all humans, and supports use of the molecular clock to estimate species divergence at neutral DNA sites.
