This lecture traced how natural selection, inheritance, and DNA together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies over time within a population, based on three postulates: organisms face a struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits that affect survival and reproduction, and those traits are heritable. Darwin recognized that variation was essential but lacked a mechanism to explain how it was maintained or newly produced. Examples of artificial selection in dogs, pigeons, and crops illustrated that selective breeding can produce rapid, observable changes across just a few generations. Evolutionary rates can differ significantly, as shown by the fast emergence of placenta‑like structures in minnows compared with the gradual development of a complex eye through many small steps over roughly a million years.

Mendel’s pea‑plant experiments (1856–1863) established the principle of particulate inheritance: traits are determined by genes occurring in alternate forms called alleles that are passed unchanged from each parent, maintaining variation rather than blending. Rediscovery of his work in the early 1900s spurred discoveries about chromosomes. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, while Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri linked genes to chromosomes through the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans have 46 chromosomes organized in homologous pairs—22 somatic pairs and one pair of sex chromosomes (XX or XY). Understanding cell division clarified that mitosis reproduces identical diploid cells, whereas meiosis halves the chromosome number for gametes, keeping the total constant across generations and allowing variation through mutation and the recombination of genetic material.

Mendel’s distinctions between genotype (genetic makeup) and phenotype (expressed traits) and the concepts of dominant and recessive alleles enabled prediction of offspring characteristics. Later work on linkage and recombination showed that genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but those located close together may be inherited together unless crossing over occurs during meiosis. Such recombination can produce new combinations of alleles, increasing genetic diversity.

The discovery of DNA’s double‑helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images, revealed how genetic information is stored and replicated through complementary base pairing (A‑T, C‑G). Genes are functional sequences of DNA, while mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)—a small, maternally inherited genome lacking recombination—has been used to trace ancestry. mtDNA provides evidence for a common matrilineal ancestor (“Mitochondrial Eve”) from East Africa about 100–200 thousand years ago and supports molecular‑clock approaches for estimating species divergence.
