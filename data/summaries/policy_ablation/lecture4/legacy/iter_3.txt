This lecture traced how natural selection, inheritance, and DNA together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies over time within a population, driven by three postulates: organisms struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits related to survival and reproduction, and such variation is heritable. Darwin recognized that variation was essential but could not explain how it was produced or maintained. Artificial selection in dogs, pigeons, and crops demonstrated how targeted breeding can produce rapid changes within a few generations, showing that substantial variation exists within species. Later examples contrasted tempos of evolutionary change—rapid placenta evolution in minnows versus the slow, stepwise development of the eye across 1,800 incremental changes over roughly one million years—illustrating both swift and gradual evolutionary rates.

Mendel’s pea‑plant experiments (1856–1863) revealed the particulate nature of inheritance: traits are controlled by genes, which occur in alternative forms called alleles and are passed intact from generation to generation, preserving variation. The early 1900s rediscovery of Mendel’s work spurred cellular research. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and the process of mitosis, while Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri formulated the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, linking genes to specific chromosomes. Humans possess 46 chromosomes arranged in homologous pairs, with one pair determining sex. Understanding cell division clarified how mitosis maintains chromosome number and how meiosis halves it, ensuring stability across generations while introducing variation through independent assortment, recombination, and mutation.

Mendel’s definitions of genotype, phenotype, and dominant versus recessive alleles enabled prediction of offspring traits, as modeled in heterozygote crosses and Punnett squares. Linkage and recombination refined these patterns: genes on different chromosomes assort independently, but those close together may be inherited together unless crossing over during meiosis exchanges segments, generating new allele combinations. The recombination rate measures how often such exchanges occur.

The molecular basis of heredity was established when Watson and Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, described DNA’s double‑helix structure in 1953. DNA’s sugar‑phosphate backbone and complementary base pairing (A‑T, C‑G) explain replication and protein coding. Genes are functional DNA sequences, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)—a small, maternally inherited genome without recombination—serves as a powerful tool for tracing ancestry. mtDNA comparisons have identified a most recent common matrilineal ancestor (“Mitochondrial Eve,” about 100–200 thousand years ago in East Africa) and underpin molecular‑clock analyses that estimate when species diverged using neutral DNA sites.
