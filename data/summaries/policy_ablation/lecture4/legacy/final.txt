This lecture examined how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population across generations, supported by Darwin’s postulates that populations face a struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits tied to survival and reproduction, and this variation is heritable. Darwin’s key challenge was explaining how variation arises and persists, which later discoveries in genetics resolved. Artificial selection in domesticated animals and plants illustrated how targeted breeding can produce marked changes within a few generations. Examples such as rapid placental evolution in minnows and incremental eye evolution in small, cumulative steps showed that evolutionary rates can vary, from swift shifts to gradual transformations over millions of years.  

Mendel’s experiments with pea plants established the particulate nature of inheritance—traits are determined by discrete “particles” (genes) passed from each parent, maintaining variation generation after generation. His work resurfaced in the early 1900s and was connected to cellular structures. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and Sutton and Boveri proposed the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, identifying chromosomes as carriers of genetic material.  

Understanding cell division clarified how inheritance operates. Mitosis produces identical diploid somatic cells, while meiosis creates haploid gametes in which variation can arise through mutation, recombination, and independent assortment. Mendel’s laws distinguished genotypes (genetic makeup) from phenotypes (observable traits) and showed dominant and recessive allele interactions. When heterozygotes are crossed, predictable offspring ratios emerge, demonstrated through Punnett squares. Linkage and crossing over explained how nearby genes may be inherited together or reshuffled, introducing new genetic combinations measurable by recombination rate.  

The discovery of DNA’s double-helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x-ray data, revealed the molecular basis of heredity. DNA’s sugar-phosphate backbone holds paired bases—adenine with thymine and cytosine with guanine. Genes are functional DNA sequences that can vary as alleles. Mitochondrial DNA, inherited only from mothers and lacking recombination, is shorter and more conserved than nuclear DNA. Comparisons of mtDNA enable estimation of common ancestry, identifying “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all humans, dated roughly 100–200 thousand years ago in East Africa—an example of how molecular data illuminate evolutionary history.
