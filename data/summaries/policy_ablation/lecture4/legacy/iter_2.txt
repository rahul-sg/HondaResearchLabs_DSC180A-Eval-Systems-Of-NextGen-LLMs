This lecture examined how natural selection, genetic inheritance, and the molecular basis of DNA together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, based on three postulates: organisms compete for existence, traits vary in ways related to survival and reproduction, and these variations are heritable. Darwin recognized that variation was essential but could not explain its source or maintenance. Artificial selection in domesticated plants and animals illustrated how, through selective breeding, rapid evolutionary changes can occur in just a few generations, showing that significant variation exists within species. Examples such as rapid placenta evolution in minnows and gradual improvement of the eye over many small steps demonstrated both rapid and incremental rates of evolutionary change.

Mendel’s experiments with pea plants (1856–1863) provided a mechanism for inheritance. He showed that traits are determined by particulate units—genes—passed from each parent to offspring, preserving variation across generations. His rediscovered work in the early 1900s prompted further cellular research. Walther Flemming’s identification of chromatin and mitosis, and Sutton and Boveri’s Chromosome Theory of Inheritance, linked chromosomes to hereditary material. Humans possess 46 chromosomes in homologous pairs, with one pair determining sex. Understanding mitosis and meiosis clarified how genetic material is maintained or halved during cell division, allowing recombination, mutation, and independent assortment to create variation.

Mendel’s definitions of genotype, phenotype, dominance, and recessiveness explained predictable inheritance patterns, illustrated through heterozygote crosses and Punnett squares. Linkage and recombination further refined this model: while genes on different chromosomes assort independently, those located close together may segregate less freely, with crossing over during meiosis producing new gene combinations. Recombination rate measures how often such exchanges occur.

The molecular era began with the discovery of DNA’s double-helix structure by Watson and Crick in 1953, derived from Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data. DNA’s sugar‑phosphate backbone and complementary base pairing (A–T, C–G) revealed how genetic information replicates and directs protein formation. Some DNA encodes genes, the fundamental units of heredity. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), inherited only from mothers and lacking recombination, provides a conserved record for tracing ancestry. Comparing mtDNA allows estimation of the age of the most recent common matrilineal ancestor—“Mitochondrial Eve”—likely 100–200 thousand years ago in East Africa, and supports molecular‑clock methods that estimate evolutionary divergence using neutral DNA sites.
