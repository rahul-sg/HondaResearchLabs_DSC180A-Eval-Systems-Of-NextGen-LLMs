This lecture examined how nested SQL queries—also called subqueries—operate within different clauses and how their behavior relates to query monotonicity and optimization. Building on prior SQL topics such as projections, selections, joins, aggregates, and grouping, it introduced subqueries as queries embedded within another query, which can appear in the SELECT, FROM, or WHERE clauses. Subqueries may return single values or full relations, enabling expressions that depend on intermediate computations. While powerful, they can be less efficient; therefore, the general rule is to avoid nesting when equivalent results can be achieved through joins or simpler expressions.

In the **SELECT clause**, subqueries were used to compute information such as the genre of a movie an actor appeared in, the number of actors in each movie, or the average salary of actors for high‑rated films. The lecture highlighted “subquery unnesting,” showing that many—but not all—nested forms can be rewritten as joins with aggregation and grouping. Importantly, examples (Slide 10) emphasized that these rewritten queries are not always equivalent when outer joins are involved, since join semantics can alter the inclusion of tuples.

Subqueries in the **FROM clause** act as derived relations or temporary tables. They can simplify multi‑step filtering or transformation logic, and are conceptually similar to Common Table Expressions (CTEs), as shown through examples refactoring rating filters using a named subquery. In the **WHERE clause**, subqueries introduce logical quantifiers. The lecture illustrated existential quantifiers using EXISTS and IN, which test for the existence or membership of related tuples. These can often be rewritten as traditional join queries, exemplifying their logical equivalence.

Universal quantifiers (“for all”) are harder to express directly in SQL. Demonstrations included retrieving actors who acted only in action movies and finding movies where all actors’ salaries exceed $100K. Such conditions were expressed using NOT IN or NOT EXISTS constructs, requiring multiple logical steps to filter and exclude counterexamples. These examples showed that universal quantification corresponds to negation and is more complex than existential conditions.

The lecture concluded with the notion of **monotone and non‑monotone queries**. A theorem stated that a SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE query with no subqueries or aggregates is monotone—adding tuples to input tables cannot remove results. Proof intuition relied on nested loop semantics. Monotone queries can typically be unnested for optimization, while non‑monotone ones—those involving negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregation—cannot. This link between monotonicity and unnesting underscores the structural limits of SQL query rewriting.
