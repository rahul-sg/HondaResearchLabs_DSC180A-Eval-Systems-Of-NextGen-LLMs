This lecture examined nested and subqueries in SQL—queries that operate within other queries—and the theoretical limits of rewriting them, focusing on unnesting and monotonicity. Building on prior SQL topics such as projections, selections, joins, and aggregates, it began by defining subqueries as embedded queries that may appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, or **WHERE** clauses. Subqueries can return scalar values or entire relations and are sometimes unavoidable, though the “rule of thumb” is to avoid them when possible for efficiency reasons.

In the **SELECT** clause, subqueries can compute derived values, such as a movie’s genre or average actor salary. These examples illustrated *correlated subqueries*, where the inner query depends on an attribute from the outer query. The lecture compared these forms to equivalent join-based queries, showing that unnesting (rewriting a nested query as a join) often yields simpler and faster results. However, slide 10 emphasized that equivalence fails in certain cases—particularly when outer joins are involved—because join semantics may include tuples absent in the subquery result.

In the **FROM** clause, subqueries serve as intermediate tables or “refactored” relations. This can be done inline or through **WITH** clauses (common table expressions), which improve readability but can still be flattened through unnesting without changing results.

In the **WHERE** clause, subqueries support logical tests using quantifiers. **EXISTS** and **IN** implement *existential quantifiers*, returning true if any tuple satisfies the condition (e.g., finding actors in at least one Sci‑Fi movie). Conversely, *universal quantifiers* express “for all” conditions, which SQL lacks native syntax for. The lecture demonstrated workarounds using **NOT IN** or **NOT EXISTS**, such as retrieving actors who acted only in action films or movies where all salaries exceed $100K. These require multi-step reasoning and can’t always be simplified into joins.

The lecture concluded by linking these query types to **monotonicity**. Monotone queries—those whose outputs cannot decrease when new tuples are added—include basic SELECT-FROM-WHERE forms without subqueries or aggregates and can generally be unnested. By contrast, **non-monotone queries**, which involve negation, aggregation, or universal quantifiers, lose monotonicity and therefore cannot be safely unnested. This distinction grounds both the practical and theoretical limits of subquery optimization in SQL.
