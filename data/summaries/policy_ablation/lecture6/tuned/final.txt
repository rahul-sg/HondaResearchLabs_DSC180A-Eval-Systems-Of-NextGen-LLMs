This lecture examined **nested SQL queries**, detailing how subqueries operate within different clauses and linking their behavior to the concept of **monotonicity** in query evaluation. It built on prior SQL constructs—projections, selections, joins, grouping, and aggregates—and emphasized that while subqueries can be powerful, they are often less efficient than joins. The general rule is to **avoid nesting when possible**, though in some cases it is necessary.

**Subqueries in the SELECT clause** were introduced first. They may return single values (scalars) or full relations and can be correlated, meaning they reference attributes from the outer query. Examples included retrieving a movie’s genre for each actor, counting the number of actors per movie, and computing the average salary for movies above a certain rating. The lecture compared these statements to equivalent join-based queries, explaining the notion of **subquery unnesting**—rewriting nested queries using joins and groupings for efficiency. However, the slides highlighted that not all such rewrites are semantically identical: for instance, versions using **left outer joins** may differ in their handling of movies without matching actors, showing that equivalence depends on the join type and null semantics.

**Subqueries in the FROM clause** act as intermediate results or “views.” An example built a subquery selecting movies with ratings greater than 8, then filtered that result again to isolate ratings under 9. This logic can also be expressed through **common table expressions (CTEs)** or directly unnested into a single query. Such refactoring improves readability and shows how nested filters can be simplified.

**Subqueries in the WHERE clause** illustrated logical quantifiers. Using **EXISTS** or **IN**, SQL expresses **existential quantification**—testing whether related tuples exist or a value is part of a set. These queries, like “return actors who acted in a Sci‑Fi movie,” can typically be rewritten as equivalent joins. In contrast, **universal quantifiers** (“for all”) are more complex. To find, for example, actors who acted **only in action movies**, the slides presented a clear two‑step construction:  
1. Identify actors who appeared in *non‑action* movies.  
2. Select all others not in that list.  
Similar reasoning applied to universal salary conditions using `NOT EXISTS` or `ALL`.

The lecture concluded by defining **monotone and non‑monotone queries**. A query is monotone if adding tuples to input tables never removes results. Simple SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE queries without subqueries or aggregates are monotone and can generally be unnested. Queries involving negation, aggregation, or universal quantifiers are **non‑monotone**, meaning they cannot be safely unnested. This distinction underpins both query correctness and optimization strategy.
