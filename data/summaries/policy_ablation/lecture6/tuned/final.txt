This lecture focused on nested SQL queries, explaining how subqueries function within different clauses and how their logical properties relate to query optimization and monotonicity. Building on prior topics such as projection, selection, joins, aggregation, and grouping, it defined a subquery as a query that is part of another and may itself be nested. Subqueries can occur in SELECT, FROM, or WHERE clauses, and although they are sometimes necessary, the rule of thumb is to avoid nesting when equivalent results can be expressed through joins—a process called subquery unnesting.

In the SELECT clause, subqueries can return a single value or relation to compute context-dependent attributes, such as finding a movie’s genre per actor or the average salary for actors in highly rated movies. Equivalent join-based queries often simplify computation, though care must be taken when outer joins change result semantics. In the FROM clause, subqueries act as derived tables or temporary relations, forming a clear intermediate step that can also be expressed with common table expressions (CTEs). These help refactor complex filtering logic into smaller, named subqueries without correlation.

Subqueries in the WHERE clause were discussed through logical quantifiers. **Existential quantifiers** test whether some related row exists, using *EXISTS* or *IN*, expressing “there exists” or “for some.” Such queries can usually be converted into equivalent joins. **Universal quantifiers** express that all related rows meet a condition—something SQL cannot directly represent. The slides illustrated a procedural, two-step approach for expressing “only acted in action movies”: first, find all actors who have appeared in any non‑action movie; then, in a separate query, return actors not in that set. The same reasoning applied when checking if every actor in a movie earns more than $100K, using *NOT IN* or *NOT EXISTS* constructs to handle the universal logic.

The lecture concluded with the concept of **monotone queries**, which never lose output tuples when input data grow. Using nested loop semantics, a plain SELECT–FROM–WHERE query without subqueries, aggregates, or negation is monotone. Monotonicity determines unnesting feasibility: a monotone query can be unnested, whereas queries involving negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates are non‑monotone and thus cannot. This connects the logical structure of queries to their practical optimization behavior. (≈390 words)
