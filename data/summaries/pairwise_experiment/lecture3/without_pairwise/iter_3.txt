The lecture examines **Aníbal Quijano’s** concept of the **coloniality of power** within the broader **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework to show how colonial relations of domination continue through race, labor, knowledge, and identity in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist whose work bridges dependency theory, political economy, and decolonial thought—argues that modernity and coloniality are **co‑constituted**, not separate stages of “progress.” The MCD project also includes **coloniality of knowledge**, emphasizing that Western epistemology has privileged particular categories of thought while obscuring Indigenous and other non‑Western ways of knowing. **Decoloniality**, therefore, calls for centering suppressed epistemologies, languages, and cosmologies rather than merely revising Western narratives.  

Slides 11–13 outline how coloniality developed through (1) the **construction of difference** between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) the **creation of racialized labor systems** such as slavery, the encomienda, and the mita. These arrangements intertwined racial hierarchy with economic control, turning race into a “mental category of modernity.” Slide 1 of Chunk 2 extends this by linking the constitution of race with the emergence of the global capitalist order, where Eurocentrism normalized privilege and dependency.  

**Eurocentrism** (Chunk 2 Slides 3–5) describes how Europe produced itself as the measure of civilization. Enlightenment dualisms—“developed/underdeveloped,” “First/Third World”—naturalized the idea of a universal Western path of advancement. Drawing on **Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest”**, the lecture highlights how such knowledge systems shape representation and legitimize global inequalities.  

The **heterogeneity and discontinuity** slide (Chunk 2 Slide 7) disrupts linear notions of history. Indigenous temporalities, such as Aymara ideas of *pachakuti*, emphasize cycles, complexity, and agency in social transformation. Politics and history are thus contingent and plural rather than uniform evolutions toward Western modernity—linking directly to decolonial thinking.  

Later sections apply these frameworks to questions of **national identity**. **Rebecca Earle’s** analysis of *Indianesque nationalism* shows how early republics celebrated symbolic Indigenous pasts while marginalizing living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant peoples. Myths of **mestizaje** further disguised racial hierarchies under claims of harmony. Cultural examples like *Roma* and the *Throughline* podcast invite students to locate contemporary echoes of coloniality.  

Portfolio activities ask students to engage these questions through textual analysis and thematic connections, culminating in Quijano’s **call for decoloniality** (Slide 19): to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror” and affirm plural, non‑Western ways of being and knowing.
