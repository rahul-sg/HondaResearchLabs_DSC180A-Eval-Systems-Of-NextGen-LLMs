The lecture explores **Aníbal Quijano’s** concept of the **coloniality of power** within the broader **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework to explain how colonial structures continue to shape race, labor, knowledge, and identity in Latin America. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist whose work spans from dependency theory and political economy to decolonial thought, links modernity’s rise with ongoing forms of colonial domination. The MCD project emphasizes that modernity, coloniality, and decoloniality are **co‑constituted processes**, not linear stages of historical “progress.”  

Slides 11–13 identify the core of Quijano’s argument: coloniality emerged through (1) the **construction of difference** between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) the **creation of new global labor systems** such as slavery, the encomienda, and the mita. These arrangements tied race to economic hierarchy, embedding a “mental category of modernity” that naturalized European superiority and justified domination. The linkage of racial classification and labor control (Chunk 2, Slide 1) positioned non‑white peoples in subordinate roles, forming the basis for the expansion of global capitalism and ongoing dependency.  

Eurocentrism, presented in Slides 3–5 of Chunk 2, describes how European perspectives became the model for producing knowledge and defining civilization. Enlightenment dualisms such as “developed/underdeveloped” and “First/Third World” reinforced the illusion of a universal Western trajectory. Quijano, drawing attention to this **Eurocentrification of world capitalism**, parallels **Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest”** in revealing how Europe constructed its identity through opposition and exclusion.  

Later slides trace how these logics persist in the formation of **national identity** after independence. **Rebecca Earle’s** idea of *Indianesque nationalism* illustrates how early republics celebrated symbolic Indigenous pasts while marginalizing living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant populations. The ideal of **mestizaje**, though framed as racial harmony, often reinforced whitened national norms. Works like *Roma* and the *Throughline* podcast are used to apply these ideas, showing how colonial hierarchies appear in contemporary culture and media.  

Throughout, students are asked to engage these concepts directly through **portfolio activities** (Slides 9–10): selecting passages from Quijano’s text, linking them to key themes, and reflecting on how “coloniality of power” or “Eurocentrism” clarify enduring inequalities.  

The lecture concludes with Quijano’s **call for decoloniality** (Slide 19): freeing ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts identity and acknowledging plural, non‑Western ways of knowing and being.
