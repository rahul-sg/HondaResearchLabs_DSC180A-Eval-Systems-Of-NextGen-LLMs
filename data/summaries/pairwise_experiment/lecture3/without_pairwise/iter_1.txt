The lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **“coloniality of power”** within the broader **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework to explain enduring hierarchies of race, labor, and knowledge in Latin America. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist whose work evolved from dependency theory to decolonial thought, argues that colonialism was not only a political or economic project but also an epistemic one: it created durable patterns of domination by defining who could produce legitimate knowledge and who was excluded from it. His framework links **modernity**, **coloniality**, and **decoloniality** as mutually constitutive processes rather than sequential historical stages.

According to Slide 11, two historical processes define the coloniality of power: the construction of difference between **“West” and “Rest”** and the creation of new global systems of **labor control**. In the Americas, racial classifications legitimized slavery, the encomienda, and the mita, embedding racial hierarchies in capitalist markets. Quijano shows that the idea of **race as a mental category of modernity** naturalized European dominance and organized access to privilege, authority, and resources. Slide 2 of Chunk 2 extends this logic by linking race and labor systems to **global capitalism**, where Eurocentrism rationalizes inequality as “progress.”

Throughout the lecture, Quijano’s ideas are read alongside **Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest”** discourse, which similarly exposes how Enlightenment narratives and dualisms—developed versus underdeveloped, First versus Third World—reinforce Western self‑definition through contrast. Both scholars reveal how Eurocentrism structures knowledge production and historical interpretation, positioning Europe as the exclusive protagonist of modernity.

Slides 8 and 13 trace how these logics persist in **national identity formation**. Historian **Rebecca Earle’s** notion of *Indianesque nationalism* illustrates how early independence movements romanticized an Indigenous past through symbols and art while sidelining living Indigenous peoples. Later, **mestizaje**—framed as inclusive racial mixing—often reinforced whiteness and erased Afro‑descendant and Indigenous identities. These shifts exemplify how Eurocentric notions of civilization and progress continued to shape post‑independence societies.

Finally, the lecture returns to Quijano’s **call for decoloniality** (Slide 19): freeing ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts self‑understanding by centering suppressed epistemologies, cosmologies, and heterogeneous historical experiences. Decolonial thought thus invites a plural vision of modernity—one that dismantles global systems of racialized power and opens space for knowledge production rooted in diverse, situated perspectives.
