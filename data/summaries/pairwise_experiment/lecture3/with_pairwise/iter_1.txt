This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the “coloniality of power” and its continuing influence on modernity, capitalism, and national identity in Latin America. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist, built upon dependency theory and cultural studies to develop decolonial theory—a framework that links knowledge, power, and colonial history. His work situates Latin American experience within the broader Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project, which views modernity, coloniality, and decoloniality as interconnected rather than separate stages. Modernity’s supposed Western origins are reinterpreted as historically contingent outcomes, while coloniality exposes how domination persists through racial, economic, and epistemological hierarchies. Decoloniality, in turn, seeks to reclaim suppressed worldviews and non‑Western forms of knowledge.

According to the lecture, the “coloniality of power” emerged through two linked historical processes: the creation of racial divisions between conquerors and conquered—the “West” and the “Rest”—and the development of new systems of labor control in the Americas. These systems, including slavery, the encomienda, and the mita, embedded racialized hierarchies into global capitalism, structuring who gained access to wealth, authority, and mobility. Quijano argues that race became a mental category of modernity and that Eurocentrism naturalized Western dominance by presenting European progress as universal. Over time, a Eurocentric worldview shaped global narratives of development, democracy, and success, obscuring alternative histories and perspectives.

The lecture connects these ideas to contemporary examples such as Alfonso Cuarón’s *Roma* and the Throughline podcast “The Retelling of the Conquest.” Both illustrate how colonial patterns of race, class, and representation endure in modern storytelling and social relations. Students are asked to identify passages and reflect on how Quijano’s framework helps interpret these ongoing legacies.

In its second half, the lecture traces Eurocentrism’s role in Latin American nation‑building and ideologies of mestizaje. Historian Rebecca Earle’s discussion of “Indianesque nationalism” shows how early independence movements romanticized Indigenous symbols to claim an ancient past while marginalizing living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant communities. Later, mestizaje promoted a narrative of racial harmony that continued to privilege whiteness. These cases demonstrate how national identities often reproduced colonial hierarchies even as they sought independence. Concluding with Quijano’s call for decoloniality, the lecture urges freeing knowledge and self‑understanding from the Eurocentric mirror, recognizing multiple modernities, and imagining plural ways of being beyond colonial frameworks.
