This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** and its continuing influence on modernity, capitalism, and identity across Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano’s intellectual path—from dependency theory to cultural analysis to decolonial thought—shows a widening focus from political economy to epistemology, linking knowledge and power. His work aligns with the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** project, which views modernity and coloniality as *co‑constituted* rather than sequential stages. Modernity is not the natural achievement of the West but a historically contingent outcome shaped by colonization. Coloniality identifies the enduring structures—racial, economic, and epistemic—that maintain domination, while decoloniality calls for centering non‑Western languages, worldviews, and forms of knowledge.

Quijano defines the **coloniality of power** through two historical processes: (1) the creation of differences between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) the establishment of new systems for organizing labor and controlling American resources. Slavery, the *encomienda*, and *mita* tied racial categories to economic hierarchy, embedding “race” as a mental category of modernity. These arrangements produced a global model of labor control and legitimized whiteness as a sign of civilization and authority.

The lecture’s second focus is **Eurocentrism**, highlighting how Western dominance in knowledge production and global capitalism became naturalized. Eurocentrism treats European experience as universal and organizes history through binaries like “developed/underdeveloped.” This framing supported economic dependency and cultural hierarchies after independence, prompting the slide’s question: *Who decides measures of success?* The 2018 film *Roma* serves as a contemporary example, revealing how racial and class divisions rooted in colonial labor systems continue to shape representation and social order.

The third section links Eurocentrism to **historical experience and national identity** in Latin America. Historian Rebecca Earle’s notion of “Indianesque nationalism” illustrates how post‑independence elites celebrated an idealized Indigenous past while marginalizing present Indigenous and Afro‑descendant peoples. Later *mestizaje* ideologies proclaimed harmony but still privileged whiteness, reproducing colonial hierarchies within national narratives. Independence thus reconfigured, rather than dismantled, colonial legacies.

The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality**—to “free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror” that distorts self‑understanding—and to recognize multiple modernities grounded in diverse cosmologies and ways of being.
