This lecture explores Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the *coloniality of power* and its enduring imprint on modernity, capitalism, and identity in Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano moved from dependency theory and cultural approaches to develop decolonial thought—a framework linking knowledge, power, and colonial history. His arguments form part of the Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project, which insists these are intertwined, co‑constituted processes rather than separate stages. Modernity is thus viewed as a historically contingent outcome, not a natural property of the West, while coloniality highlights how domination persists through racialized, economic, and epistemic hierarchies. Decoloniality calls for centering non‑Western languages, cosmologies, and ways of knowing.

The *coloniality of power* arose through two interrelated historical processes: first, the formation of differences between “conquerors” and “conquered,” constructing the racialized divide of “West” and “Rest”; and second, the organization of new labor systems in the Americas—slavery, encomienda, and the mita—that linked racial classification to economic control. These systems embedded race as a mental category of modernity, producing a global model in which whiteness framed progress and authority. Eurocentrism, sustained by narratives of Western superiority, naturalized these hierarchies and defined measures of democracy and development through a distorted, self‑reinforcing lens. 

Illustrations such as Alfonso Cuarón’s *Roma* show how this logic endures: the film’s focus on a Mixtec domestic worker and its reception in Mexico expose how social, racial, and class hierarchies from colonial times still shape representation and recognition. Similarly, the Throughline podcast’s retelling of the conquest invites listeners to question dominant myths and uncover silenced perspectives.

The lecture then connects Eurocentrism to Latin American nation‑building and the ideology of *mestizaje*. Historian Rebecca Earle describes “Indianesque nationalism,” in which post‑independence leaders celebrated an ancient Indigenous past while erasing living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant peoples. Later mestizaje discourses proclaimed racial harmony but continued to privilege whiteness, reproducing the colonial order under a national guise. These examples reveal that independence often reconfigured rather than dismantled colonial structures. Concluding with Quijano’s injunction “to cease being what we are not,” the lecture emphasizes that decoloniality requires releasing ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts self‑understanding and acknowledging plural modernities beyond colonial frameworks.
