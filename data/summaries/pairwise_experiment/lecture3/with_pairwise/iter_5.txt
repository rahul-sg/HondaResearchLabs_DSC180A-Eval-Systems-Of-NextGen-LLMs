This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** and its continuing influence on modernity, capitalism, and national identity in Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano moved from dependency theory’s political‑economic focus in the 1960s–70s to cultural and epistemological analyses that helped shape the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** project. MCD scholars argue that modernity and coloniality were *co‑constituted*: Western modernity arose through colonial domination rather than apart from it. Coloniality names the ongoing racial, economic, and epistemic hierarchies establishing Western superiority, while decoloniality seeks to center non‑Western languages, cosmologies, and knowledges as valid and transformative.

**Point 1: Coloniality of Power.**  
Quijano identifies two interrelated historical processes in the making of “America.” First, the *constitution of difference* separated conquerors (“West”) from conquered peoples (“Rest”). Second, new *structures of labor control* organized the exploitation of American resources, producing a **new model of global power**. Systems such as slavery, *encomienda*, and *mita* tied racial classification to labor status, embedding white European dominance in the emerging capitalist world economy. “Race” thus became a mental category of modernity that rationalized social hierarchy and persists in today’s global inequalities.

**Point 2: Eurocentrism.**  
Eurocentrism naturalized Western dominance by presenting European experience as universal and arranging history in binary stages—“underdeveloped/developed,” “Third World/First World.” This worldview sustained economic dependency and cultural hierarchies after formal colonialism. The film *Roma* (2018) exemplifies how colonial logics survive: through the eyes of a Mixtec domestic worker, the film exposes Mexico’s racialized class order and the Eurocentric gaze that shapes public reception. Quijano challenges Europe’s self‑portrayal as exclusive protagonist of world progress and calls for recognizing knowledge produced from other historical locations.

**Point 3: Historical Experience and National Identity.**  
Post‑independence elites constructed “Indianesque nationalism” that idealized an ancient Indigenous past yet marginalized living Indigenous peoples. Later *mestizaje* ideologies celebrated racial mixture while still privileging whiteness. Slides on **Homogeneity/Continuity and Heterogeneity/Discontinuity** underscored the emerging “crisis of the European perspective of knowledge,” opening space for plural, nonlinear understandings of history—echoing Aymara notions of *pachakuti* (cyclical transformation). Such perspectives challenge linear, Eurocentric progress narratives and affirm local agency in shaping social change.

The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality**: to free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror, acknowledge heterogeneous modernities, and recover suppressed epistemologies to dismantle the enduring matrix of colonial power.
