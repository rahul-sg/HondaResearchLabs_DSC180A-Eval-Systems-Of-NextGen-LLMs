This lecture explores Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** and its lasting effects on modernity, capitalism, and national identity in Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano moved from dependency theory’s focus on political economy in the 1960s–70s to a later emphasis on culture, identity, and epistemology. His work is central to the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** project, which argues that modernity and coloniality were *co‑constituted*: Western modernity emerged through colonial domination, not apart from it. Coloniality refers to the enduring hierarchies—racial, economic, and epistemic—that continue to shape power, while decoloniality calls for centering non‑Western languages, cosmologies, and worldviews as sources of knowledge.

**Point 1: Coloniality of Power.**  
Quijano identifies two historical processes in the constitution of “America”: (1) the creation of distinctions between conquerors (“West”) and conquered (“Rest”), and (2) the formation of new labor systems controlling American resources. Enslavement, the *encomienda*, and the *mita* organized production for global markets while linking race to labor status. “Race” thus became a mental category of modernity, legitimizing whiteness as superior and embedding a global hierarchy of labor and authority—conditions still visible in structures of inequality.

**Point 2: Eurocentrism.**  
Eurocentrism naturalized Western dominance in knowledge and global capitalism by treating European experience as universal and arranging history through binary stages such as “pre‑capitalist/developed.” These narratives justified cultural and economic dependency long after independence. The lecture used the film *Roma* (2018) to illustrate how colonial hierarchies persist in representation and social imagination: the Mixtec domestic worker’s point of view exposes modern Mexico’s racialized class divisions and the public’s Eurocentric reception of her image.

**Point 3: Historical Experience and National Identity.**  
Post‑independence elites, as historian Rebecca Earle shows, promoted “Indianesque nationalism” that romantically celebrated an ancient Indigenous past while excluding living Indigenous peoples from the nation. Later *mestizaje* ideologies portrayed racial harmony but continued to privilege whiteness, reproducing colonial hierarchies. Slides on **Homogeneity/Continuity and Heterogeneity/Discontinuity** emphasized that histories are plural and nonlinear—echoing Aymara notions of *pachakuti*, or cyclical transformation, that resist Eurocentric, linear progress narratives.

The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality**: to free ourselves from the “Eurocentric mirror,” recognize heterogeneous modernities, and recover suppressed epistemologies as part of transforming the lingering matrix of colonial power.
