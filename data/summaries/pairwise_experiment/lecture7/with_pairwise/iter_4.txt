The lecture focused on **within-subjects designs**, introduced as a complement to between-groups methods. A between-groups design tests each condition with different participants, so individual differences add nuisance variability that can obscure true effects and require larger samples. A within-subjects design instead has each participant complete every condition, allowing direct comparison of each person to themselves. This design reduces participant-related noise, increases statistical power, and yields the same number of data points with fewer participants.

Several types of within-subjects designs were presented. A **pretest–posttest design** measures each participant before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more conditions within a session, such as varying stimulus intensity. A **longitudinal design** measures participants over extended time intervals, providing rich data but taking far longer to complete. Overall, within-subjects designs are efficient and statistically powerful, especially when tasks are brief.

However, they introduce potential threats to validity. **Demand characteristics** can occur when participants, experiencing all conditions, infer the study’s intent. **Carryover effects** arise when one condition influences later ones, including **fatigue effects** (worse performance due to tiredness or boredom) and **practice effects** (better performance from task familiarity). Additional sources of confounding include the **history effect** (outside events coinciding with testing, such as midterms or major news), the **maturation effect** (changes due to aging or development rather than manipulation), and the **testing effect** (improvement or strategic behavior from repeated exposure to a test). Each represents a potential confound between the manipulation and another variable over time.

Lecture examples, such as children’s understanding of accented speech and reading improvement with spaced text in dyslexia, illustrated how to identify within- versus between-subjects structures.

The session then addressed **order effects**, where results depend on the sequence of conditions. Remedies include running a between-subjects design or using **counterbalancing** to distribute order effects evenly. **Complete within-subjects counterbalancing** exposes each participant to all possible orders—through an **ABBA design** for two conditions or **block randomization** for three or more. When this is infeasible, **incomplete counterbalancing** is used, such as **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square designs**, ensuring each condition appears in each position and follows each other condition equally often. The lecture concluded by noting that some manipulations cannot be reversed—one cannot “un-take” a drug, “un-have” a surgery, or “unlearn” a technique—placing practical limits on counterbalancing.
