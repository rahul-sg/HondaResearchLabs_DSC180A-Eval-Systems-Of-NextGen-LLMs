The lecture examined **within-subjects designs** as a complement to between-groups methods, emphasizing how they reduce participant-related variability. In a between-groups design, each person contributes data to only one condition, so individual differences add random noise that can mask effects and require larger samples. A within-subjects design instead has every participant complete all conditions and be compared to themselves, removing much of that noise and improving statistical power using fewer participants.

The lecture emphasized, however, that this efficiency is not a universal advantage but a function of careful experimental design. Within-subjects approaches are often valuable but must be matched appropriately to the research question and task.

Several forms were described. A **pretest–posttest design** tests each participant before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** involves two or more conditions within the same session (e.g., stimulus coherence at 10%, 20%, 30%). A **longitudinal design** tracks change across time points—highly informative but slow to complete. Together, these designs highlight how comparing participants to themselves can strengthen inference when conditions are brief and well controlled.

Potential threats to validity include **demand characteristics**, where participants might infer the purpose when exposed to all conditions, and **carryover effects**, when one condition influences subsequent performance. Carryover includes **fatigue effects** (decline from tiredness) and **practice effects** (improvement through familiarity). Other temporal confounds noted were the **history effect** (outside events like exams or festivals), **maturation effect** (natural developmental or biological change), and **testing effect** (score changes from repeated exposure or strategy). Each can blur interpretation of a treatment’s impact.

Examples such as children judging accented speech and reading studies with differently spaced text prompted students to decide whether these setups were within- or between-subjects and whether the order of conditions was complete or incomplete—illustrating design logic rather than reporting results.

The lecture then explored **order effects**, where outcome depends on sequence, and introduced **counterbalancing** as a remedy. In **complete counterbalancing**, participants experience every possible order (e.g., **ABBA** patterns for two conditions or **block randomization** for more). **Incomplete counterbalancing** gives each participant all conditions but not all orders, using methods like **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square designs**, which distribute order positions evenly. The session closed by noting that some manipulations—such as taking a drug or performing a surgery—cannot be “undone,” limiting when counterbalancing is feasible.
