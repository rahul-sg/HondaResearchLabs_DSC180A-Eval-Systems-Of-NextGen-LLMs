The lecture focused on **within-subjects designs** as a methodological alternative to between-groups designs. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data in just one condition, so individual differences across participants create nuisance variability that can obscure effects and require larger samples. In contrast, a within-subjects design tests each participant in all conditions, comparing individuals to themselves. This structure removes some participant-related noise, produces a more powerful statistical test, and achieves the same number of data points with far fewer participants.

Several types of within-subjects designs were introduced. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention; a **repeated-measures design** involves two or more conditions within the same session; and a **longitudinal design** captures changes across time. Each provides efficiency and reduced variability but also presents unique challenges. Participants might recognize the manipulation (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects**, where performance in one condition influences another. Carryover effects can include **fatigue** (decline due to tiredness) and **practice** (improvement through experience). Beyond these, the lecture emphasized additional, distinct concerns:  
- **History effects**, when external events during the study confound results (e.g., exams or major events).  
- **Maturation effects**, where natural development or aging—not the manipulation—drives change.  
- **Testing effects**, where prior exposure to the same test aids later performance.

**Order effects** occur when the sequence of conditions affects results, such as performing better when one condition always comes first. To control this, researchers use **counterbalancing**—systematically varying order across participants. Complete designs include the **ABBA** sequence for two conditions, or **block randomization** for three or more. When the number of possible orders becomes excessive, **incomplete counterbalancing** methods are used, such as **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square designs**, ensuring each condition appears equally often in each position and follows every other condition equally often. The lecture noted that counterbalancing “can get pretty complex pretty quickly—but it’s worth it” for controlling order effects.

In cognitive science experiments with many quick trials, random intermixing of conditions can often eliminate order effects, though counterbalancing is preferable when tasks are long or confusing. Finally, counterbalancing has limits: some manipulations—like taking a drug, undergoing surgery, or learning a strategy—cannot be undone. Overall, within-subjects designs provide an efficient and powerful approach to detecting effects, but they require careful planning to manage order, carryover, and time-related influences.
