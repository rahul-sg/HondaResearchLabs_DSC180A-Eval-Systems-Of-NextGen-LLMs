The lecture examined **within-subjects designs** as an alternative to between-groups methods. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so individual differences add nuisance variability and make effects harder to detect—requiring more participants overall. A within-subjects design instead has every participant complete all conditions, comparing each person to themselves. This reduces participant-related noise, increases statistical power, and achieves the same number of data points with fewer participants.

Specific forms of within-subjects designs include the **pretest–posttest design** (measurement before and after an intervention), the **repeated-measures design** (responses across two or more conditions within a session), and the **longitudinal design** (tracking change over time). These designs efficiently capture differences but can introduce complications. The lecture highlighted **demand characteristics**, where participants detect the study’s purpose, and **carryover effects** when one condition influences another. Carryover can stem from **fatigue** or **practice**. Additional confounds include **history effects** (outside events that alter performance), **maturation effects** (change due to aging or development rather than intervention), and **testing effects** (improved scores from prior exposure).

Slides also connected these ideas to empirical examples, such as studies on children’s accent comprehension and text spacing in dyslexia—used to help students classify whether experiments were within- or between-subjects designs.

The lecture then addressed **order effects**, in which the sequence of conditions alters outcomes. To manage these, researchers can switch to a between-subjects design or use **counterbalancing** to distribute orders evenly. Counterbalancing strategies build sequentially from complete to incomplete versions. In **complete within-subjects designs**, every participant experiences all possible orders—examples include the **ABBA design** for two conditions, or **block randomization** when there are three or more. When condition numbers make this impractical, **incomplete counterbalancing** is used. Options include **random order with rotation** (each participant’s sequence shifts systematically) and **Latin-square designs**, which balance how often each condition appears in each position and after every other condition.

In cognitive science studies, random intermixing of trials can sometimes substitute for formal counterbalancing, though counterbalancing remains important when tasks are lengthy or confusing. The lecture concluded by clarifying that some manipulations cannot be reversed—participants cannot “un-take” a drug or “unlearn” a technique—so counterbalancing has limits.
