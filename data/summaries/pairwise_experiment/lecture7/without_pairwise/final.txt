This lecture focused on **within‑subjects designs**, contrasting them with **between‑groups designs** to illustrate how researchers can reduce nuisance variability and increase statistical power. In a between‑groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so individual differences add noise that can obscure effects and require larger samples. In a within‑subjects design, each participant experiences all conditions and is compared to themselves across them, removing much participant‑related variability and allowing more precise results with fewer participants.

The lecture covered three main within‑subjects formats. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention, a **repeated‑measures design** includes two or more levels of an independent variable (e.g., 10%, 20%, 30%, 40% coherence), and a **longitudinal design** tracks change across multiple time points. These formats share efficiency and sensitivity benefits but bring distinctive complications.

Several examples illustrated these principles. In research on **accent comprehension** among 4‑ and 7‑year‑olds and the **spaced‑text dyslexia study**, the slides prompted students to consider whether conditions were tested within or between participants and how order balancing affects interpretation.

Potential problems include **demand characteristics** and **carryover effects**, such as fatigue or practice, when earlier conditions influence later ones. Other **concerns**—**history**, **maturation**, and **testing effects**—arise when external events, normal development, or test familiarity alter performance. Collectively, these lead to **order effects**, which can make one condition appear better simply because it was encountered first or second.

To manage order effects, the lecture introduced **counterbalancing**. In **complete designs**, participants experience all possible condition orders, such as an **ABBA sequence** for two conditions or **block randomization** for three or more. However, the number of possible orders grows factorially, so full counterbalancing is feasible only when tasks are very short. **Incomplete designs** balance order exposure without testing every arrangement—through **random order with rotation** (participant count must match number of conditions) or a **Latin‑square design**, ensuring each condition appears equally often in each position. In cognitive studies with very many trials, randomly intermixing conditions can help cancel order effects.

The lecture concluded by noting practical limitations: some manipulations cannot be reversed—people cannot un‑take a drug, unlearn a skill, or unsee a stimulus—so within‑subjects designs are powerful but not always possible.
