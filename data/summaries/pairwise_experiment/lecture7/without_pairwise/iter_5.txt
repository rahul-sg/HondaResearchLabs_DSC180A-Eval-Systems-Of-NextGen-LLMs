This lecture introduced **within-subjects designs**, contrasting them with **between-groups designs** to show how researchers can reduce noise and increase power. In a between-groups study, each participant provides data for only one condition, and differences among individuals add nuisance variability that can obscure true effects. In a within-subjects design, each participant experiences all conditions and is compared to themself across those conditions. This structure removes *at least some* participant-related noise, making tests more powerful and requiring fewer participants—twenty participants can yield twenty data points per condition instead of the forty needed for a comparable between-groups study.

Three key formats were outlined: **pretest–posttest designs**, with measurements before and after an intervention; **repeated-measures designs**, involving multiple condition levels (e.g., 10%, 20%, 30%, 40% coherence); and **longitudinal designs**, which track the same participants across extended time points. These approaches provide fine-grained data while minimizing nuisance variability.

The lecture then emphasized potential challenges within-subjects studies introduce. Because participants encounter all conditions, they may detect the manipulation (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects**, where performance in one condition influences later ones. Carryover can take the form of **fatigue** (declining performance) or **practice** (improvement with repetition). Additional **history**, **maturation**, and **testing effects** also threaten validity. Slides illustrated these with concrete examples: anxiety or lost sleep from midterms, distraction from major world events or campus festivals, vocabulary growth due to natural development, or improved scores simply from retaking a test. These cases show how external and time-based factors can confound interpretations of an intervention’s true effect.

To handle **order effects**, researchers use **counterbalancing**. In **complete designs**, participants experience all possible condition orders—for instance, **ABBA** sequences for two conditions or **block randomization** when three or more conditions are present. When every possible order is impractical, **incomplete designs** such as **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square designs** ensure fair distribution of conditions across participants. In some cognitive tasks with many trials, conditions may be randomly intermixed to average out order effects. Yet counterbalancing has clear limits: it cannot reverse irreversible experiences such as taking a drug, undergoing surgery, or learning a skill. Within these boundaries, within-subjects designs remain an elegant, efficient way to reduce variability and strengthen experimental inference.
