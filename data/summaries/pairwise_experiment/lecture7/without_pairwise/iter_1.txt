This lecture focuses on within-subjects designs as a methodological alternative to between-groups designs. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, so differences among participants add noise that can obscure effects. In contrast, a within-subjects design tests each participant in all conditions, enabling comparisons within individuals rather than across groups. This structure removes some participant-related variability, increases statistical power, and requires fewer participants to achieve the same number of data points per condition.

Several specific within-subjects formats were introduced. A **pretest–posttest design** involves two measurements surrounding an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** includes two or more conditions within the same session (for example, different levels of a stimulus). A **longitudinal design** repeatedly assesses participants across time, providing rich data but requiring extended duration. The key advantages of all within-subjects designs are efficiency—fewer participants needed—and reduced nuisance variability, resulting in a more powerful statistical test.

However, these benefits come with challenges. Participants exposed to all conditions may discern the purpose of the study (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects**, where experience in one condition influences performance in the next. Such effects include **fatigue** (declining performance), **practice** (improvement through learning), **history** (external events coinciding with the study), **maturation** (changes due to time or aging), and **testing** (familiarity with repeated measures). While these are not always “catches,” researchers must recognize when they may confound results.

**Order effects** occur when the sequence of conditions affects outcomes. One remedy is **counterbalancing**, which deliberately varies condition order across participants. Complete counterbalancing methods include **ABBA designs** (useful with two conditions, controlling linear practice effects) and **block randomization** (cycling through all possible orders of three or more conditions). When the number of conditions makes full counterbalancing impractical, **incomplete designs**—such as **random order with rotation** or the **Latin-square design**—ensure each condition appears equally often in each order position and follows every other condition equally often. As emphasized in the lecture, counterbalancing “can get pretty complex pretty quickly—but it’s worth it.”

In cognitive science studies with many short trials, researchers may instead intermix conditions randomly to cancel order effects, unless doing so would confuse participants. Nonetheless, counterbalancing has limits for irreversible manipulations such as drug administration, surgery, or exposure to unique stimuli. Overall, within-subjects designs provide a powerful and often efficient approach when applied appropriately and with careful control of order and carryover effects.
