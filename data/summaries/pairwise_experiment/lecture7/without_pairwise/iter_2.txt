This lecture introduces **within-subjects designs** as an alternative to **between-groups designs**. In a between-groups study, each participant contributes to a single condition, and individual differences introduce noise that can obscure real effects. A within-subjects design, in contrast, tests each participant in all conditions, allowing each person to serve as their own comparison. This design reduces participant-related variability, increases statistical power, and requires fewer participants to reach the same number of observations per condition.

Several common within-subjects formats were outlined. A **pretest–posttest design** measures the same participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** tests several conditions within a single session (for example, varying task difficulty). A **longitudinal design** collects data across extended time spans to track changes, though it requires patience and consistent follow-up. Overall, within-subjects designs are efficient and statistically powerful because they control noise and make fuller use of each participant’s data.

The lecture also highlighted possible drawbacks. Participants who experience every condition may detect the experimental purpose (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects**, where performance in one condition alters the next. Carryover can appear as **fatigue** (declining performance), **practice** (improvement through learning), or as confounds from **history**, **maturation**, or **testing** effects. For instance, external events like exams or aging processes might mimic treatment effects, and repeated testing can change how participants respond. The accent comprehension example—“Do children understand speech better when it’s in their own accent?”—illustrates how responses across repeated, related conditions must be interpreted cautiously within individuals.

**Order effects** occur when sequence influences performance. One solution is to vary the order through **counterbalancing**. Complete counterbalancing methods include **ABBA designs** (effective with two conditions) and **block randomization** (for three or more). When all possible orders become impractical, **incomplete designs** such as **random order with rotation** or a **Latin-square design** ensure that each condition appears in each position and after each other condition equally often. In cognitive science tasks with many short trials, researchers may instead **randomly intermix** conditions to cancel order effects—but should **counterbalance** when there are few items or each trial is lengthy. Still, counterbalancing has limits for irreversible manipulations like drug administration or unique experiences. With careful control of order and carryover influences, within-subjects designs offer an elegant and efficient approach to experimental research.
