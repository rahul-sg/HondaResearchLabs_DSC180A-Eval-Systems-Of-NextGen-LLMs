This lecture examined **within-subjects designs**, contrasting them with **between-groups designs** to show how researchers can reduce nuisance variability and strengthen statistical power. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to only one condition, and individual differences create unwanted noise that obscures effects—often requiring a larger sample. By contrast, a within-subjects design has each participant experience all conditions, allowing direct comparison of a person’s own performance across conditions and removing much participant-related variability. This means fewer participants are needed to achieve the same precision.

Three major within-subjects formats were outlined. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention; a **repeated-measures design** includes two or more levels of the independent variable (for example, 10%, 20%, 30%, and 40% coherence in a perceptual task); and a **longitudinal design** follows the same individuals over extended periods, offering rich insight but requiring substantial time. These approaches all benefit from reduced variability and more powerful tests, but each can introduce particular challenges.

The lecture highlighted several empirical illustrations of these principles. Slides on children’s **accent comprehension** asked whether 4‑ and 7‑year‑olds understand speech better in their own accent—a case showing how participant age and condition order might interact. Another example, the **spaced‑text study on dyslexia**, tested whether extra‑wide letter spacing improves reading for children with dyslexia, prompting discussion about whether the design was within‑subjects or between‑groups and how order balancing matters for interpretation.

Key threats unique to within‑subjects designs include **demand characteristics**, when participants infer the study’s purpose, and **carryover effects**, where performance in one condition influences later ones through fatigue or practice. Additional concerns—**history**, **maturation**, and **testing effects**—arise when outside events, natural developmental changes, or repeated testing alter outcomes independently of the manipulation. These all contribute to **order effects**, which the lecture illustrated with examples showing how condition sequence can distort results.

To manage order effects, researchers use **counterbalancing**. In **complete designs**, participants experience all possible condition orders, such as an **ABBA sequence** for two conditions or **block randomization** when there are three or more. **Incomplete designs** (e.g., **random order with rotation** or the **Latin‑square method**) ensure that each condition appears equally often in each position without exhausting every order. In cognitive studies with many brief trials, random intermixing can also cancel order effects. Yet the lecture closed on a cautious note: some experiences cannot be undone—people cannot “un‑take” a drug or “unlearn” a skill—so researchers must judge when within‑subjects approaches remain appropriate.
