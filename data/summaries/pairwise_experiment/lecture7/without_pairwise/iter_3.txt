This lecture introduces **within-subjects designs** as a contrast to **between-groups designs**. In a between-groups design, each participant contributes data to just one condition, so differences between individuals add variability—or noise—that can mask real effects. A within-subjects design has each participant complete all conditions, comparing each person to themselves across conditions. This removes much of the participant-related noise, resulting in a more powerful statistical test. It is also far more efficient: for example, to obtain 20 data points per condition, a between-groups study may require 40 participants, whereas a within-subjects study needs only 20.

Several major within-subjects formats were outlined. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** involves multiple conditions within the same session (e.g., different motion coherence levels: 10%, 20%, 30%, 40%). A **longitudinal design** measures performance over long time spans, offering rich developmental data but requiring patience and consistency. These approaches all share the benefit of reducing nuisance variability and using participants efficiently.

The lecture also discussed limitations and threats to validity. Participants who experience all conditions may guess the study’s purpose (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects** that alter later performance. Carryover can appear as **fatigue** (worse later), **practice** (better with experience), or broader **history**, **maturation**, and **testing** effects. For example, midterms or aging could influence improvement independently of an intervention, and retaking a test may enhance performance simply through familiarity. Illustrations included the **accent comprehension study**, in which children’s performance across speech conditions had to be interpreted in light of within-person comparisons, and the **dyslexia spacing study**, which asked whether wider letter spacing improved reading—an example of manipulating conditions within participants to isolate an effect.

A recurring concern in such designs is **order effects**, where sequence alone changes outcomes. To address these, researchers use **counterbalancing**. Complete methods include **ABBA designs** (for two conditions) and **block randomization** (for three or more). When all possible orders are impractical, **incomplete designs**—such as **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square designs**—ensure balanced exposure across participants. In cognitive experiments with many rapid trials, conditions can also be **randomly intermixed** to cancel order effects, though counterbalancing remains important when tasks are long or confusing. Finally, counterbalancing has limits for irreversible manipulations such as drugs or training. With thoughtful use of these controls, within-subjects designs remain an elegant and efficient way to reveal genuine experimental effects.
