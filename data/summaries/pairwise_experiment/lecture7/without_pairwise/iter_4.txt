This lecture focused on **within-subjects designs**, contrasting them with **between-groups designs**. In a between-groups study, each participant provides data for only one condition, so differences among individuals add nuisance variability (noise) that can obscure true effects. In a within-subjects study, each participant experiences all conditions, and results are compared within the same person. This approach removes much of the participant-related noise and yields a more powerful, efficient test: twenty participants can supply twenty data points per condition, whereas a between-groups study would require twice as many.

Key types of within-subjects designs include the **pretest–posttest design** (measuring before and after an intervention), **repeated-measures design** (collecting data across multiple condition levels, such as 10%, 20%, 30%, 40% coherence), and **longitudinal design** (tracking change across extended time points). These structures share the advantages of smaller sample requirements and reduced variability, making them powerful tools for detecting experimental effects.

However, several limitations and validity threats accompany this efficiency. When participants encounter all conditions, they may infer the study’s purpose (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects**, where performance in one condition influences another. Carryover can appear as **fatigue** (declining performance) or **practice** (improvement with repetition). Time-based influences such as **history**, **maturation**, and **testing effects** also complicate interpretation. For instance, external events, natural development, or familiarity with a repeated test can change outcomes independently of any manipulation. The slides used brief research examples—such as children’s accent comprehension and spaced text for dyslexia—as prompts to recognize how within-person comparisons work in applied contexts.

To manage **order effects**, researchers often apply **counterbalancing**. In **complete designs**, participants experience all condition orders (e.g., **ABBA** sequences for two conditions or **block randomization** for three or more). When full ordering is infeasible, **incomplete designs**—such as **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square arrangements**—distribute condition orders evenly across participants. Some cognitive tasks rely instead on random intermixing of trials to cancel sequence effects. Importantly, counterbalancing has **practical limits**: it cannot reverse irreversible experiences, such as taking a drug or learning a skill. When carefully planned and bounded by these constraints, within-subjects designs offer an elegant and efficient way to reduce noise and strengthen experimental inference.
