This lecture introduced **within-subjects designs**, contrasting them with **between-groups designs** to show how researchers can reduce nuisance variability and increase statistical power. In a between-groups study, each participant contributes data to just one condition, and differences among people add noise that makes effects harder to detect—for instance, doubling the number of participants needed per condition. In a within-subjects design, each participant completes all conditions and is compared to themself across those conditions. This setup removes at least some participant-related noise, allowing smaller samples to yield clearer results.

The lecture outlined three common forms. A **pretest–posttest design** measures participants before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** tests participants at two or more condition levels (e.g., 10%, 20%, 30%, 40% coherence). A **longitudinal design** tracks performance across multiple time points, offering rich data but often taking substantial time to complete. Across these formats, the central benefit is higher power from reduced variability and fewer required participants.

However, the instructor emphasized that within-subjects designs can introduce new threats. Because participants experience all conditions, they may guess the purpose of the study (**demand characteristics**) or show **carryover effects** when performance in one condition influences another. These can appear as **fatigue** (doing worse as time passes) or **practice** (doing better through repetition). Additional sources of bias include **history effects**—external events like exams or campus festivals that coincide with data collection; **maturation effects**, such as developmental or aging-related changes mistaken for treatment outcomes; and **testing effects**, in which familiarity with a repeated measure alters later responses. Slides invited the audience to spot “what’s wrong with this picture?” in examples where order or timing created these confounds.

To handle such **order effects**, researchers apply **counterbalancing**. In **complete designs**, participants experience all possible orderings of conditions, such as an **ABBA sequence** for two conditions or **block randomization** for three or more. When all orders are impractical, **incomplete designs** like **random order with rotation** or **Latin-square designs** ensure that each condition appears equally often in each position. In cognitive studies with very noisy dependent variables and many trials, conditions may instead be randomly intermixed—a strategy that effectively cancels order effects—but formal counterbalancing is preferred when tasks are few or lengthy. The lecture concluded by noting that counterbalancing has practical limits: one cannot “un-take” a drug, “unlearn” a skill, or fully reverse experience, yet within-subjects designs remain an elegant way to reduce variability and reveal reliable effects.
