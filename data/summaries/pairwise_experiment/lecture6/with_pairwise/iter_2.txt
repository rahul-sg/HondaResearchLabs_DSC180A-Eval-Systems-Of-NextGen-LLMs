This lecture focused on nested SQL queries, showing how subqueries appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, and **WHERE** clauses, how they can often be transformed through *unnesting*, and how these behaviors relate to logical quantifiers and query monotonicity. Building on earlier topics such as joins, grouping, and aggregates, it defined a *subquery* as a query embedded within another that may return either a scalar value or a relation. Subqueries are useful for expressing conditions that require intermediate computation, though the rule of thumb is to avoid them when possible because join‑based (unnested) forms are typically more efficient—except in cases where equivalence does not hold.

In the **SELECT** clause, examples included retrieving an actor’s movie genre, the average actor salary in movies rated above 9, and the number of actors per film. These correlated subqueries can often be replaced with joins and GROUP BY clauses through *subquery unnesting*. However, the slides cautioned that equivalence depends on join semantics: for instance, when a LEFT OUTER JOIN is needed to preserve rows without matches, a simple unnesting may alter results.

Subqueries in the **FROM** clause serve as derived tables or *common table expressions (CTEs)*, enabling *subquery refactoring*. The lecture illustrated this by defining an intermediate relation of movies with ratings over 8, then filtering for those below 9. These subqueries are non‑correlated and improve readability while maintaining results identical to the direct form.

The **WHERE** clause examples introduced logical quantifiers. *Existential quantifiers* use `EXISTS` or `IN` to test whether at least one matching record exists and can usually be rewritten as joins. *Universal quantifiers* express “for all” conditions indirectly because SQL lacks a direct construct. Queries such as finding actors who acted **only** in Action movies or movies where **all** actors earned over $100K used `NOT IN` or `NOT EXISTS` patterns to simulate universals; the `< ALL` form was shown but noted as unsupported in SQLite. The lecture also included a quantitative variant—retrieving actors who acted in *at most two* action films—demonstrating count‑based constraints formulated with subqueries.

Finally, the lecture connected quantifiers to **query monotonicity**. A simple SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE query without subqueries or aggregates is monotone: adding input tuples never removes output tuples. Such monotone queries can be safely unnested. Queries with negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates are **non‑monotone**, meaning added data may retract results, and therefore cannot always be unnested.
