This lecture examined nested SQL queries, focusing on subqueries in the SELECT, FROM, and WHERE clauses, their optimization through unnesting, and the relationship between quantifiers and query monotonicity. Building on core SQL concepts such as projections, joins, grouping, and aggregates, it formalized subqueries as queries embedded within other queries that can produce a single value or an entire relation. While subqueries can simplify logical expression, the guiding rule was to avoid them when possible because unnested (join-based) forms tend to be clearer and more efficient.

In the SELECT clause, examples showed subqueries retrieving values such as each actor’s movie genre, the count of actors per movie, and the average salary of actors in highly rated films. Equivalent results could often be derived using joins and GROUP BY, illustrating “subquery unnesting.” However, the slides also questioned whether these forms remain equivalent under different join semantics—for example, when LEFT OUTER JOIN is required to preserve rows without matches, equivalence may break.  

Subqueries in the FROM clause can define intermediate results, termed inline views or common table expressions (CTEs). The lecture illustrated this using a derived table of movies with ratings above 8, filtered again by an outer query to find those below 9. Such refactoring enhances readability while preserving semantics, and these subqueries are not correlated with the outer query.

The WHERE clause examples centered on existential and universal quantifiers. Using EXISTS or IN expresses existential conditions—true when one or more matching tuples exist—and can be rewritten as standard joins. In contrast, universal quantifiers (“for all”) require indirect expression because SQL offers no direct construct. Queries such as finding actors who only acted in Action movies or movies where all actors earn over \$100K used negated subqueries like NOT IN or NOT EXISTS to simulate “for every.” The slides highlighted that SQLite does not support the `< ALL` operator for expressing universal comparison, leading to the NOT EXISTS formulation as the practical alternative.

The lecture concluded with query monotonicity. A SELECT‑FROM‑WHERE query without subqueries or aggregates is provably monotone: adding tuples to input tables never removes tuples from results. Monotone queries can therefore be safely unnested. Non‑monotone queries—those involving negation, universal quantifiers, or aggregates—cannot, since adding data may retract results. This distinction formally determines when subquery unnesting is possible.
