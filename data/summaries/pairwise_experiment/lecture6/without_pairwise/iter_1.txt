This lecture examined **nested SQL queries**, focusing on how subqueries operate within different clauses and how their structure affects efficiency and expressiveness. It built on prior coverage of basic SQL operations—projections, selections, joins, grouping, and aggregation—then introduced subqueries as queries embedded within other queries. These can appear in the **SELECT**, **FROM**, or **WHERE** clauses and may return either a single value or a relation. The lecture emphasized a core efficiency principle: **avoid nested queries when possible**, as they are typically more costly to evaluate, though sometimes necessary for certain conditions.

In the **SELECT clause**, subqueries were shown to compute attribute values such as movie genre, actor count, and average salary. These correlated subqueries reference outer query variables. Equivalent results can often be obtained by “**unnesting**” the query—rewriting it as a join with aggregation. Several examples demonstrated how subquery unnesting provides the same output while improving performance, though equivalence may depend on the use of inner versus outer joins.

Subqueries in the **FROM clause** can be used to define intermediate results or temporary tables, either inline or through **common table expressions (CTEs)**, to simplify complex filtering logic. The lecture referred to this as *subquery refactoring* and showed that such subqueries can also be unnested into equivalent simple queries.

In the **WHERE clause**, subqueries express logical conditions. The lecture first covered **existential quantifiers**—implemented using the `EXISTS` or `IN` operators—to test whether related tuples exist or belong to a set. These were shown to be straightforward and equivalent to join conditions. On the other hand, **universal quantifiers** (“for all”) are more challenging: the slides emphasized that SQL constructs covered so far **do not have the capability to handle universal quantifiers directly**. Workarounds using `NOT IN` or `NOT EXISTS` were shown to retrieve, for example, actors who only acted in action movies or movies in which all actors earn above $100K.

The lecture closed with the concept of **monotone and non-monotone queries**. A query is monotone if adding tuples to input relations never removes existing output tuples. Simplified SELECT-FROM-WHERE queries without subqueries or aggregates are monotone and can be unnested safely. In contrast, queries involving **negation, aggregation, or universal quantifiers** are non-monotone, meaning their rewriting into simpler joins can change results. This theoretical distinction explains when and why subquery unnesting is feasible.
