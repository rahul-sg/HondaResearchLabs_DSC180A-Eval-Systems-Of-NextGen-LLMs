The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces students to both the goals of the course and the fundamental framework of financial accounting. The class focuses on how accounting records, organizes, and communicates information about business activities through financial statements to support decision-making across all fields. Key objectives include understanding how financial statements are created, interpreting their meaning, and using accounting information to make informed business judgments.

Financial accounting is described as the “language of business” that tells a company’s financial story—its successes, challenges, and risks. It serves two primary functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which provides information to external decision makers through reports. Users of this information include investors assessing management performance and financial condition, creditors and suppliers determining repayment or reliability, managers and boards evaluating operations and strategy, and other stakeholders such as employees, customers, analysts, and regulators.

The lecture introduces accounting’s measurement of three central business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and creditors providing funds), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of productive resources like equipment or inventory), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production, marketing, and administrative transactions). These activities culminate in four key reports—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—which together convey a company’s financial position and performance.

Financial reporting is governed by **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under the oversight of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**. Public firms must issue periodic reports (10‑K annual, 10‑Q quarterly, and 8‑K current reports). Internationally, standards are set by the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** under **IFRS**. Independent **auditors** add credibility by evaluating whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements, issuing one of four opinions ranging from unqualified to adverse.

The lecture concludes with emphasis on the **balance sheet**, which presents the company’s financial position at a specific date and must satisfy the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Examples illustrate how financing, investing, and operational transactions—such as issuing stock, borrowing funds, buying equipment, paying prepaid rent, or purchasing supplies—affect the equation yet always keep it balanced. This foundation sets the stage for analyzing how every business transaction shapes financial statements and decision‑useful accounting information.
