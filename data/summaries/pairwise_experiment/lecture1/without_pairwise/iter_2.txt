The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the purpose of the course and the essential framework of financial accounting. Students are introduced to accounting as the means of recording business transactions, producing financial statements, and using this information to make informed business decisions across disciplines. Course objectives emphasize understanding how financial statements are created, read, and applied in decision‑making.

Accounting is presented as the *language of business*, telling a company’s financial story—its performance, risks, and opportunities. It fulfills two central functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to decision makers through financial reports. Users of accounting information include investors evaluating performance, creditors assessing repayment ability, suppliers considering trade credit, managers and boards reviewing operations, and other stakeholders such as employees, customers, regulators, and analysts.

Financial accounting measures three main business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and lenders that provide funds), **investing** (purchases and disposals of productive assets like equipment or inventory), and **operating** (day‑to‑day transactions for production, sales, and administration). These activities feed into four fundamental financial statements—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—which together summarize a company’s position and performance.

The reporting process follows **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)** established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under oversight of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**. Public firms issue required reports: the annual 10‑K with audited statements, the quarterly 10‑Q, and event‑based 8‑K filings. Globally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** sets **IFRS** standards. Independent **auditors** enhance the credibility of financial statements by expressing an opinion—unqualified, qualified, or adverse—on whether they fairly present results in accordance with GAAP.

The lecture concludes with detailed work on the **balance sheet**, which reports assets, liabilities, and stockholders’ equity at a specific date, following the **accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Step‑by‑step “Klein, Inc.” examples show this balance in action: issuing stock and borrowing funds increase both assets and claims to them; purchasing equipment or prepaid rent decreases one asset while creating another; and buying supplies on credit increases both assets and liabilities. Each transaction’s dual effect keeps the equation balanced, illustrating how every business event shapes a company’s financial position.
