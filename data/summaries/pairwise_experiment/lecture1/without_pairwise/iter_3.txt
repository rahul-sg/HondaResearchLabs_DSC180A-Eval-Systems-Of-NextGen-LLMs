The opening lecture of *Principles of Accounting* introduces the scope and framework of financial accounting and how it supports informed business decisions. The course begins with the purpose of accounting—recording business transactions, generating financial statements, and interpreting those statements to assess a company’s performance and condition.

Accounting is described as the **language of business**, communicating a company’s economic story—its successes, risks, and opportunities. It fulfills two main functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to external users through financial reports. Key users of accounting information include investors evaluating management performance, creditors assessing repayment capacity, suppliers determining credit terms, managers and boards overseeing operations, and broader stakeholders such as employees, customers, regulators, and analysts.

Financial accounting measures three categories of business activities. **Financing activities** involve transactions with investors and creditors that supply capital. **Investing activities** cover acquiring and disposing of resources needed for operations, such as equipment or inventory. **Operating activities** encompass day‑to‑day processes—production, marketing, and sales. These activities culminate in four central financial statements: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**, each capturing different aspects of a company’s financial position and performance.

The reporting process operates under **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, established by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under authority delegated by the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**. Public firms release audited annual reports (10‑K), unaudited quarterly reports (10‑Q), and event‑specific updates (8‑K). Internationally, the **IASB** issues **IFRS** standards. Independent **auditors** enhance reliability by assessing whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements. Their opinions may be *unqualified*, *unqualified with explanatory or emphasis paragraph*, *qualified*, or *adverse*—each communicating a different level of assurance.

The session concludes with the **balance sheet** and the **accounting equation**—*Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity*—demonstrated through the *Klein, Inc.* example. Transactions such as issuing stock, obtaining loans, purchasing equipment, paying rent in advance, or buying supplies on credit reveal that every financial event has a dual effect, preserving the equation’s balance and illustrating how accounting records represent the firm’s financial position at any point in time.
