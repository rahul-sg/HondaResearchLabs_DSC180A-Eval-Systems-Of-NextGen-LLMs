This lecture strengthens foundational accounting concepts by showing how financial statements connect and how transactions are recorded through the double-entry framework. It begins with the idea that because economic activity is complex, accounting requires judgment and critical thinking to represent business events accurately. Companies communicate financial information through four linked statements—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows. The balance sheet reports position at specific points in time, while the others describe performance over a fiscal period. The slides highlight how these statements link: net income and revenues increase stockholders’ equity, while expenses and losses decrease it, creating continuity between periods.  

The accounting equation, **Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity**, is gradually expanded to show that equity includes contributed capital and retained earnings. Retained earnings change with net income and dividends, and when expressed in detail, the equation becomes **Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + (Beginning Retained Earnings + Revenues – Expenses – Dividends)**. This expanded form clarifies how performance transactions drive changes in equity and maintains the balance sheet relationship over time. Ending balances at period‑end become beginning balances in the next period, reinforcing the equation’s continuous nature.  

The lecture then develops the double‑entry system, showing that every transaction has a give and a take and affects at least two classes—assets, liabilities, or equity. Each transaction is recorded with equal debits and credits so that the equation remains balanced. Debits increase assets, expenses, and dividends, while credits increase liabilities, contributed capital, and revenues. The “DEALOR” mnemonic (Dividends, Expenses, Assets—Debit; Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, Revenues—Credit) summarizes these normal balances. The “Super T‑account” integrates all categories, demonstrating how debits and credits apply consistently across account types.  

Step‑by‑step examples—borrowing funds, acquiring equipment or inventory, paying suppliers, prepaying insurance, and issuing shares—illustrate how transactions change accounts. The process moves from journal entries, where debits and credits are first recorded, to posting in T‑accounts, where entries are aggregated and ending balances determined. Slides emphasize that properly posted ending balances form the next period’s starting point, ensuring continuity in financial records.  

The lecture concludes by summarizing the accounting process: (1) analyze each transaction, (2) record debits and credits in journal form, and (3) post these to the ledger. Throughout, the rule **Debits = Credits** guarantees that every account remains balanced and that the accounting equation always holds.
