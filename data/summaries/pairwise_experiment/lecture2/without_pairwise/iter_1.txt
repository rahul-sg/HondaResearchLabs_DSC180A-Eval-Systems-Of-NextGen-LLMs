This lecture extends the foundation of accounting by illustrating how financial statements connect to one another and how double-entry recordkeeping maintains those relationships. It begins by reaffirming that because economic activity is complex, accounting involves judgment and critical thinking to communicate financial information accurately through structured reports.  

The discussion revisits the four core financial statements—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—as complementary parts of a communication system. The balance sheet reports a company’s position at specific points in time (beginning and end of period), while the income statement, equity statement, and cash flow statement describe performance and changes across a fiscal year. Net income increases stockholders’ equity; a net loss reduces it. Revenues raise equity, expenses decrease it, and the combined effect flows through retained earnings on the statement of stockholders’ equity, linking all statements across periods.  

The lecture then expands the accounting equation: Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity. Equity is divided into contributed capital and retained earnings, which evolve with revenues, expenses, and dividends through time. The expanded form—Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Beginning Retained Earnings + Revenues – Expenses – Dividends—shows how each transaction affects both accounts and time periods, preserving balance sheet equality.  

Building on this framework, the lecture introduces double-entry accounting, emphasizing that every economic event has two sides—a give and a take—and must affect at least two accounts. Each entry uses debits and credits, where debits increase assets and expenses but decrease liabilities and equity, while credits do the opposite. Every transaction requires at least one debit and one credit, and **no negative numbers are permitted**; equality of debits and credits ensures that the accounting equation always remains balanced.  

Through examples—borrowing from a bank, buying equipment or inventory, paying suppliers, purchasing insurance, and issuing stock—students see how journal entries translate into T-accounts. Ending balances summarize all changes and become the next period’s beginning balances. The lecture concludes by restating the accounting process: analyze transactions, record debits and credits in journals, and post them to ledgers. The principle that “debits equal credits” safeguards both the structure and integrity of financial statements within the accounting system.
