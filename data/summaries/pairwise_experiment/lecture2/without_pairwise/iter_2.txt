This lecture deepens the foundation of accounting by showing how financial statements interconnect and how double-entry recordkeeping maintains those links. It begins by emphasizing that because economic activity is complex, accounting demands critical judgment and systematic communication through financial statements.  

The four primary statements—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—operate as a unified reporting system. The balance sheet presents resources and claims at specific points in time (beginning and end of a period), while the income, equity, and cash flow statements describe performance and changes during the fiscal year. Net income increases stockholders’ equity; a net loss decreases it. Revenues raise equity, expenses lower it, and the effects flow through retained earnings, connecting the statements across periods.  

The lecture then expands the accounting equation from **Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity** to show how equity evolves:  
**Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Beginning Retained Earnings + Revenues – Expenses – Dividends.**  
This version highlights how business activities—earning revenue, incurring expenses, and distributing dividends—affect both the balance sheet and income statement while preserving equality.  

Building on this structure, the concept of **double-entry accounting** is introduced. Every economic event has two sides—a give and a take—and must be recorded in at least two accounts so the accounting equation always balances. Debits (left side) increase assets and expenses but decrease liabilities and equity; credits (right side) have the opposite effect. Every transaction must include at least one debit and one credit, and **no negative numbers are used**—this rule ensures that total debits always equal total credits.  

Examples such as bank loans, equipment purchases, inventory transactions, insurance payments, and stock issuance illustrate how accounts respond to each transaction. The **Super T‑account** and **DEALOR mnemonic** (Dividends, Expenses, Assets = Debit; Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, Revenue = Credit) support recall of these debit‑credit rules. Ending balances derived from T‑accounts feed directly into the balance sheet and become the next period’s beginning balances.  

Finally, the lecture outlines the accounting process: analyze each transaction, record it in journal entries, and post those entries to the ledger’s T‑accounts, where balances are aggregated. The core principle—that **debits equal credits**—upholds the integrity and internal consistency of the financial reporting system.
