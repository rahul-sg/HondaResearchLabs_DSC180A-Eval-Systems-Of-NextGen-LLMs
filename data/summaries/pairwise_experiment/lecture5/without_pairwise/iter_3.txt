The lecture explored how race and culture are socially constructed systems that influence identity, law, and schooling, while also inviting students to examine their own experiences and assumptions. It opened with Sonia Nieto’s argument that society categorizes people by visible and invisible traits—such as race, ethnicity, gender, or class—attaching exaggerated meanings that privilege some groups and harm others. Through questions like “Did teachers label you?” and “Did those labels shape how you saw yourself?,” students were prompted to reflect on labeling as a lived example of social construction. This dialogic approach connected personal experience to the theme of **Silence, Fear, and Denial**, emphasizing that fear of discussing race or cultural difference is common but must be countered with courage to confront how upbringing and social context shape one’s views.

Slides then clarified that race has no biological basis and is instead a historical and political construct that has become “common sense.” The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project was cited as scientific support for this view. Legal scholar Ian Haney López framed **law as coercion**, showing how exclusionary and immigration laws constrained physical appearance and belonging, and **law as ideology**, explaining how laws persuade people to accept racial hierarchies as natural. Legal cases involving Armenians, Japanese, and Mexicans, as well as the long ban on interracial marriage, illustrated how courts have continuously drawn and redrawn racial boundaries. James Baldwin’s observation that “no one was white before coming to America” highlighted how whiteness itself was legally and socially produced. Shifting U.S. Census categories showed how official classifications institutionalize these changing definitions of race and citizenship.

In the second half, the focus turned to **culture**. Nieto described culture as a complex, evolving process of shared values, relationships, and worldviews created and transformed by people—not inherited through biology. Cultural identities are multiple and hybrid, with individuals selecting and rejecting elements across contexts. Factors such as family, class, peers, language, religion, media, and schooling mediate cultural experience. Schools were presented as their own cultural institutions, shaped by assumptions that ability is innate and hierarchically ordered. Reflective prompts about what it means to be “smart” or a “bad” student encouraged students to question these embedded beliefs. The lecture closed by linking social constructions of race and culture to the need for reflective courage—an ongoing process of examining how educational structures and cultural norms shape understanding and opportunity.
