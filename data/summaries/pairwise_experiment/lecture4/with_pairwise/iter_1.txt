This lecture examined how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time. According to Darwin’s postulates, organisms face a struggle for existence, vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and transmit this variation to offspring. His difficulty was explaining how variation persisted if advantageous traits were favored and how new traits arose. Artificial selection in domesticated plants and animals, such as dogs and pigeons, illustrated that species can change rapidly when specific traits are chosen, showing evolution can occur in observable timeframes.  

Later research on heredity provided insight into the mechanisms underlying inheritance. Gregor Mendel’s experiments with pea plants in the 1850s and 1860s revealed that traits are determined by discrete hereditary “particles,” later known as genes, that are inherited from each parent without blending. This model explained how variation can be maintained across generations. In the early 1900s, Walther Flemming identified chromatin and described mitosis, while Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri demonstrated that chromosomes carry hereditary material, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Understanding chromosomes clarified how inheritance operates through cell division: mitosis produces genetically identical diploid somatic cells, whereas meiosis forms haploid gametes whose recombination and independent assortment generate genetic diversity.  

Mendel’s results further distinguished genotype—the underlying allele combination—from phenotype, the physical expression of a trait. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, and predictable outcomes from genetic crosses can be represented using tools like Punnett squares. Linkage and recombination studies showed that genes located close together on the same chromosome may be inherited jointly, though crossing over during meiosis can produce new combinations that enhance variation.  

Discoveries about DNA connected these genetic principles to molecular structure. Watson and Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x-ray data, described DNA’s double-helix form consisting of a sugar-phosphate backbone and paired bases (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional segments of DNA, while mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)—inherited only from mothers and more conserved than nuclear DNA—has enabled estimation of evolutionary relationships and dating of ancestral lineages. Analyses of mtDNA, for instance, identify “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all humans, who lived roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.
