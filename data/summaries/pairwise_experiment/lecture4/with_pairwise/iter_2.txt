This lecture traced how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Darwin’s postulates noted three conditions: organisms experience a struggle for existence, they vary in traits influencing survival and reproduction, and this variation is heritable. He faced challenges explaining how variation persisted if natural selection consistently favored certain traits and how new forms originated. Artificial selection in domesticated species such as dogs and pigeons illustrated that major changes can occur over only a few generations, emphasizing that selection can produce substantial modification.  

Research on heredity resolved Darwin’s uncertainty about variation. Gregor Mendel’s experiments with pea plants (1856–1863) showed that traits are determined by discrete hereditary “particles,” later termed genes, that are inherited from each parent without blending. This explained how variation is maintained from generation to generation. At the turn of the 20th century, Walther Flemming identified chromatin and described mitosis, while Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri independently demonstrated that chromosomes carry hereditary material, leading to the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Understanding chromosomes clarified that inheritance operates through two cell division processes: mitosis, which produces genetically identical diploid somatic cells, and meiosis, which produces haploid gametes. Mutation, recombination, and independent assortment during meiosis introduce genetic variation while maintaining constant chromosome numbers across generations.  

Mendel’s findings distinguished genotype—the specific allelic combination—from phenotype, the observable trait expression. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, and predictable offspring outcomes can be represented using a Punnett square. Linkage and recombination principles showed that genes close together on the same chromosome may be inherited together, but crossing over during meiosis can create new genetic combinations, thereby increasing variation. The recombination rate (r) expresses how frequently a chromosomal crossover occurs between two genes.  

Subsequent discoveries linked these patterns to molecular structure. Watson and Crick, drawing on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray evidence, described DNA’s double‑helix structure with a sugar‑phosphate backbone and complementary base pairs (A‑T, C‑G). Genes are functional segments of DNA, and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), found only in mitochondria and transmitted maternally without recombination, has enabled estimates of evolutionary timing. Comparisons of mtDNA identify “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of all humans, who lived roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
