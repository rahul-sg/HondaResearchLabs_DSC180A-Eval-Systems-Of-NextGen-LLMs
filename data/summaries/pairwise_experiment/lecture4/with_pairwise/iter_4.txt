This lecture traced how the concept of natural selection connects to the genetic mechanisms that make evolution possible. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Darwin’s postulates identified that organisms face a struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and this variation is heritable. Yet Darwin could not explain exactly how variation was inherited or maintained, leading to questions about blending inheritance and the origin of new traits. Artificial selection in plants and animals—such as selective breeding in dogs and pigeons—demonstrated how substantial change can occur over just a few generations. Examples such as rapidly evolving minnows and the gradual evolution of the eye illustrated that evolutionary change can proceed at both fast and gradual rates.  

Gregor Mendel’s pea plant experiments (1856–1863) revealed that inheritance operates through discrete hereditary “particles,” now recognized as genes, which are inherited from each parent without blending. This discovery explained how variation persists. Around the turn of the 20th century, work by Walther Flemming, Walter Sutton, and Theodor Boveri showed that these hereditary factors lie on chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Understanding chromosomes was key to understanding inheritance, which in turn is key to understanding evolution. Chromosomes contain an organism’s DNA arranged in homologous pairs, and their behavior during cell division explains how genetic information is transmitted. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells, while meiosis halves the chromosome number to produce haploid gametes. Meiosis introduces variation through mutation and recombination while maintaining a stable chromosome count across generations.  

Mendel distinguished genotype—the specific allele combination—from phenotype—the physical expression of a trait. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, and offspring outcomes can be predicted using tools like Punnett squares. During meiosis, crossing over allows genes that are close together to recombine, creating new allele combinations. The recombination rate (r) measures how often such crossover events occur, thereby increasing genetic diversity.  

In 1953, James Watson and Francis Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, identified DNA’s double‑helix structure of complementary base pairs (A–T, C–G) along a sugar‑phosphate backbone. Genes are functional DNA sequences, about 20,000–25,000 in humans. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), inherited only from mothers without recombination, provides evidence for a common matrilineal ancestor—“Mitochondrial Eve”—from about 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa. Comparisons of DNA form the basis of the molecular clock, used to estimate when species diverged.
