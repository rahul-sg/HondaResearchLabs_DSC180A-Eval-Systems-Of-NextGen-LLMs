This lecture connected natural selection with genetic inheritance to show how evolutionary change occurs. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Darwin’s postulates stated that organisms face a struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and this variation is heritable. However, Darwin faced difficulties explaining how variation persisted if selection eliminated it and how new forms appeared. Artificial selection in domesticated animals, such as dogs and pigeons, demonstrated that dramatic change can occur within a few generations when selection is strong. Comparative examples of evolutionary rate, such as minnows evolving placenta-like structures rapidly and the gradual evolution of the eye over about one million years, illustrated that evolution can proceed at either rapid or incremental tempos.  

Mendel’s pea plant experiments (1856–1863) revealed that inheritance operates through discrete hereditary “particles,” later known as genes, inherited from each parent without blending. This explained how variation is maintained across generations. Around the turn of the 20th century, cytological studies by Walther Flemming, Walter Sutton, and Theodor Boveri established that hereditary material resides in chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Understanding chromosomes clarified that inheritance depends on two division processes: mitosis, producing identical diploid cells, and meiosis, producing haploid gametes. Meiosis introduces variation through mutation and recombination while maintaining a constant chromosome number across generations.  

Mendel’s findings distinguished genotype—the allele combination—from phenotype, the expressed trait. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, allowing prediction of offspring outcomes using tools such as Punnett squares. Linkage and recombination principles showed that genes close together on a chromosome may be inherited together, but crossing over during meiosis can create new combinations of alleles. The recombination rate (r) quantifies how often a crossover occurs between genes, contributing to genetic diversity.  

Molecular discoveries unified these principles. In 1953, Watson and Crick, building on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, identified DNA’s double‑helix structure of paired bases (A–T, C–G) along a sugar‑phosphate backbone. Genes are functional sequences within DNA, numbering about 20,000–25,000 in humans. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), inherited maternally without recombination, retains conserved sequences useful for tracing ancestry. It underlies studies such as Mitochondrial Eve—our most recent matrilineal ancestor from about 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa—and the molecular clock method, which compares DNA to estimate when species diverged.
