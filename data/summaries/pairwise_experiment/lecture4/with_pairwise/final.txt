This lecture traced the development of evolutionary theory from Darwin’s ideas on natural selection to the genetic and molecular mechanisms that explain how evolution occurs. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time. Darwin proposed that organisms struggle for existence, individuals vary in traits that affect survival and reproduction, and this variation is heritable. However, he could not explain how variation was inherited or maintained—raising questions about blending inheritance and the origin of new traits. Artificial selection in domesticated species, such as dogs and pigeons, illustrated how targeted selection can produce major changes within a few generations. Examples like rapidly evolving minnows and the gradual evolution of complex organs such as eyes showed that evolution can proceed both quickly and incrementally.  

Gregor Mendel’s experiments with pea plants (1856–1863) demonstrated that inheritance operates through discrete hereditary “particles,” now known as genes, which are passed intact from each parent rather than blending. His work explained how variation persists across generations. Subsequent discoveries by Walther Flemming, Walter Sutton, and Theodor Boveri connected these hereditary units to chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Understanding this relationship between genes and chromosomes clarified how cell division—through mitosis and meiosis—transmits genetic material. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells, while meiosis yields haploid gametes, ensuring chromosome stability across generations and introducing variation through mutation and recombination.  

Mendel distinguished between genotype—the allele combination—and phenotype—the observable expression. Dominant alleles mask recessive ones, and predictable offspring outcomes can be shown with Punnett squares. During meiosis, crossing over allows genes on different chromosomes to assort independently; however, genes that lie close together on the same chromosome are less likely to do so. Recombination, measured by its frequency (r), produces new genetic combinations that contribute to diversity within populations.  

By the mid‑20th century, the discovery of DNA’s double‑helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray images, revealed how genetic information is stored and replicated. Genes, specific functional segments of DNA, number about 20,000–25,000 in humans. Mitochondrial DNA, inherited only from mothers, supports evolutionary studies such as tracing “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor living in East Africa roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago. Understanding DNA’s structure not only clarifies how genetic information functions but also enables comparisons across species to estimate the timing and causes of evolutionary events.
