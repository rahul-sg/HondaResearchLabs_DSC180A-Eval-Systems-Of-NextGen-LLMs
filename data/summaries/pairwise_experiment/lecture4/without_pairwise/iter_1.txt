This lecture examined how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, guided by Darwin’s postulates: populations struggle for existence, exhibit heritable variation related to survival and reproduction, and differ in reproductive success. Darwin’s challenge was explaining how variation is maintained if selection removes less fit traits and how novel forms arise—questions later addressed by genetic research. Artificial selection in domesticated plants and animals demonstrated how targeted breeding can cause rapid evolutionary change, as seen in the dramatic differences among dog or pigeon breeds achieved within a century. Examples such as minnows evolving placenta‑like structures quickly and the gradual evolution of the eye over roughly one million years illustrated both rapid and incremental evolutionary rates.

Mendel’s mid‑19th‑century experiments with pea plants established that inheritance operates through discrete “particles,” later recognized as genes, which preserve variation rather than blending it. Rediscovered around 1900, this work spurred investigation into hereditary structures. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and later Sutton and Boveri showed that chromosomes carry genetic material, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans possess 46 chromosomes, organized as 23 homologous pairs, including one pair of sex chromosomes.

Understanding cell division clarified genetic transmission. Mitosis produces identical diploid somatic cells, while meiosis creates haploid gametes, halving chromosome number so that fertilization restores it. Variation arises through mutation, recombination, and the independent assortment of chromosomes. Mendel’s crosses showed predictable inheritance: genotypes (allele combinations) determine phenotypes (observable traits), with dominant alleles masking recessive ones. Linkage and crossing‑over during meiosis may alter the independence of assortment when genes are close together, with recombination rates reflecting the likelihood of exchange and generating new genetic combinations.

Discovery of DNA’s double‑helix structure by Watson and Crick, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, provided the molecular foundation for inheritance. DNA includes a sugar‑phosphate backbone and complementary bases (A–T, C–G). Genes are functional DNA sequences, and mitochondrial DNA—short, maternally inherited, and highly conserved—has enabled tracing shared ancestry, including identification of “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of all humans about 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.
