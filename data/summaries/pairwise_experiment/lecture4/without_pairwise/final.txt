This lecture traced how discoveries in genetics and molecular biology clarified the mechanisms underlying evolution by natural selection and connected early theories to modern applications. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, consistent with Darwin’s postulates: organisms face a struggle for existence, vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and pass those variations on to offspring. Darwin’s central difficulty lay in explaining how variation was maintained and how new variants arose—questions that spurred later genetic research rather than being fully resolved. Artificial selection in domesticated species illustrated how pronounced changes can occur over just a few generations, as seen in dogs and pigeons, while natural rates of evolutionary change vary: minnows evolved placenta‑like structures rapidly, whereas the eye evolved through about 1,800 small steps over roughly one million years.

Mendel’s nineteenth‑century experiments with pea plants showed that inheritance involves discrete “particles,” now recognized as genes, preserving variation instead of blending it. The rediscovery of his work around 1900 prompted study of the cellular basis of heredity. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and Sutton and Boveri demonstrated that hereditary material resides in chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans possess 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 homologous pairs, one pair being the sex chromosomes. Recognizing how chromosomes behave in cell division became key to understanding inheritance and, by extension, evolution.

Mitosis produces identical diploid somatic cells, while meiosis generates haploid gametes, restoring the full chromosome set upon fertilization. Genetic variation arises through mutation, recombination, and independent assortment. Mendel’s crosses linked genotypes (allele combinations) to phenotypes (observable traits) and demonstrated dominant and recessive inheritance. Crossing‑over during meiosis can reshuffle linked genes, producing new combinations measured by the recombination rate.

In 1953, Watson and Crick, drawing on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, described the double‑helix structure of DNA. Understanding structure revealed how replication occurs and how genetic code directs protein formation. DNA’s sugar‑phosphate backbone and paired bases (A–T, C–G) form genes—the functional units of heredity. Maternally inherited mitochondrial DNA, which encodes 37 genes, is used to trace lineage, identifying “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor who lived about 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa. The lecture concluded with modern applications such as using molecular clocks and personal‑genomics tools like *23 & Me* to estimate evolutionary divergence and explore genetic ancestry.
