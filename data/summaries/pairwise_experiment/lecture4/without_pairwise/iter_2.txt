This lecture traced how discoveries in genetics and molecular biology clarified the mechanisms underlying evolution by natural selection. Evolution was defined as a change in allele frequencies within a population over time, consistent with Darwin’s postulates: organisms face a struggle for existence, vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and pass these heritable variations to offspring. Darwin’s major difficulty was explaining how variation persists if selection removes less fit traits and how new variants arise—questions resolved through later genetic research. Artificial selection in domesticated species illustrated that substantial change can occur over a few generations, as in the marked differences among dog or pigeon breeds achieved within roughly a century. Rates of change in nature can also vary: minnows rapidly evolved placenta‑like structures, while the eye is estimated to have evolved through about 1,800 successive 1 % improvements over roughly one million years.

Mendel’s mid‑19th‑century experiments with pea plants demonstrated that inheritance involves discrete “particles,” now called genes, which preserve rather than blend variation. Rediscovered around 1900, his work led to investigations of cellular structures. Walther Flemming identified chromatin and mitosis, and Sutton and Boveri showed that hereditary material resides in chromosomes, forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans possess 46 chromosomes arranged in 23 homologous pairs, including one pair of sex chromosomes. Understanding chromosomes proved key to understanding inheritance, which in turn is key to understanding evolution.

Cell‑division processes clarified transmission of this material. Mitosis produces identical diploid somatic cells, whereas meiosis yields haploid gametes, restoring the full set at fertilization. Variation arises through mutation, recombination, and independent assortment. Mendel’s crosses showed that specific allele combinations (genotypes) determine observable traits (phenotypes), with dominant alleles masking recessive ones. When genes lie close together, linkage can reduce independent assortment, but crossing‑over during meiosis creates new gene combinations; the recombination rate measures the frequency of such exchanges.

In 1953, Watson and Crick, using Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, described DNA’s double‑helix structure. Understanding structure helped explain function and replication. DNA’s sugar‑phosphate backbone and paired bases (A–T, C–G) form genes—the functional units of heredity. Maternally inherited mitochondrial DNA, encoding 37 genes, has been used to trace human ancestry, identifying “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common matrilineal ancestor who lived about 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.
