The lecture explored how race, culture, and schooling are socially constructed systems that shape identity, belonging, and institutional practices. It opened with an invitation for **personal reflection** on labeling in education—asking students to consider whether teachers, counselors, or administrators assigned them labels that influenced their sense of intelligence, motivation, and capability. These questions framed self‑inquiry as part of the learning process, linking individual experience to broader social patterns.  

In Sonia Nieto’s discussion of how we “construct others,” society was shown to categorize people by visible and invisible traits, attach fixed meanings to those categories, and sustain unequal treatment through policy and practice. The lecture warned that **silence, fear, and denial** about race only reinforce inequities; cultivating courage means facing how one’s upbringing and social context shape these views.  

Race was presented as **not biologically determined** but created through historical, political, and economic processes that make racial categories seem natural or “common sense.” The *RACE: Are We So Different?* project illustrated scientific evidence rejecting biological myths of race.  

Law emerged as a key force in sustaining racial hierarchies. Ian Haney López explained **law as coercion**—through restrictions on immigration, marriage, and citizenship that shaped both physical appearance and racial meaning—and **law as ideology**, persuading society that race is natural rather than socially made. Legal examples—including naturalization policies defining moral and linguistic “fitness,” court decisions on Armenians and Japanese applicants, and shifting Census labels—revealed how whiteness and citizenship evolved together. James Baldwin’s insight that whiteness required “generations and coercion” summarized this process of constructing privilege.  

The lecture then turned to language, showing how **terminology about race and ethnicity changes** with social and political climates. Words like *Negro, Black, African American, Hispanic, Latino/a, Latinx, Asian American,* and *White* reflect shifting identities and power relations.  

Nieto defined **culture** as dynamic, learned, and hybrid—expressed through shared values, traditions, and worldviews. Everyone has culture, which cannot be reduced to race or ethnicity. Complementing this, **Small et al.** framed culture through *values* (goals guiding behavior), *frames* (lenses for interpreting life), and *repertoires* (strategies for action).  

Finally, the lecture examined the **culture of schooling** in the United States. Schools hold assumptions that ability is fixed and measurable, structuring tracking, resources, and expectations. Reflective prompts on being “smart” or “bad” students encouraged recognition that these judgments rest on cultural beliefs. School culture, therefore, profoundly shapes students’ participation, achievement, and sense of possibility.
