This lecture introduced **within-subjects designs** as a counterpart to **between-groups designs**. In a between-groups setup, each participant appears in only one condition, so individual differences add nuisance variability that can obscure real effects. A within-subjects design instead measures the same person across all conditions, comparing each participant to their own baseline. Holding participants constant reduces person-related noise, yielding a more powerful test with fewer participants—for example, twenty people can yield twenty data points per condition rather than forty in a between-groups study.

The lecture described several forms of within-subjects designs: **pretest–posttest designs**, which include pre- and post-measures around an intervention; **repeated-measures designs**, comparing performance across multiple conditions (such as different motion coherence levels); and **longitudinal designs**, following participants over time. These approaches efficiently reduce variability, but they also create specific threats to validity that must be managed.

The instructor noted that such challenges are not “catches,” but normal design considerations that require control procedures. **Demand characteristics** arise when participants experience all conditions and infer the study’s purpose. **Carryover effects** occur when exposure to one condition influences later ones, through **fatigue**, **practice**, or broader contextual influences. Additional threats include **history effects** (outside events like exams or world news affecting results), **maturation** (developmental or biological change), and **testing effects** (repeated exposure improving performance). To address these, researchers can include control groups, vary condition order, or use alternate test forms.

Empirical illustrations grounded these principles. The **accent comprehension study** compared how 4‑ and 7‑year‑olds understood speech in their own accent—a comparison that could test within-person performance under different auditory conditions. The **dyslexia study** tested whether children read better with extra letter spacing, using either a within- or between-subjects structure depending on whether each child saw both text types and orderings.

Because the sequence of conditions can bias results, **order effects** must be handled through **counterbalancing**. **Complete counterbalancing** includes the ABBA design for two conditions or block randomization for three or more. **Incomplete counterbalancing**—such as random order with rotation or **Latin-square designs**—ensures each condition appears equally often in each position. In cognitive science, researchers may instead **randomly intermix** trials to cancel order effects. Some manipulations, however, cannot be undone, such as drug administration or training. Overall, within-subjects designs offer efficiency and statistical power when issues of order and carryover are properly controlled.
