Within-subjects designs provide an efficient alternative to between-groups designs by comparing each participant’s performance across multiple conditions rather than comparing separate groups. In between-groups designs, individual differences contribute nuisance variability (“noise”) that can obscure true effects and require larger samples—e.g., 40 participants to yield 20 data points per condition. Within-subjects designs instead compare each person to themselves, removing much of this participant-related noise. With the same 20 participants supplying data under all conditions, researchers still obtain 20 data points per condition, producing a more powerful, sample-efficient test.

Several specific forms of within-subjects designs were highlighted. A **pretest–posttest design** measures each participant before and after an intervention. A **repeated-measures design** involves two or more conditions within a single study (such as different levels of stimulus difficulty). A **longitudinal design** tracks participants across multiple time points, yielding informative trends but taking substantial time. Examples included research on children’s speech comprehension by accent and a dyslexia study testing whether wider letter spacing improves reading—cases illustrating how comparing each participant’s own performance allows finer-grained inferences.

While within-subjects designs reduce noise, they also raise distinctive risks. Experiencing all conditions can make participants aware of a study’s purpose (**demand characteristics**) or produce **carryover effects**, where one condition influences later performance through **practice** or **fatigue**. Other time-related confounds include **history effects** (external events like exams or local news altering participant state), **maturation effects** (natural developmental or biological change over time), and **testing effects** (improved performance from repeated exposure to the same test). Incorporating a pretest–posttest **control group** can help separate these time-based influences from the experimental intervention itself.

**Order effects** further complicate interpretation—performance can appear better merely because a condition was encountered earlier or later. To manage this, researchers apply **counterbalancing**, varying condition order across participants. **Complete counterbalancing** uses every possible order (e.g., ABBA for two conditions or all six for three), but the number of orders grows factorially with more conditions. **Incomplete counterbalancing** techniques, such as **rotation** or a **Latin-square design**, achieve balanced exposure with fewer participants. In cognitive research, many short trials may instead be randomly intermixed to cancel order effects. However, some manipulations, like drug administration or new learning, cannot be counterbalanced because their effects are irreversible.
