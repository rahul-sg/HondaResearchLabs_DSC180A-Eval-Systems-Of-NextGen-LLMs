This lecture builds on Week 1’s framework by showing how the core financial statements connect and how transactions are recorded through the double‑entry system. It begins by emphasizing that economic activity is complex and that accounting requires judgment and critical thinking in representing this activity through structured financial reports.  

The four interrelated financial statements—the balance sheet, income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows—serve as the primary means of financial communication. The balance sheet captures a firm’s position at points in time (the beginning and end of a period), while the other three statements describe activities over a fiscal period. These reports are linked: the income statement explains profit generation, which affects retained earnings in the statement of stockholders’ equity; the statement of cash flows details movements in cash between two balance sheet dates; and the equity statement connects changes across periods. Slides clarify that net income increases stockholders’ equity and that revenues raise, while expenses reduce, retained earnings.  

Building on these linkages, the lecture expands the accounting equation—Assets = Liabilities + Shareholders’ Equity—into its components: contributed capital and retained earnings. Retained earnings evolve from the beginning‑of‑period balance plus net income, minus dividends; income itself reflects revenues minus expenses. This expanded equation underpins all transaction recording and ensures that every entry maintains balance.  

The double‑entry system records both sides of each economic event so that debits and credits always offset. Debits increase assets and decrease liabilities and equity; credits do the reverse. The DEALOR mnemonic (Dividends, Expenses, Assets = normally debit; Liabilities, Owners’ equity, Revenues = normally credit) summarizes these patterns. Step‑by‑step examples—borrowing from a bank, buying equipment or inventory, paying suppliers, purchasing insurance, and issuing stock—illustrate that each transaction affects at least two accounts and keeps Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Ending balances from these T‑accounts appear on the balance sheet and become beginning balances for the next period, explicitly linking accounting cycles across time.  

The lecture concludes with the accounting process itself: analyze each transaction, record it as a journal entry, and post it to T‑accounts (the ledger). Maintaining Debits = Credits in every step preserves the balance‑sheet equation and ensures the reliability and integrity of all financial statements.
