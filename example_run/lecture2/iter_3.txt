This lecture builds on Week 1’s foundation by connecting the financial accounting framework to the mechanics of double‑entry recordkeeping and the expanded accounting equation. It opens by emphasizing that while economic activity is complex, accounting provides a structured way to represent it through judgment and critical thinking.  

The first section reviews the **financial statements** as the primary communication tools in accounting. The balance sheet reports assets, liabilities, and equity at specific points in time (the beginning and end of the fiscal period), while the income statement, statement of stockholders’ equity, and statement of cash flows summarize changes over the year. These reports are interdependent: revenues and expenses determine net income on the income statement, which increases or decreases retained earnings in the statement of stockholders’ equity. The statement of cash flows explains changes in cash between consecutive balance sheets. The slides highlight that revenues raise stockholders’ equity, expenses reduce it, and dividends decrease retained earnings, illustrating how all statements link through the equity account.  

Next, the lecture **expands the accounting equation** step by step: first from A = L + SE to A = L + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings; then to show that retained earnings equal the beginning balance + net income − dividends; and finally to A = L + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings (Beginning of Period + Revenues − Expenses − Dividends). This incremental derivation reveals how revenues, expenses, and dividends influence equity over time and formally ties the income statement to the balance sheet.  

The core of the lecture introduces **double‑entry accounting**, where each economic event has two sides—a give and a take—and where every transaction must keep total debits equal to total credits. Debits increase assets and decrease liabilities and equity, while credits have the opposite effect. Detailed examples demonstrate this rule through six transactions, such as receiving a bank loan, buying equipment and inventory, paying suppliers, purchasing insurance, and issuing stock. T‑accounts show how these entries balance and how ending balances become the starting point of the next period.  

Finally, the lecture details the **recording process**: analyze each transaction, record it as a journal entry (listing debits first and indenting credits), and post entries to the ledger using T‑accounts. The DEALOR mnemonic summarizes normal balances—Dividends, Expenses, and Assets are debits; Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, and Revenues are credits—ensuring that Debits = Credits and maintaining the fundamental equation A = L + SE.
