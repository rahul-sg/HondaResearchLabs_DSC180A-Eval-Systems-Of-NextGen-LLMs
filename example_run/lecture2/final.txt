This lecture extends the financial accounting framework by showing how financial statements connect through the double‑entry system. It begins with the idea that economic activity is complex and accounting requires thoughtful judgment. Accounting functions as a communication tool through four interrelated statements: the **Balance Sheet**, **Income Statement**, **Statement of Stockholders’ Equity**, and **Statement of Cash Flows**. The Balance Sheet presents a company’s position at a point in time, while the other statements summarize activity over a fiscal period. These reports link across time—revenues and expenses yield net income on the Income Statement, which increases retained earnings and, in turn, stockholders’ equity on the Balance Sheet. Net losses reduce equity, explaining how results reconcile changes between two balance‑sheet dates.  

The lecture revisits the accounting equation **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**, expanding it to **Assets = Liabilities + Contributed Capital + Retained Earnings**. Retained earnings evolve from the beginning balance plus net income minus dividends, and further break down into **revenues – expenses – dividends**. The instructor emphasizes that the expanded equation remains balanced and underlies all transaction recording.  

Double‑entry accounting recognizes two sides to every event—a give and a take—affecting at least two accounts. In **T‑accounts**, debits appear on the left and credits on the right: **debits increase assets, expenses, and dividends**, whereas **credits increase liabilities, contributed capital, and revenues**. The mnemonic **DEALOR** helps identify normal balances (Dividends, Expenses, Assets = Debit; Liabilities, Owners’ Equity, Revenue = Credit). Every transaction includes at least one debit and one credit, and **Debits = Credits** preserves **A = L + SE**.  

Worked examples trace how transactions reshape accounts: a $100,000 loan raises both cash and notes payable; equipment, inventory, supplier payments, insurance prepayments, and stock issuance adjust asset, liability, and equity balances accordingly. The lecture then shows how ending balances are computed using **BB + account increases – account decreases = EB**, reinforcing that debits and credits quantitatively reconcile to the accounting equation. These ending balances appear on the Balance Sheet and roll forward as the next period’s beginning balances.  

Finally, the lecture sets out the accounting process: **(1) analyze each transaction, (2) record debits and credits as journal entries (debits first, credits indented), and (3) post to the ledger (T‑accounts) to determine ending balances**. This cycle ensures internal consistency through the enduring rule that **Debits = Credits**.
