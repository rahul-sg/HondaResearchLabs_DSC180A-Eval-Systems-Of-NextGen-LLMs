The first week of **Principles of Accounting** introduces the purpose and foundations of financial accounting and its role in business decision-making. The course begins by defining accounting as the **language of business**, a system for recording and communicating a company’s financial story—its successes, challenges, risks, and opportunities. The objectives for students are to understand how financial statements are created, interpreted, and used to make informed business decisions. Accounting serves two key functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in value terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to external decision makers through financial statements.

The lecture highlights the many **users of financial information**, including investors evaluating performance, creditors and suppliers assessing repayment ability or reliability, management and boards overseeing operations, and other groups such as employees, customers, analysts, regulators, and competitors who rely on accounting data to guide their choices.  

Financial accounting captures three categories of business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and creditors such as stock issuance or borrowing), **investing** (acquiring resources like land, buildings, or equipment), and **operating** (production, marketing, and selling activities). Results of these activities are reported through four core **financial statements**—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—that together communicate a company’s financial position and performance. These statements are prepared in accordance with **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, developed by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** and overseen by the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues standards under **IFRS**. Independent **auditors**, required for publicly traded firms, assess whether these financial statements follow GAAP and are free of material misstatements, issuing one of four possible opinions that add credibility to financial reporting.

The lecture concludes with a focus on the **balance sheet**, described as a snapshot of a company’s financial position at a point in time. It reflects the **accounting equation**—**Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**—which must always remain balanced. Assets represent the company’s resources, liabilities are obligations to creditors, and equity shows the owners’ residual claims. Transaction examples, such as issuing stock, borrowing funds, purchasing equipment, paying prepaid rent, or buying supplies on credit, demonstrate how each event affects different parts of the equation while maintaining balance. This framework lays the groundwork for analyzing how transactions shape a company’s financial health and decision-making.
