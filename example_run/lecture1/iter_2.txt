The first week of **Principles of Accounting** introduces the foundations and purpose of financial accounting as the language of business—a system that records and communicates a company’s financial story, including performance, opportunities, and risks. Students learn that accounting serves two essential functions: **recordkeeping**, which involves identifying, measuring, and recording economic events, and **communication**, which delivers this information to external decision makers through financial statements. The course aims to build the ability to understand how these statements are created, interpreted, and applied in business decision-making across industries.

The lecture explains that **financial information** supports a wide range of users. **Investors** and **creditors** analyze a firm’s condition and repayment ability, **suppliers** assess reliability and future partnerships, **management** and **boards of directors** use results to evaluate performance, and **employees**, **customers**, and **other groups** such as analysts, regulators, and competitors rely on accounting data to inform their choices.

**Financial accounting** is organized around three business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and creditors, such as obtaining loans or issuing stock), **investing** (purchasing long-term or short-term resources like buildings, equipment, or inventory), and **operating** (core actions such as production, marketing, and selling). The outcomes of these activities appear in four key **financial statements**: the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**. These reports follow **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, developed by the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)** under the authority of the **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)**, which oversees public company reporting. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues comparable standards under **IFRS**.

Public firms must also engage independent **auditors**, who evaluate whether financial statements are prepared in accordance with GAAP and free from material misstatements. Auditors issue one of four opinions—**unqualified**, **unqualified with explanatory or emphasis paragraph**, **qualified**, or **adverse**—that influence how much confidence users place in the reported information.

The module closes with an introduction to the **balance sheet**, a snapshot of a company’s financial position at a specific date, expressed through the **accounting equation**: **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. Examples such as issuing stock, borrowing funds, acquiring equipment, paying rent in advance, and purchasing supplies on credit illustrate how each transaction affects this balance, showing that every event has a dual effect and that the equation must always remain equal.
