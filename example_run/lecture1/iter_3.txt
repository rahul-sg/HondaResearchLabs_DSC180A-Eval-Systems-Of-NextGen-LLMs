The first week of **Principles of Accounting** introduces financial accounting as the *language of business*—a process for recording and communicating a company’s financial story of performance, risks, and opportunities. Accounting fulfills two essential functions: **recordkeeping**, which identifies, measures, and records economic events in monetary terms, and **communication**, which conveys this information to external decision makers through formal financial statements. The course emphasizes understanding how these statements are prepared, interpreted, and used to support sound business decisions.

Financial information serves many users. **Investors** and **creditors** rely on it to assess performance and repayment capacity; **suppliers**, **management**, and **boards of directors** use it to evaluate reliability, operations, and strategic results; **employees** judge an employer’s stability; and **customers**, **analysts**, **regulators**, and competitors depend on it to gauge a firm’s long‑term prospects.

Financial accounting captures three types of business activities: **financing** (transactions with investors and creditors such as loans or stock issuance), **investing** (acquiring and disposing of resources like buildings, equipment, or inventory), and **operating** (day‑to‑day production, marketing, and selling). These activities generate information summarized in four key **financial statements**—the **balance sheet**, **income statement**, **statement of stockholders’ equity**, and **statement of cash flows**—which together communicate the company’s financial position and results.

These statements must follow **Generally Accepted Accounting Principles (GAAP)**, the standardized rules ensuring comparability across firms. The **Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC)** has ultimate authority over public company reporting and *delegates the responsibility for setting accounting standards* to the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB)**, an independent private body. Internationally, the **International Accounting Standards Board (IASB)** issues similar guidance through **IFRS**, supporting cross‑border consistency.

Public companies are also required to employ **independent auditors**, who assess whether financial statements comply with GAAP and are free of material misstatements. Auditors enhance credibility by issuing one of four opinions—**unqualified**, **unqualified with explanatory paragraph**, **qualified**, or **adverse**—that signal the reliability of the reports.

The lecture concludes with the **balance sheet**, a snapshot of a firm’s financial position at a particular moment, expressed by the **accounting equation**: **Assets = Liabilities + Stockholders’ Equity**. The “Klein, Inc.” examples—issuing stock, borrowing funds, acquiring equipment, prepaying rent, and purchasing supplies on credit—demonstrate that every transaction has a dual effect and the equation must always remain balanced.
