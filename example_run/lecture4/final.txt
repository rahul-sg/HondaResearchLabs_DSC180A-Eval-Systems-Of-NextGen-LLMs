The lecture traced the development of ideas linking natural selection, heredity, and modern genetics. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, with Darwin’s three postulates outlining its mechanism: organisms struggle for existence, they vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and these traits are heritable. Darwin recognized the central role of variation but could not explain how it arose, persisted, or produced new forms. Examples such as domesticated dogs, pigeons, and plants illustrated artificial selection—rapid evolutionary change achieved through human choice—while the evolution of the minnow’s placenta-like structure and the gradual formation of the eye showed that evolutionary rates can vary dramatically.

Mendel’s experiments with pea plants (1856–1863) demonstrated particulate inheritance: discrete hereditary factors passed from each parent maintain variation rather than blending. He defined genes (now known as sections of DNA) with alternative forms called alleles, distinguished between genotype and phenotype, and identified dominant and recessive allele expression. These patterns were later modeled using event diagrams and Punnett squares to predict offspring ratios.

The rediscovery of Mendel’s work around 1900 connected genetics with cell biology. Walther Flemming observed chromatin and mitosis, while Walter Sutton and Theodor Boveri, working independently on grasshoppers and roundworms, showed that hereditary material resides on chromosomes—forming the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance. Humans have 46 chromosomes (23 homologous pairs), including 22 somatic pairs and one pair of sex chromosomes. Understanding how chromosomes behave during cell division clarified the mechanisms of inheritance. Mitosis produces identical diploid cells for growth, whereas meiosis generates haploid gametes, halving the chromosome number to preserve the species total after fertilization. Meiosis introduces variation through mutation, recombination, and independent assortment. Crossing over creates new gene combinations, and the recombination rate measures how frequently such exchanges occur.

The discovery of DNA’s double-helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray work, explained the molecular basis of heredity. DNA’s sugar‑phosphate backbone and paired bases (A–T, C–G) allow replication and protein coding, linking structure to function. Some DNA sequences form genes, while mitochondrial DNA—shorter, maternally inherited, and nonrecombining—traces lineage back to “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent matrilineal ancestor of humans about 100,000–200,000 years ago in Africa.
