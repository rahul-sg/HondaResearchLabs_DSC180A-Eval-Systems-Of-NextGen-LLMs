This lecture examined how natural selection and genetic inheritance together explain evolutionary change. Evolution was defined as the change in allele frequencies within a population over time, reflecting Darwin’s postulates that organisms struggle for existence, vary in traits affecting survival and reproduction, and pass those traits to offspring. Darwin’s main difficulty was explaining how variation arises and persists when natural selection seems to remove it. Evidence from artificial selection showed that targeted breeding by humans could produce rapid changes in a few generations, as seen in domesticated dogs and pigeons. Examples such as placental evolution in minnows and the gradual 1% steps in eye evolution illustrated that evolutionary change can occur both rapidly and incrementally.

Gregor Mendel’s 19th‑century experiments with pea plants established the particulate theory of inheritance—traits are controlled by discrete “particles” inherited from each parent, maintaining variation rather than blending it. His work later linked to cell structures observed by Walther Flemming, who described chromatin and mitosis, and to the Chromosome Theory of Inheritance proposed by Sutton and Boveri, showing that genes reside on chromosomes passed from parents to offspring.

Cell division through mitosis produces identical diploid somatic cells, while meiosis forms haploid gametes. Variation arises through mutation, recombination, and independent assortment during meiosis. Mendel’s principles distinguish genotype (genetic makeup) from phenotype (observable traits), and show how dominant alleles mask recessive ones. Crosses of heterozygotes—illustrated with Punnett squares and event trees—demonstrate predictable ratios of inherited traits. Linkage and recombination further explain that genes on different chromosomes assort independently, while genes located near each other may segregate together; the recombination rate (r) quantifies the frequency of crossing‑over events.

The discovery of DNA’s double‑helix structure by James Watson and Francis Crick in 1953, based on Rosalind Franklin’s x‑ray data, clarified how DNA’s structure relates to genetic function. DNA consists of a sugar‑phosphate backbone and nucleotide base pairs: adenine with thymine, cytosine with guanine. Genes are functional segments of this molecule, with human DNA containing about 20,000–25,000 genes. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)—inherited only maternally and coding for 37 genes—has been key in tracing evolutionary relationships. Analyses of mtDNA have identified “Mitochondrial Eve,” the most recent common matrilineal ancestor of all humans, living roughly 100,000–200,000 years ago in East Africa.
