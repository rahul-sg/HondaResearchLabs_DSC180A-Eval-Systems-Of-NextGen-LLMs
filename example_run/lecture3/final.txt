This lecture deepens the study of Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power** as a framework for understanding how colonial domination continues to structure race, labor, knowledge, and identity in modern Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano’s intellectual trajectory—from dependency theory and political economy to decolonial thought—charts how the global capitalist order and its epistemologies originate in colonial relations of domination. His work situates economics, culture, and knowledge as interlocking dimensions of power rather than separate historical stages.

Within the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) project**, the lecture explains that modernity and coloniality were co‑constituted. Western modernity was built through conquest and resource extraction, while its narratives silenced other histories. *Coloniality* refers to the ongoing logic that privileges Eurocentric modes of thinking and obscures Indigenous and Afro‑descendant worldviews. *Decoloniality* aims to center those suppressed epistemologies and plural modernities, rejecting the assumption of a single Western path to progress.

Quijano’s first key point, **coloniality of power**, identifies two intertwined processes in the making of “America”: the construction of racial difference between conquerors and conquered, and the establishment of new labor systems—slavery, encomienda, and mita—that organized production for global markets. This racialized division of labor became the foundation of global capitalism and dependency, channeling resources and privileges toward Europe while subordinating colonized peoples and nations.

His second point, **Eurocentrism**, challenges the notion that Europe’s dominance was inevitable. Enlightenment and capitalist ideologies promoted linear hierarchies—“First” versus “Third World,” “developed” versus “underdeveloped”—that portrayed European history as universal. The lecture connects this critique to Stuart Hall’s “West and the Rest” discourse and to cultural examples such as *Roma* (2018), which exposes how colonial hierarchies of race and labor persist in the present.

The third theme links **Eurocentrism to Latin American national identity**. Rebecca Earle’s research shows how early elites adopted *Indianesque nationalism* to celebrate an idealized Indigenous past while sustaining exclusion. Later, *mestizaje* ideology proclaimed racial harmony but reinforced whitened norms and social hierarchy.  

Finally, the lecture highlights a **crisis of the European perspective on knowledge**—a move from homogeneous, linear histories toward recognition of heterogeneity and discontinuity. This shift opens space for decolonial thinking that values diverse histories and local forms of knowledge. For Quijano, decoloniality means freeing ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror to imagine ourselves and the world anew.
