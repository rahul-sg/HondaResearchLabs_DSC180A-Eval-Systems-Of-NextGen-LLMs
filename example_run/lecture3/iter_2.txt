This lecture explores Aníbal Quijano’s theory of the **coloniality of power** and its continuing influence on modernity, capitalism, and identity in Latin America. Quijano—a Peruvian sociologist whose work progressed from dependency theory to decolonial thought—traced how colonial hierarchies of race, labor, and knowledge endure beyond formal colonial rule. His ideas sit within the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework developed by Latin American scholars such as Arturo Escobar, Walter Mignolo, and Catherine Walsh.  

The lecture distinguishes three interrelated elements: **modernity** as a Eurocentric narrative of progress anchored in Western dominance; **coloniality** as the lasting matrix of power that privileges Western epistemologies; and **decoloniality** as the project of centering non‑Western languages, cosmologies, and ways of being. Unlike colonialism, which names a historical system of rule, coloniality describes the enduring logics of domination that organize social relations after independence.  

According to Quijano (Slides 11–13), the coloniality of power originated through two linked processes: the creation of distinctions between “West” and “Rest,” and the establishment of racialized structures of labor control in the Americas. Systems such as slavery, the encomienda, and mita tied non‑wage labor to ideas of racial difference. This arrangement produced a world economy in which whiteness and European norms determined access to wealth and authority.  

**Eurocentrism** (Slides 2.3–2.5) reinforced these hierarchies by framing Europe as the sole origin of progress. It structured global capitalism and knowledge through evolutionary, dualistic categories—“pre‑capital” versus “industrial,” “Third World” versus “First World.” The slides on democracy and development further show how these Eurocentric measures persist in neocolonial political and economic models across the Americas.  

Later slides (2.7–2.14) highlight responses that question linear histories and recognize **heterogeneity** in social change. Rebecca Earle’s notion of “Indianesque nationalism” illustrates how post‑independence elites romanticized Indigenous symbols to craft new national identities while marginalizing living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant communities. The ideology of **mestizaje** similarly portrayed racial harmony yet often upheld whiteness as the national norm.  

The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality** (Slide 2.19): to free ourselves from the “Eurocentric mirror” that distorts self‑understanding and to affirm diverse historical experiences, epistemologies, and modernities capable of transforming the enduring matrix of colonial power.
