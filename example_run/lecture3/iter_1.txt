This lecture examines Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the “coloniality of power” and its lasting imprint on modernity, capitalism, and identity in Latin America. Quijano, a Peruvian sociologist whose work evolved from dependency theory to decolonial thought, redefined how colonial hierarchies of knowledge, race, and labor continue to organize the modern world. Within the Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD) framework, the lecture distinguishes these three interrelated projects: *modernity* as a Eurocentric account of historical progress, *coloniality* as the enduring matrix of domination that privileges Western ways of knowing, and *decoloniality* as the effort to center non‑Western epistemologies, languages, and cosmologies.

A crucial distinction emphasized in the lecture is that *coloniality* extends beyond *colonialism*. Whereas colonialism refers to a specific political‑economic system of rule, coloniality names the persistent logics that survive after formal independence—hierarchies of race, labor, and knowledge that continue under global capitalism. According to Slide 11, the “coloniality of power” emerged through two historical processes: the formation of differences between “West” and “Rest” and the creation of new structures of labor control in the Americas. Systems such as slavery, the encomienda, and the mita organized production for global markets while naturalizing racial hierarchies that tied labor exploitation to notions of European superiority.

Quijano links this racialized labor order to *Eurocentrism*, a worldview that presents Europe as the origin and measure of modernity. Slides 2.3–2.5 show how Eurocentrism produced linear narratives of progress—“underdeveloped” to “developed,” “Third World” to “First World”—and justified dependency and inequality. Yet the lecture also highlights emerging perspectives of *heterogeneity* and *discontinuity* (Slide 2.7): by questioning linear history, scholars and Indigenous thinkers open space for plural, locally grounded understandings of power and change.

Applying these ideas, the lecture considers Cuáron’s *Roma* as an example of how colonial hierarchies persist in gendered and racialized domestic labor. It then turns to post‑independence nation‑building: historian Rebecca Earle’s notion of “Indianesque nationalism” reveals how elites romanticized Indigenous symbols while excluding living Indigenous and Afro‑descendant peoples. Later discourses of *mestizaje* claimed racial harmony but often reasserted whiteness as the national ideal. The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call for *decoloniality*—to unlearn Eurocentric narratives, recognize the co‑constitution of modernity and coloniality, and affirm diverse ways of knowing and being beyond the colonial mirror.
