This lecture explores Aníbal Quijano’s concept of the **coloniality of power**, tracing how the intertwined histories of race, capitalism, and knowledge shape modern Latin America. A Peruvian sociologist, Quijano advanced from **dependency theory** to broader analyses of culture and epistemology, helping establish the **Modernity/Coloniality/Decoloniality (MCD)** framework alongside scholars like Arturo Escobar, Walter Mignolo, and Catherine Walsh.  

The lecture develops three interrelated dimensions—**modernity**, **coloniality**, and **decoloniality**. Modernity is presented as a contingent historical process, not a natural Western achievement. Coloniality exposes the continuing epistemological logic of colonial domination through the privileging of Western categories that obscure Indigenous and non‑Western knowledges. Decoloniality, in turn, seeks to center suppressed languages, cosmologies, and worldviews, redirecting cultural and intellectual authority away from Europe.  

According to Quijano, the **coloniality of power** emerged through two simultaneous processes: the classification of peoples into “West” (conquerors) and “Rest” (conquered); and the creation of new systems of labor control such as slavery, the **encomienda**, and the **mita**. These structures fused racial hierarchy with economic exploitation, grounding the first global model of capitalist power.  

**Eurocentrism** is the ideological extension of this matrix. Quijano describes it as the **Eurocentrification of world capitalism**—a worldview that makes Europe appear the universal agent of history while assigning other regions derivative or “underdeveloped” status. Linear binaries like “First World/Third World” or “developed/underdeveloped” naturalize dependency and reproduce colonial hierarchies even within post‑independence democracies and development policies. The lecture asks, in this context, who decides the measures of “progress” or “success.”  

Later slides (2.7–2.14) challenge these linear narratives through **heterogeneity and discontinuity**, emphasizing that social change is uneven and locally situated. The **Aymara concept of *pachakuti*** illustrates a non‑Western understanding of cyclical transformation that resists European notions of sequential progress.  Rebecca Earle’s analysis of *Indianesque nationalism* extends this critique: post‑independence elites romanticized Indigenous ancestry while excluding living Indigenous groups, and the ideology of **mestizaje** reinforced myths of racial harmony that disguised enduring inequality.  

The lecture concludes with Quijano’s call for **decoloniality**: to free ourselves from the Eurocentric mirror that distorts self‑understanding and to dismantle the racial, economic, and epistemic structures through which coloniality continues to organize the modern world.
